**The circle given, the straight line found.** III.33 had the straight line and found the circle; here the circle is given, and Euclid must find the straight line. He draws EF touching the circle at B, copies the given angle at B as the angle FBC (I.23), and III.32 carries it across into the segment BAC. The angle FBC chooses the straight line BC. Where A falls on its circumference does not matter, so long as it is on the other side of BC from F.

**Double the angle instead.** Heath offers another route: "An alternative construction here would be to make an 'angle at the centre' (in the extended sense, if necessary) double of the given angle; and, if the given angle is right, it is only necessary to draw a diameter of the circle" (vol. 2, p. 71). The extended sense is the one Heath discusses under III.20: an obtuse given angle needs an angle at the centre greater than two right angles. Euclid's straight line touching the circle serves acute, right and obtuse angles alike, with no extended sense at all.

**Why does BC meet the circle again?** Euclid gives no citation for drawing EF: it is III.16's porism, at right angles to the radius at B (I.11), and for that our statement gives the circle its centre. We copy the angle on the centre's side of EF. Then III.16 puts a point of the new straight line within the circle, so it meets the circle again at C, and on the centre's side of EF, for the reason found in III.32.

**The modern way.** With the centre at 0, turn a point B of the circle through 2θ (0 < θ < π) to C = Be^{2iθ}: every angle at the circumference standing on the arc so swept is θ, so the segment beyond BC from that arc admits θ. That is Heath's doubled angle at the centre, in the extended sense when θ is obtuse, rather than Euclid's tangent.
