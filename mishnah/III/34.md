**An angle copied twice.** Euclid first copies the given angle at B, where a straight line touches the circle, and then carries it into the alternate segment by III.32. This makes a construction out of the earlier theorem. The circle is given, but the straight line BC is ours to choose: its direction fixes the angle FBC, and that angle fixes the angle in the segment BAC. We take A on the other side of BC from F. Its place along that circumference does not matter; the choice of side does.

**Double the angle instead.** Heath offers another route: "An alternative construction here would be to make an 'angle at the centre' (in the extended sense, if necessary) double of the given angle; and, if the given angle is right, it is only necessary to draw a diameter of the circle" (vol. 2, p. 71). The extended sense is the one discussed under III.20: an obtuse given angle needs an angle at the centre greater than two right angles. Euclid's construction with a touching straight line works for acute, right and obtuse given angles alike.

**Why does the new arm meet the circle?** Our statement supplies a centre so that we can draw the straight line at right angles to the radius at B, using I.11; III.16's porism says it touches the circle. Euclid calls for no finding of the centre here. We copy the given angle on the centre's side of EF. III.16 then gives a point within the circle on the new straight line, which meets the circle again at C. Lean must also put C on the chosen arm: otherwise the straight line joining C to the centre would cross EF within the circle, contradicting contact.

**The modern way.** For a circle of radius r and 0 < θ < π, a chord of length 2r sin θ bounds segments whose inscribed angles are θ and π − θ, by the extended law of sines. III.34 chooses the segment with angle θ.
