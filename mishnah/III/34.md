**A theorem put to work.** III.32 showed that a straight line drawn across from the point of contact makes, with the straight line touching the circle, angles equal to those in the alternate segments. Euclid now builds with it. He draws EF touching the circle at B, copies the given angle at B as the angle FBC (I.23), and III.32 carries it across into the segment BAC. The circle is given; the straight line BC is Euclid's to choose, and the angle FBC chooses it. Where A falls on its circumference does not matter, so long as it is on the other side of BC from F.

**Double the angle instead.** Heath offers another route: "An alternative construction here would be to make an 'angle at the centre' (in the extended sense, if necessary) double of the given angle; and, if the given angle is right, it is only necessary to draw a diameter of the circle" (vol. 2, p. 71). The extended sense is the one Heath discusses under III.20: an obtuse given angle needs an angle at the centre greater than two right angles. Euclid's straight line touching the circle serves acute, right and obtuse angles alike, with no extended sense at all.

**Why does BC meet the circle again?** Euclid gives no citation for drawing EF: it is III.16's porism, at right angles to the radius at B (I.11), and for that our statement gives the circle its centre. We copy the angle on the centre's side of EF. Then III.16 puts a point of the new straight line within the circle, so it meets the circle again at C. Euclid reads off his figure that C lies on that side of EF. Lean wants a reason, and finds one: otherwise the straight line from C to the centre would cross EF within the circle, and no point of a straight line touching the circle is within it (`Touches.not_inside`).

**The modern way.** In a circle of radius r, a straight line of length 2r sin θ, with 0 < θ < π, cuts off two segments, admitting the angles θ and π − θ, by the extended law of sines; III.34 cuts off the first.
