**Back from the circumference to the straight line.** III.28 starts from equal straight lines and finds equal circumferences; here Euclid travels the other way. Equal circumferences give equal angles at the centres (III.27), the radii give the sides about them, and I.4 makes the bases BC, EF equal. That is why the circles must be equal: equal angles alone would give no equal lengths.

**A diameter leaves no triangle.** Heath writes: "The particular case of this converse of III. 28 in which the given arcs are arcs of semicircles is even easier than the corresponding case of III. 28 itself" (vol. 2, p. 61). When BC passes through the centre K, BGC is a semicircle, and so is the equal circumference EHF. Both straight lines are then diameters, each made of two radii, and we need no triangles to see that they are equal.

**Which circumference does the angle see?** Euclid's figure puts K beyond BC from G, so that the angle BKC stands on BGC. K may also lie on G's side, where BGC is greater than a semicircle (`Prop29.size`), and then the angle BKC stands on BAC, the circumference that remains. So we take the equal circumferences from the equal circles (`Circumference.whole`), and Euclid's argument runs on BAC and EDF. Equal circumferences are both less than a semicircle, both semicircles or both greater, so L falls as K does.

**One circle will do.** Heath adds that III.26–III.29 "are of course equally true if the *same* circle is taken instead of *two equal* circles" (vol. 2, p. 61). Our two circles may be one (`EqualCircles.refl`), for a circle's radii are equal to one another, and nothing in the proof needs the circles apart, as the figure draws them.

**The modern way.** With the centre at 0, the ends of an arc of angular measure θ in a circle of radius r are B and Be^{iθ}, so the straight line joining them has length |Be^{iθ} − B| = r|e^{iθ} − 1|, which depends only on r and θ: equal arcs in equal circles have equal chords, arcs greater than a semicircle included.
