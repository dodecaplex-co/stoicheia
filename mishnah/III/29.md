**What does congruence leave for III.29 to do?** III.28 went from equal straight lines to equal circumferences, and our statement of it concludes that they coincide: one placed on the other, end on end and every distance kept, so that by Common Notion 4 they are equal. `Prop29` states III.29 with circumferences BGC, EHF that coincide so. Read that way, little is left: the placing that lays BGC on EHF carries B to E and C to F and keeps the distance between them, so BC is already equal to EF. The circles are equal too, and there "in equal circles" adds nothing to what is given. Euclid comes back by another road, and we keep it.

Equal angles alone give no equal lengths. In Euclid's lesser case, III.27 gives equal angles at the centres, equal circles give equal radii, and I.4 gives equal bases. For the greater circumferences we give the edition's own argument on the lesser ones, bounded by the same straight lines. Its basis is the last step of Euclid's III.28, where taking equal circumferences from equal whole circles leaves equal circumferences (Heath, vol. 2, p. 60); here Common Notion 3 gives the equal lesser measures. The measure companion needs equal circles: equal measures in unequal circles can have unequal straight lines; congruence fixes the distance between the ends.

The case of semicircles, Heath says, "is even easier than the corresponding case of III. 28 itself" (vol. 2, p. 61). When BC passes through the centre K, BGC is a semicircle, and so is the equal circumference EHF. Both straight lines are then diameters, and equal circles have equal diameters, the fact from which Heath completes that case of III.28 (vol. 2, p. 60). Ours are each two equal radii, and we need no triangles.

**Which circumference does the angle see?** When K lies on G's side of BC, BGC is greater than a semicircle and the triangle angle BKC belongs to the lesser remainder BAC. To pass to the lesser circumferences BAC and EDF, we use the subtraction from equal whole circles at the end of Euclid's III.28: taking BGC and EHF leaves equal measures by Common Notion 3. Points of the circles on K's and L's sides supply standing angles on those remainders. III.27 gives the equal triangle angles, and I.4 gives BC = EF.

Heath adds that III.26–III.29 "are of course equally true if the *same* circle is taken instead of *two equal* circles" (vol. 2, p. 61). Our two circles may be one (`EqualCircles.refl`), for a circle's radii are equal to one another. Nothing in the proof needs two circles, though the figure draws two.

**The modern way.** Put the centre K at 0 in the complex plane, and let the radius be $r$. The arc whose angle at the centre is $\theta$, with $0 \lt \theta \lt 2\pi$, runs from B to B turned through $\theta$, that is, to $\mathrm{B}e^{i\theta}$. So the straight line joining its ends has length $|\mathrm{B}e^{i\theta} - \mathrm{B}| = r|e^{i\theta} - 1| = 2r\sin(\theta/2)$, which depends only on $r$ and $\theta$: in equal circles, equal arcs have equal straight lines joining their ends, arcs greater than a semicircle included.
