**Back from the circumference to the straight line.** III.28 starts from equal straight lines and finds equal circumferences; here Euclid travels the other way. Equal circumferences give equal angles at the centres (III.27), the radii give the sides about them, and I.4 makes the bases BC, EF equal. That is why the circles must be equal: equal angles alone would give no equal lengths. The radii are equal by Definition III.1 itself, which Heath, in the note quoted under III.26, defends as a definition proper.

**A diameter leaves no triangle.** Heath writes: "The particular case of this converse of III. 28 in which the given arcs are arcs of semicircles is even easier than the corresponding case of III. 28 itself" (vol. 2, p. 61). When BC passes through the centre K, BGC is a semicircle, and so is the equal circumference EHF. Both straight lines are then diameters, each made of two radii, and we need no triangles to see that they are equal.

**Which circumference does the angle see?** Euclid's figure puts K beyond BC from G, so that the angle BKC stands on BGC. Lean asks where else K might be: on BC, or on G's side (`Prop29.size`). On G's side, BGC is greater than a semicircle and the angle BKC stands on BAC, the circumference that remains; so we take the equal circumferences from the equal circles (`Circumference.whole`), and Euclid's argument runs on BAC and EDF. Equal circumferences are both less than a semicircle, both semicircles or both greater, so L falls as K does. Heath asks as much of III.26, whose proof covers every case only if an angle at the centre may reach two right angles or more.

**One circle will do.** Heath adds that III.26–III.29 "are of course equally true if the *same* circle is taken instead of *two equal* circles" (vol. 2, p. 61). Our two circles may be one (`EqualCircles.refl`), for a circle's radii are equal to one another, and nothing in the proof needs the circles apart, as the figure draws them. Nor need G and H lie on the circles: our statement asks of them only that they name the side of BC and of EF on which each circumference lies.

**The modern way.** An arc of angular measure θ in a circle of radius r is subtended by a chord of length 2r sin(θ/2), so equal arcs in equal circles have equal chords, arcs greater than a semicircle included.
