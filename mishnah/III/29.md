**Why must the circles be equal?** Equal angles alone give no equal lengths, and Euclid needs the radii. III.28 went from equal straight lines to equal circumferences, and here he comes back: equal circumferences give equal angles at the centres (III.27), the equal circles give equal sides about them, and I.4 makes the bases BC, EF equal.

**A diameter leaves no triangle.** Heath writes: "The particular case of this converse of III. 28 in which the given arcs are arcs of semicircles is even easier than the corresponding case of III. 28 itself" (vol. 2, p. 61). When BC passes through the centre K, BGC is a semicircle, and so is the equal circumference EHF. Both straight lines are then diameters, equal as in III.26, and we need no triangles.

**Which circumference does the angle see?** Euclid's figure puts K beyond BC from G, so that the angle BKC stands on BGC. K may also lie on G's side, where BGC is greater than a semicircle (`Prop29.size`), and then the angle BKC stands on BAC, the circumference that remains. So we take the equal circumferences from the equal circles (`Circumference.whole`), and Euclid's argument runs on BAC and EDF. Equal circumferences are both less than a semicircle, both semicircles or both greater, so L falls as K does.

**One circle will do.** Heath adds that III.26–III.29 "are of course equally true if the *same* circle is taken instead of *two equal* circles" (vol. 2, p. 61). Our two circles may be one (`EqualCircles.refl`), for a circle's radii are equal to one another. Nothing in the proof needs two circles, though the figure draws two.

**The modern way.** With the centre at 0, an arc whose angle at the centre is θ runs from B to B turned through θ, that is, B multiplied by cos θ + i sin θ. So the straight line joining its ends has length r·|cos θ + i sin θ − 1|, which depends only on the radius r and on θ: equal arcs in equal circles have equal straight lines joining their ends, arcs greater than a semicircle included.
