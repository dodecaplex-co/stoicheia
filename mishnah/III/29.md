**What does congruence leave for III.29 to do?** III.28 went from equal straight lines to equal circumferences, and our statement of it concludes that they coincide: one placed on the other, end on end and every distance kept, so that by Common Notion 4 they are equal. `Prop29.of_congruent` states III.29 with circumferences BGC, EHF that coincide so. Read that way, little is left: the placing that lays BGC on EHF carries B to E and C to F and keeps the distance between them, so BC is already equal to EF. The circles are equal too, and there "in equal circles" adds nothing to what is given. Euclid comes back by another road, and we keep it.

`Prop29` states III.29 with circumferences equal in measure, and in equal circles the two readings agree, as theorems after III.29 prove. Its proof takes Euclid's road. Equal angles alone give no equal lengths, and he needs the radii: equal circumferences give equal angles at the centres (III.27), the equal circles give equal sides about them, and I.4 makes the bases BC, EF equal. Here the equal circles do real work, for in unequal circles circumferences equal in measure are cut off by unequal straight lines. `Prop29.of_congruent` follows the same road, taking its equal angles from III.27's companion and its radii from the equal circles.

The case of semicircles, Heath says, "is even easier than the corresponding case of III. 28 itself" (vol. 2, p. 61). When BC passes through the centre K, BGC is a semicircle, and so is the equal circumference EHF. Both straight lines are then diameters, each two equal radii, and we need no triangles.

**Which circumference does the angle see?** Euclid's figure puts K beyond BC from G, so that the angle BKC stands on BGC. K may also lie on G's side, where BGC is greater than a semicircle and the ordinary angle BKC stands on BAC, the circumference that remains. Our proof first uses III.27 for equal angles at the circumferences, then doubles them by III.20. The resulting angles standing at the centres on BGC and EHF are reflex; removing them from four right angles gives the ordinary angles BKC and ELF for I.4. This is an additional argument for a case within the enunciation, so the route remains modified. Equal angles at the centres place K and L alike (`Prop26.alike`), and the diameter case needs only the equal radii.

Heath adds that III.26–III.29 "are of course equally true if the *same* circle is taken instead of *two equal* circles" (vol. 2, p. 61). Our two circles may be one (`EqualCircles.refl`), for a circle's radii are equal to one another. Nothing in the proof needs two circles, though the figure draws two.

**The modern way.** Put the centre K at 0 in the complex plane, and let the radius be $r$. The arc whose angle at the centre is $\theta$, with $0 \lt \theta \lt 2\pi$, runs from B to B turned through $\theta$, that is, to $\mathrm{B}e^{i\theta}$. So the straight line joining its ends has length $|\mathrm{B}e^{i\theta} - \mathrm{B}| = r|e^{i\theta} - 1| = 2r\sin(\theta/2)$, which depends only on $r$ and $\theta$: in equal circles, equal arcs have equal straight lines joining their ends, arcs greater than a semicircle included.
