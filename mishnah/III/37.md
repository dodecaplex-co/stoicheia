**III.36 turned round.** III.36 goes from a straight line that touches to the equal square; III.37 comes back from the square to the touching. Euclid's way in is a second straight line from D, DE, that touches already. By III.36 its square is the rectangle too, so DE is equal to DB, and with the equal radii FE, FB and the common base FD, I.8 carries the right angle at E across to B. Euclid gives no citation for drawing DE or taking the centre F: they are III.17 and III.1. Nor does he for "therefore DE is equal to DB": equal squares stand on equal straight lines, as in III.14.

**Can a drawing hide an indirect proof?** Augustus De Morgan, Heath reports, finds here "the same defect as in I. 48, i.e. an apparent avoidance of indirect demonstration by drawing the tangent DE on the opposite side of DF from DB" (vol. 2, pp. 76–77). Drawn on DB's side, DE would need I.7, an indirect proof, to put E on B. Drawn on the other, it lets I.8 compare the angles; but I.8 rests on I.7, so the indirect argument is there all the same, out of sight.

**Is DBF a triangle?** Euclid's figure says so, and Lean asks why. If D, B and F lay on one straight line, either D would be within the circle, or two sides of the triangle DEF would together be equal to the third, which I.20 forbids; `Prop37.triangle` says so once. After that the proof works whichever straight line III.17 draws touching, even if E is B, for I.8 does not ask on which side of DF the triangles lie. Euclid's last case, with the centre on AC, needs nothing new: we cite III.36 by its statement, which holds wherever the centre lies.

**"The undisguised indirect proof is easy."** Heath first sets Johannes Campanus' proof beside Euclid's: through the centre, by II.6 and then I.48, "the very proposition to which De Morgan's original remark relates" (vol. 2, p. 77). Then he gives the open one: "If DB does not touch the circle, it must cut it if produced, and it follows that the square on DB must be equal to the rectangle contained by DB and a longer line: which is absurd" (vol. 2, p. 77). We keep Euclid's two triangles.

**The modern way.** With centre F and radius r, D's power is DA·DC = DF² − r², so the hypothesis gives DB² = DF² − FB², and hence (D − B)·(F − B) = 0: FB is perpendicular to DB. This is Campanus' route, by the converse of Pythagoras' theorem, rather than Euclid's.
