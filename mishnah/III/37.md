**A touching line to compare with.** III.36 gives an equality of areas from a straight line touching a circle. Euclid now starts with the equality and recovers the contact. The useful comparison is with DE, already known to touch: its length must equal DB, and the equal radii let I.8 carry the right angle at E across to B. We draw DE by III.17 and take F by III.1, the two constructions Euclid leaves without a bracket; both ask for the centre our statement gives the circle, since Lean's circles have one only by hypothesis. "Therefore DE is equal to DB" has no bracket either: equal squares stand on equal straight lines, which Lean proves once as `BookI.Prop46.sq`, bringing in I.3, I.4, I.28, I.34 and I.46.

**Can a drawing hide an indirect proof?** Augustus De Morgan sees the same difficulty here as in I.48. Heath reports "an apparent avoidance of indirect demonstration by drawing the tangent DE on the opposite side of DF from DB" (vol. 2, pp. 76–77). Put both triangles on the same side, and I.7 must show that their vertices coincide. Put them on opposite sides, and I.8 compares their angles. But I.8 itself rests on I.7, so the indirect argument is still there.

**Does DBF make a triangle?** Lean needs this before it can compare the angles. If D, B, F lay on one straight line, either D would lie within the circle, or two sides of the triangle DEF would together equal the third. We exclude the latter possibility by I.20 in `Prop37.triangle`. This is a fact Euclid leaves to his figure. The proof then works whichever touching line III.17 supplies, even if E is B: I.8 does not ask on which side the triangles lie. Euclid's final case, with F on AC, needs no new argument, since III.36 already covers it.

**"The undisguised indirect proof is easy."** Heath continues: "If DB does not touch the circle, it must cut it if produced, and it follows that the square on DB must be equal to the rectangle contained by DB and a longer line: which is absurd" (vol. 2, p. 77). He also gives Campanus' route through II.6 and I.48, drawing the straight line from D through the centre instead of a second touching line. We keep Euclid's comparison of the two triangles.

**The modern way.** With radius r, D's power is DA·DC = DF² − r², so the hypothesis gives DB² = DF² − FB² and hence (D − B)·(F − B) = 0: FB is perpendicular to DB. This is Campanus' route through the converse of Pythagoras' theorem in vector form, rather than Euclid's comparison.
