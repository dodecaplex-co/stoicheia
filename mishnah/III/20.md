**Double, whether added or taken away.** Euclid produces a straight line through the centre and finds two equal angles in the triangle made by two radii. The exterior angle at the centre is their sum. When E lies within the angle BAC, two such doubles are added; when E lies outside the angle BDC, one is taken from the other. We draw both arguments in one circle, as Heath does. Our statement keeps E on A's side of BC: the angle at the centre is less than two right angles, and both angles stand on the same circumference. Heron, as Heath reports from an-Nairīzī, extended the proposition to a segment less than a semicircle: the angles remaining at the centre, which fill up four right angles with BEC, are double of the angle in that segment, and so III.21 holds in every segment (vol. 2, p. 47).

**A shoemaker's knife.** The region bounded by two radii and the circumference between them is a sector. A scholiast, Heath reports, says that its Greek name came from the *shoemaker's knife*, whose shape it recalls (vol. 2, p. 5). Heath also quotes a wider definition found in a Greek scholiast and in an-Nairīzī: "There are two varieties of sectors; the one kind have the angular vertices at the centres, the other at the circumferences" (vol. 2, p. 5). Their exact agreement suggests Heron as the authority. III.20 sets the two sorts of vertex side by side: the angle at one is double the angle at the other.

**What if the centre lies on an arm?** Euclid leaves that case out. Heath approves: "Euclid's proof gives so obviously the means of proving this that it is properly left out" (vol. 2, p. 49). We write it out: the straight line through A and E reaches B or C, and one isosceles triangle gives the whole result. Lean also makes us find where AE produced crosses BC (`crossbar`) before treating one angle as part of another. In the subtraction case, EB must lie within the angle GEC; otherwise the words "which remains" would have no warrant.

**The modern way.** With E = 0, B = e^{iβ}, C = e^{iγ}, A = e^{iα} and β < γ < α < β + 2π, the quotient (C − A)/(B − A) = e^{i(γ−β)/2} · sin((α−γ)/2)/sin((α−β)/2) is a positive multiple of e^{i(γ−β)/2}. Thus the inscribed angle is half the central angle on the same arc; Euclid's case has γ − β < π.
