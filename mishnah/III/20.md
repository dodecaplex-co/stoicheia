**Double, whether added or taken away.** Euclid joins AE and draws it through to F. The triangle EAB has equal angles at A and B (I.5), and the exterior angle BEF is both together (I.32), so it is double of the angle EAB. At A the centre lies within the angle BAC, and two such doubles are added; at D it lies outside the angle BDC, and one is taken from the other.

**Can an angle be greater than two right angles?** Heath reports from an-Nairīzī that Heron carried the proposition to a segment less than a semicircle: the angles at the centre that fill up four right angles with BEC are double of the angle in it, and so III.21 holds in every segment (vol. 2, p. 47). To Euclid that sum was no angle: "Euclid had no idea of III. 20 applying *in terms* … to the case where the angle at the circumference, or the angle in the segment, is *obtuse*" (vol. 2, p. 48). So our statement keeps E on A's side of BC, where the angle at the centre is less than two right angles.

**A shoemaker's knife.** Two straight lines from the centre and the circumference they cut off make a sector (Definition III.10), and a scholiast says that the shoemaker's knife gave it its Greek name (Heath, vol. 2, p. 5). Heath quotes a wider definition, which he thinks is Heron's: "There are two varieties of sectors; the one kind have the angular vertices at the centres, the other at the circumferences" (vol. 2, p. 5). III.20 sets the two kinds side by side, and the angle at the centre is double.

**"Properly left out."** Euclid leaves out the case where AB or AC passes through the centre, and Heath approves: "Euclid's proof gives so obviously the means of proving this that it is properly left out" (vol. 2, p. 49). We write it out all the same, with one isosceles triangle. And we prove what Euclid reads off his figure: at A, that AE produced crosses BC between B and C (`crossbar`), so that BEF and FEC make up the angle BEC; at D, that B lies within the angle GEC, so that the angle BEC "remains".

**The modern way.** With E = 0, B = e^{iβ}, C = e^{iγ}, A = e^{iα} and β < γ < α < β + 2π, the quotient (C − A)/(B − A) = e^{i(γ−β)/2} · sin((α−γ)/2)/sin((α−β)/2) is a positive multiple of e^{i(γ−β)/2}. Thus the inscribed angle is half the central angle on the same arc; Euclid's case has γ − β < π.
