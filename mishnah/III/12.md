**"But I will show how."** Heath prints III.12 in square brackets. Heron, he reports, says on III.11: "Euclid in proposition 11 has supposed the two circles to touch internally … *But I will show how it is to be proved if the contact is external.*" Heron's proof and figure are substantially these, and Heath judges: "It is most probable that Theon or some other editor added Heron's proof in his edition and made Prop. 12 out of it" (vol. 2, p. 28). We state and prove it like the rest: it completes III.11, putting the point of external contact between the centres.

**The radii must not overlap.** Heath finds a condition in the figure, as in III.11: "Euclid's proof is valid provided only that, if FG, joining the assumed centres, meets the circle with centre F in C and the other circle in D, C is not within the circle ADE and D is not within the circle ABC" (vol. 2, p. 28). Were either within, the radii FC and GD would overlap along FG.

**Each centre is outside the other circle.** On our reading of Definition III.3 (see III.11), circles touching externally have no point within both, and from that we prove Heath's condition. Each centre is outside the other circle (`Prop12.centre_outside`): were it on that circle, a point a little towards the other centre would be within both. So FG leaves the circle ABC at C and enters ADE at D, and C is not within ADE, for the same reason. Then FC and GD, which are the radii FA and GA, are together not more than FG, even if C and D are one point, as Heath allows. But if A is off FG, FAG is a triangle, and I.20 makes FA and AG greater.

**Could A lie beyond a centre?** Heath names a case that Euclid "would in any case have left to the reader, namely that A does not lie on FG but on FG *produced* in either direction" (vol. 2, p. 29). Our proof closes it at once: one centre would then be within both circles. The conclusion keeps the double negative Heath finds in the Greek: the straight line "will not fail to pass" through A. Modern continental textbooks, Heath notes, take another road, from "the relation in which the distance between their centres stands to the length of their radii" (vol. 2, p. 30): the modern way below.

**The modern way.** Circles with centres F, G and positive radii R, r touch externally exactly when FG = R + r, and the point of contact is then A = (rF + RG)/(R + r), on FG between the centres.
