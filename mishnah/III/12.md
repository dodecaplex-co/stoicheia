**A proposition in brackets.** Heath brackets the whole of III.12 as an addition to Euclid's text. It completes III.11 by putting the point of external contact between the centres. Heron, as Heath reports him, says: "Euclid in proposition 11 has supposed the two circles to touch internally, made his proposition deal with this case and proved what was sought in it. *But I will show how it is to be proved if the contact is external.*" He then gives substantially this proof and figure (vol. 2, p. 28).

**Heron's proof becomes III.12.** Heath concludes: "It is most probable that Theon or some other editor added Heron's proof in his edition and made Prop. 12 out of it" (vol. 2, p. 28). The argument needs a fact about its picture: "Euclid's proof is valid provided only that, if FG, joining the assumed centres, meets the circle with centre F in C and the other circle in D, C is not within the circle ADE and D is not within the circle ABC" (vol. 2, p. 28). Without that separation, the two radii might overlap along FG.

**No room within both circles.** We express external contact by saying that the circles meet at A and no point lies within both, following the whole-circle reading of Definition III.3 discussed under III.11. Lean cites III.6 to distinguish the centres. So that FG may leave ABC at C and enter ADE at D, we prove that each centre is outside the other circle (`Prop12.centre_outside`): if it lay on that circle, a point a little towards the other centre would be within both. The same reasoning excludes C from the inside of ADE. FG is therefore not less than FA and AG together, even when C and D coincide, the possibility Heath expressly allows. I.20 says it is less if FAG is a triangle.

**Could A lie beyond a centre?** Heath names one case Euclid "would in any case have left to the reader, namely that A does not lie on FG but on FG *produced* in either direction" (vol. 2, p. 29). We exclude it because one centre would then lie within both circles. The conclusion keeps the Greek double negative that Heath points out: FG cannot fail to pass through A. His survey of continental textbooks then connects contact with the distance between the centres and the lengths of the radii (vol. 2, p. 30).

**The modern way.** Circles with centres F, G and positive radii R, r touch externally exactly when FG = R + r, and the point of contact is then A = (rF + RG)/(R + r), on FG between the centres.
