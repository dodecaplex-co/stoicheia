**Heron's addition to III.11.** Heath prints the whole of III.12 in square brackets. Heron, he reports, says on III.11: "Euclid in proposition 11 has supposed the two circles to touch internally … *But I will show how it is to be proved if the contact is external.*" He then gives substantially this proof and figure (vol. 2, p. 28), and Heath judges: "It is most probable that Theon or some other editor added Heron's proof in his edition and made Prop. 12 out of it" (vol. 2, p. 28). We state and prove it like the rest: it completes III.11, putting the point of external contact between the centres.

**The radii must not overlap.** Heath finds a condition in the figure, as in III.11: "Euclid's proof is valid provided only that, if FG, joining the assumed centres, meets the circle with centre F in C and the other circle in D, C is not within the circle ADE and D is not within the circle ABC" (vol. 2, p. 28). Were either within, the radii FC and GD would overlap along FG.

**Each centre is outside the other circle.** In our reading of Definition III.3 (see III.11), circles touching without meet at A and have no point within both. We prove Heath's condition, beginning with the centres: each is outside the other circle (`Prop12.centre_outside`), for were it on that circle, a point a little towards the other centre would be within both. So FG leaves ABC at C and enters ADE at D; C is not within ADE, by the same reasoning; and FG is not less than FA and AG together, even when C and D are one point, as Heath allows. I.20 says it is less, if FAG is a triangle.

**Could A lie beyond a centre?** Heath names a case that Euclid "would in any case have left to the reader, namely that A does not lie on FG but on FG *produced* in either direction" (vol. 2, p. 29). Lean closes it at once: one centre would then be within both circles. The conclusion keeps the double negative Heath finds in the Greek: the straight line "will not fail to pass" through A. Heath's survey of continental textbooks then ties contact to the distance between the centres and the lengths of the radii (vol. 2, p. 30).

**The modern way.** Circles with centres F, G and positive radii R, r touch externally exactly when FG = R + r, and the point of contact is then A = (rF + RG)/(R + r), on FG between the centres.
