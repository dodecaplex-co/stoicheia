**"Only two equal straight lines."** Of the straight lines from F to the circle, FA, through the centre E, is greatest, and FD, the rest of the diameter, is least. Between them Euclid puts everything in order: on either side of AD, the nearer a straight line is to FA, the greater it is, "nearer" meaning the less angle at F. That order does the last part too. Euclid makes FH equal to FG on the other side of AD, and a third would be "the nearer to the straight line through the centre being thus equal to the more remote: which is impossible". So "only two" needs nothing new.

**Nearer at which point?** Augustus De Morgan saw a gap here, and Heath passes it on: "De Morgan points out that there is an unproved assumption in this demonstration" (vol. 2, p. 15). The straight lines are ordered by their angles at F, but I.24 needs the angles at the centre, between equal radii, and Euclid reads off his figure that the angle BEF is greater than CEF. Heath proves it from the crossing of FB and EC, with the perpendicular from E on FB. H. M. Taylor avoids the question by ordering the straight lines by the angle at the centre, but Heath keeps Euclid's plan as "the more instructive and useful of the two" (vol. 2, p. 16).

**The crossing lies within.** We prove De Morgan's step by the circle, in place of Heath's perpendicular. The figure of I.24 puts FB within the angle EFC (`BookI.Prop24.within`), and the crossbar theorem finds the point where FB crosses EC (`crossbar`). That point is between E and C, so it is within the circle (System E, Circle 2), and it comes before B: were B between F and it, B too would be within. So EC falls within the angle BEF, and CEF is less than BEF, just what I.24 needs. Our statement speaks of every point of the circle, and a point of it on AD itself is A or D (System E, Circle 1), the greatest or the least.

**The modern way.** Put E at the origin, let the radius be r, and put F at distance d towards D, with 0 < d < r: a ray making angle θ with FA reaches the circle at distance ρ(θ) = d cos θ + √(r² − d² sin² θ). This strictly decreases from r + d to r − d as θ runs from 0 to π; the symmetry ρ(−θ) = ρ(θ) gives exactly two equal straight lines for every intermediate length, one on each side of AD.
