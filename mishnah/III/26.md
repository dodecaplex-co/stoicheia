**"The circumference BKC which remains."** Euclid has just shown, in III.24, when two segments are equal, and here he puts it to work. The circumference BKC is what remains when the segment BAC is taken from the whole circle, so equal segments in equal circles leave equal circumferences. Our statement takes Euclid's two cases one at a time, so where the angles are given at the centres, III.20 halves them for A and D.

**Where do the equal circles do their work?** At once, in "the radii are equal": with the equal angles at G and H, I.4 makes BC equal to EF, and III.24 has its segments on equal straight lines. The measure of a circumference, twice the angle standing on it, would need no equal circles: it makes every whole circle four right angles, whatever its radius, so equal angles at A and D give equal measures even in unequal circles. It is Euclid's route that asks for them.

**An angle too large for the figure.** Heath writes: "As in III. 21, if Euclid's proof is to cover all cases, it requires us to take cognisance of 'angles at the centre' which are equal to or greater than two right angles. Otherwise we must deal separately with the cases where the angle at the circumference is equal to or greater than a right angle" (vol. 2, p. 57). Lean's angles are never straight or reflex, so we take his separate cases, sorted as in III.24. If the angle at A is acute, III.20 doubles it at the centre; if right, BC and EF are diameters, each two radii; if obtuse, III.22 passes to the acute angle in the other segment, as Heath proposes.

**The modern way.** In radians, an arc of central angle θ (0 < θ < 2π) in a circle of radius r has length rθ, and every inscribed angle standing on it is θ/2. So in circles of equal radius, equal central angles, or equal inscribed angles, cut off arcs of equal length.
