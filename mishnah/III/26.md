**Equal parts leave equal parts.** Euclid reaches the circumferences BKC and ELF by first comparing the segments on the other side of BC and EF. Equal radii and equal angles at the centres give equal bases by I.4. Equal angles at A and D then give similar segments, which III.24 makes equal. We follow him through that subtraction: take equal segments from equal circles, and the circumferences left over are equal.

**What makes two circles equal?** Heath defends Definition III.1: "The equality of circles with equal radii can of course be proved by superposition, but, as we have seen, Euclid avoided this method wherever he could, and there is nothing technically wrong in saying 'By *equal circles* I mean circles with equal radii.'" (vol. 2, p. 2). We use precisely that definition. There is no need to move one circle onto the other before comparing their radii.

**An angle too large for the figure.** Heath asks what happens when A or D lies in a segment no greater than a semicircle: "As in III. 21, if Euclid's proof is to cover all cases, it requires us to take cognisance of 'angles at the centre' which are equal to or greater than two right angles. Otherwise we must deal separately with the cases where the angle at the circumference is equal to or greater than a right angle." (vol. 2, p. 57).

**Four right angles, whatever the radius.** Lean's angles cannot express a straight or reflex angle at the centre, so we take Heath's separate cases. III.20 supplies the missing pair of equal angles. Right angles give diameters; for obtuse angles, III.22 gives equal angles in the other segments. Our circumference measure also needs care: it counts the whole circle as four right angles, whatever the radius. The same angles therefore give equal measures even in unequal circles. Equal radii let this angular equality express Euclid's equality of circumferences, and they do real work when I.4 proves BC equal to EF.

**The modern way.** In radians, an arc of central angle θ (0 < θ < 2π) in a circle of radius r has length rθ, and every inscribed angle standing on it is θ/2; so in circles of equal radius, equal central angles, or equal inscribed angles, cut off arcs of equal length.
