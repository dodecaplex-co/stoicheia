**"The circumference BKC which remains."** Euclid has just shown, in III.24, when two segments are equal, and here he puts it to work. The circumference BKC is what remains when the segment BAC is taken from the whole circle, so equal segments in equal circles leave equal circumferences. Our statement takes Euclid's two cases one at a time, so where the angles are given at the centres, III.20 halves them for A and D.

**What makes two circles equal?** Heath defends Definition III.1: "The equality of circles with equal radii can of course be proved by superposition, but, as we have seen, Euclid avoided this method wherever he could, and there is nothing technically wrong in saying 'By *equal circles* I mean circles with equal radii'" (vol. 2, p. 2). Our `EqualCircles` says just that, a radius of the one equal to a radius of the other, and Euclid uses it at once: "the radii are equal."

**An angle too large for the figure.** Heath writes: "As in III. 21, if Euclid's proof is to cover all cases, it requires us to take cognisance of 'angles at the centre' which are equal to or greater than two right angles. Otherwise we must deal separately with the cases where the angle at the circumference is equal to or greater than a right angle" (vol. 2, p. 57). An angle in Lean is never straight or reflex, so we take his separate cases (`Prop26.angle_type`). If the angle at A is acute, III.20 doubles it at the centre; if right, BC and EF are diameters, each two radii; if obtuse, III.22 passes to the acute angle in the other segment, as Heath proposes.

**Four right angles, whatever the radius.** Lean measures a circumference by the angle in the other segment, taken twice. That makes every whole circle four right angles, whatever its radius, so equal angles at A and D would give equal circumferences even in unequal circles. Euclid's equal circles do their work where their equal radii make BC equal to EF (I.4), giving III.24 its segments on equal straight lines. A proof by the measure alone would leave them unused, and Lean's linter would say so.

**The modern way.** In radians, an arc of central angle θ (0 < θ < 2π) in a circle of radius r has length rθ, and every inscribed angle standing on it is θ/2. So in circles of equal radius, equal central angles, or equal inscribed angles, cut off arcs of equal length.
