**"The circumference BKC which remains."** Euclid has just shown, in III.24, when two segments are equal, and here he puts it to work. The circumference BKC is what remains when the segment BAC is taken from the whole circle, so equal segments in equal circles leave equal circumferences. Our statement takes Euclid's two cases one at a time, so where the angles are given at the centres, III.20 halves them for A and D.

**Where do the equal circles do their work?** In Euclid's proof, at once, in "the radii are equal": with the equal angles at G and H, I.4 makes BC equal to EF, and III.24 then has its similar segments on equal straight lines. In unequal circles the same angles stand on unequal straight lines, and the segments, though similar, are not equal. Our proof takes his route, but our statement would hold without the equal circles: a circumference is measured by twice an angle, which carries no radius, so equal angles give equal measures in any two circles. The equal circles are what make equal measures mean equal circumferences.

**An angle too large for the figure.** Heath writes: "As in III. 21, if Euclid's proof is to cover all cases, it requires us to take cognisance of 'angles at the centre' which are equal to or greater than two right angles. Otherwise we must deal separately with the cases where the angle at the circumference is equal to or greater than a right angle" (vol. 2, p. 57). Our angles are never straight or reflex, so we take his separate cases, sorted as in III.24. If the angle at A is acute, III.20 doubles it at the centre; if right, BC and EF are diameters, each two radii; if obtuse, III.22 passes to the acute angle in the other segment, as Heath proposes.

**The modern way.** In radians, an arc whose angle at the centre is θ, in a circle of radius r, has length rθ, and every angle at the circumference standing on it is θ/2. So in circles of equal radius, equal angles at the centres, or at the circumferences, cut off arcs of equal length.
