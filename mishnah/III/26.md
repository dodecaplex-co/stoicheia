**"The circumference BKC which remains."** Euclid has just shown, in III.24, when two segments are equal, and here he puts it to work. The circumference BKC is what remains when the segment BAC is taken from the whole circle, so equal segments in equal circles leave equal circumferences. We read "equal circumferences" as equal in measure and congruent, which says more than Heath's words. Both conclusions follow by subtraction. For the measures this is Common Notion 3. For congruence we extend the map of BAC onto EDF to the whole circles: short rays from a point within the segment determine the image of every point, and I.8 and I.4 keep their distances. The reverse construction makes the map onto; III.9 identifies the image of the centre. Restricting that same map to what remains gives the congruent remainders, with their common bases retained.

**Where do the equal circles do their work?** In Euclid's proof, at once, in "the radii are equal": with the equal angles at G and H, I.4 makes BC equal to EF, and III.24 then has its similar segments on equal straight lines. In unequal circles the same angles stand on unequal straight lines, and the segments, though similar, are not equal. Our statement needs them too: a circumference is measured by twice an angle, which carries no radius, so equal angles give equal measures in any two circles, but congruent circumferences only in equal ones. It takes Euclid's two cases one at a time, so where the angles are given at the centres, III.20 halves them for A and D.

For obtuse angles at A and D, the useful triangles lie in the opposite segments: III.22 gives equal
acute angles there by the edition's route. Euclid's acute argument reaches BAC
and EDF, and his subtraction then reaches BKC and ELF. When A and D are right angles, BC and EF are
diameters, so their equality follows by adding equal radii. The clause given at the centres concerns
the angles BGC and EHF, with G opposite K across BC and H opposite L across EF.

**The modern way.** In radians, an arc whose angle at the centre is θ, in a circle of radius r, has length rθ, and every angle at the circumference standing on it is θ/2. So in circles of equal radius, equal angles at the centres, or at the circumferences, cut off arcs of equal length, and a rigid motion carries the one onto the other.
