**III.26 turned round.** Euclid argues by reductio. Were the angle BGC greater than EHF, the angle BGK made equal to EHF within it would stand, by III.26, on a circumference BK equal to EF, and so to BC: the less equal to the greater. The equal circles do their work inside that appeal to III.26. Our figure draws the impossible case, with BGC the greater, so that K has somewhere to fall.

**The converse inherits the cases.** Heath writes: "This proposition is the converse of the preceding one, and the remarks about the method of treating the different cases apply here also" (vol. 2, p. 59). Those remarks, under III.26, take the obtuse angle through III.22, the right angle through the diameters, and give Dionysius Lardner's halving. Euclid's last step halves the angles at the centres by III.20, which works when the angles at the circumference are acute; at a right or obtuse angle there is no angle at the centre of his kind to halve. Our statement still asks for every size, since IV.11 needs obtuse angles, and Lean reaches them through its measure: a circumference is twice the angle standing on it.

**Where does K fall?** I.23 gives the angle BGK, but not where its side GK meets the circumference. We take GK equal to the radius GB, and System E, Diagram-segment 3, puts K on the circle (`on_circle_of_len_eq`). Then BK equal to BC would make the less angle at the centre equal to the greater. We give the reductio once and use it with the circles exchanged, as in I.26.

**The modern way.** A rigid motion of the plane, a translation followed by a rotation and perhaps a reflection, carries a circle onto any circle of the same radius and an arc onto any arc of the same length, and keeps every angle. So equal arcs subtend equal angles at the centres and, since the angles in one segment are all equal, at the circumferences too. It is not Euclid's route: he argues by reductio from III.26.
