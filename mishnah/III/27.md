**A circumference equal to its own part.** Euclid argues by *reductio ad absurdum*. Were the angle BGC greater than EHF, the angle BGK made equal to EHF within it would stand, by III.26, on a circumference BK equal to EF, and so to BC: the less equal to the greater. Our figure draws the impossible case, with BGC the greater, so that K has somewhere to fall.

**The converse inherits the cases.** Heath writes: "This proposition is the converse of the preceding one, and the remarks about the method of treating the different cases apply here also" (vol. 2, p. 59). Among those remarks, under III.26, is Dionysius Lardner's way with a right or obtuse angle: bisect it, and work with the halves. Euclid's last step halves the angles BGC, EHF at the centres by III.20. Those triangle angles stand on the same circumferences as the angles at A and D only when the latter are acute. Our statement still asks for every size, since IV.11 needs obtuse angles. Our proof halves by III.20 only where Euclid does, the angle at the centre less than two right angles, as III.20's companion `Prop20.special` states it, and treats an obtuse angle by passing to the circumferences that remain, on which acute angles stand: Euclid's own argument makes those equal, and III.22 gives back the angles at A and D, each what remains of two right angles. Where the angles are right, the centres lie on BC and EF, and there are no angles BGC, EHF of ours, since our angles are never straight; the angle at the centre is then two right angles, and our proof does not halve it: each angle is an angle in a semicircle, and only there are the angles at A and D read off our measure of circumferences.

**Where does K fall?** I.23 gives the angle BGK, but not where its side GK meets the circumference. We take GK equal to the radius GB, and the proved Diagram-segment 3 puts K on the circle (`on_circle_of_len_eq`): the circle drawn about G through K by Postulate 3 is the given circle, since the centre and radius agree. The measure then turns Euclid's impossibility back into angles: BK equal to BC would make the less angle BGK equal to the greater, BGC.

**The modern way.** A rigid motion of the plane carries any arc onto any arc of the same length in a circle of the same radius, and keeps every angle, so equal arcs subtend equal angles at the centres and, since the angles in one segment are all equal, at the circumferences too. It is not Euclid's route, which runs through III.26.
