**The less equal to the greater.** Euclid turns III.26 round by supposing that one angle at the centre is greater than the other. Inside the greater he constructs an angle equal to the less. III.26 then makes the circumference BK equal to EF, which was already equal to BC. The smaller circumference would equal the larger. The equal circles matter at that appeal to III.26: their equal radii, discussed under III.26, let the comparison pass from one circle to the other. Our figure shows the unequal angles of this impossible case.

**The converse inherits the cases.** Heath writes: "This proposition is the converse of the preceding one, and the remarks about the method of treating the different cases apply here also." (vol. 2, p. 59). Those remarks under III.26 cover the obtuse angle through III.22, the right angle, and Dionysius Lardner's halving. Euclid's final appeal to III.20 works when the angles at the circumference are acute. At a right or obtuse angle, the corresponding angle at the centre is no longer a rectilineal angle of the kind he uses. The enunciation still includes these cases.

**K on the circle.** I.23 gives the angle BGK, but not where its side GK meets the circumference. We take GK equal to the radius GB; System E, Diagram-segment 3, puts K on the circle (`on_circle_of_len_eq`). Lean measures BK and BC by their angles at the centre (`Circumference.centre`), so BK equal to BC would make the less angle equal to the greater. We prove the reductio once and use it with the circles exchanged, as in I.26. For right and obtuse angles, Lean uses `Circumference.standing`: a circumference is measured by twice the angle in the other segment, whose angles are equal by III.21. Thus every size is covered, including the obtuse angles IV.11 will need. Euclid takes this agreement of the measuring angles for granted whenever he speaks of a circumference.

**The modern way.** On a circle of radius r, an arc of length s subtends the angle s/(2r) (in radians) at every point of the rest of the circle and, when it is less than a semicircle, the angle s/r at the centre. So in circles of equal radius, equal arcs subtend equal angles.
