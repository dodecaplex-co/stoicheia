**A circumference equal to its own part.** Euclid argues by *reductio ad absurdum*. Were the angle BGC greater than EHF, the angle BGK made equal to EHF within it would stand, by III.26, on a circumference BK equal to EF, and so to BC: the less equal to the greater. Our figure draws the impossible case, with BGC the greater, so that K has somewhere to fall.

**The converse inherits the cases.** Heath writes: "This proposition is the converse of the preceding one, and the remarks about the method of treating the different cases apply here also" (vol. 2, p. 59). Among those remarks, under III.26, is Dionysius Lardner's way with a right or obtuse angle: bisect it, and work with the halves. Euclid's last step halves the angles BGC, EHF at the centres by III.20. Those triangle angles stand on the same circumferences as the angles at A and D only when the latter are acute. Our statement still asks for every size, since IV.11 needs obtuse angles. Another of those remarks says that Euclid's proof needs angles at the centre of two right angles or more if it is to cover every case, and our proof admits them (`angleAtCentre`, a reading of ours after Heath's notes): in Euclid's halving "the angle BGC" is the angle at the centre standing on BC, and III.20 halves it, whatever its size. Where the angles at A and D are right, the centres lie on BC and EF, and the angles at the centres are each two right angles, equal with no reductio. Where they are obtuse, the angles at the centres are greater than two right angles, and I.23 cannot make one for the reductio; so it runs on the angles BGC, EHF, which stand on the circumferences that remain, and the angles at the centres are what is left when those are taken from four right angles.

**Where does K fall?** I.23 gives the angle BGK, but not where its side GK meets the circumference. We take GK equal to the radius GB, and the proved Diagram-segment 3 puts K on the circle (`on_circle_of_len_eq`): were K within, it would be nearer G than the point where GK produced meets the circle, and were it without, farther than the point where GK crosses the circle, though each of those points is a radius from G. The measure then turns Euclid's impossibility back into angles: BK equal to BC would make the less angle BGK equal to the greater, BGC.

**The modern way.** A rigid motion of the plane carries any arc onto any arc of the same length in a circle of the same radius, and keeps every angle, so equal arcs subtend equal angles at the centres and, since the angles in one segment are all equal, at the circumferences too. It is not Euclid's route, which runs through III.26.
