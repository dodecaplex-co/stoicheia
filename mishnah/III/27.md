**A circumference equal to its own part.** Euclid argues by *reductio ad absurdum*. Were the angle BGC greater than EHF, the angle BGK made equal to EHF within it would stand, by III.26, on a circumference BK equal to EF, and so to BC: the less equal to the greater. Our figure draws the impossible case, with BGC the greater, so that K has somewhere to fall.

**The converse inherits the cases.** Heath writes: "This proposition is the converse of the preceding one, and the remarks about the method of treating the different cases apply here also" (vol. 2, p. 59). Among those remarks, under III.26, is Dionysius Lardner's way with a right or obtuse angle: bisect it, and work with the halves. Euclid's last step halves the angles at the centres by III.20, which works only when the angles at the circumference are acute. Our statement still asks for every size, since IV.11 needs obtuse angles, and Lean reaches them through its measure of circumferences.

**Where does K fall?** I.23 gives the angle BGK, but not where its side GK meets the circumference. We take GK equal to the radius GB, and System E, Diagram-segment 3, puts K on the circle (`on_circle_of_len_eq`). The measure then turns Euclid's impossibility back into angles: BK equal to BC would make the less angle BGK equal to the greater, BGC.

**The modern way.** A rigid motion of the plane carries any arc onto any arc of the same length in a circle of the same radius, and keeps every angle, so equal arcs subtend equal angles at the centres and, since the angles in one segment are all equal, at the circumferences too. It is not Euclid's route, which runs through III.26.
