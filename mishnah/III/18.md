Euclid shows that FC is perpendicular to DE by showing that no other straight line from F is. If FG were perpendicular, the right angle at G would make FC greater than FG (I.17, I.19); but FC is equal to FB, and FB is only part of FG.

Heath reads III.18 and III.19 together: "Just as III. 3 contains two *partial* converses of the Porism to III. 1, so the present proposition and the next give two partial converses of the corollary to III. 16" (vol. 2, p. 45). The pattern is III.3's, with new things: a straight line touching the circle, a straight line from the centre to the point of contact, and right angles there. III.18 gets the right angles, and III.19 the straight line through the centre. Heath's note on the enunciation gives the Greek for "the tangent", ἡ ἐφαπτομένη (vol. 2, p. 45), a participle that names the straight line by what it does: touching.

**Where does B come from?** Euclid's figure puts B on FG between F and G, where FG leaves the circle, and so takes G to be outside it. We prove it. G cannot be within, since no point of a straight line that touches is within; and it cannot be on the circle, or DE would meet the circle at both C and G, and so cut it. With G outside, we find B with `line_circle_between`: a straight line from a point within a circle to a point outside crosses it between them.

**Every touching straight line is III.16's.** III.16 compares two angles contained by a straight line and a circumference, the angle of the semicircle and the horn-like angle of contact, with rectilineal angles, for the straight line drawn at right angles to the diameter from its extremity. III.18 shows that every straight line touching a circle is that straight line, for the diameter through the point of contact stands at right angles to it. So the companions after the proof give III.16's angles to every touching straight line. Near the point of contact the angle of contact is the same, whatever straight line into the circle names its side of the circumference (`Prop18.contact_locallyEqual`); it is less than every acute rectilineal angle, and the angle of the semicircle greater (`Prop18.contact_lt`, `Prop18.semicircle_gt`); and every acute rectilineal angle can be placed there to be compared, by I.23. The order is Euclid's, III.18 and then III.16, and nothing later is cited. As in III.16, the comparisons are argued directly from its second part, that no straight line can be interposed between the touching straight line and the circumference, where Euclid argues by reductio.

**The modern way.** Write the straight line DE as the points $\mathrm{C} + t\mathbf{v}$, with $\mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}$. The foot of the perpendicular from F is the point of it nearest F, at $t = -\overrightarrow{\mathrm{FC}} \cdot \mathbf{v}/|\mathbf{v}|^2$. If DE touches, none of its points is within the circle, so none is nearer F than C; the foot is C itself, $t = 0$, and $\overrightarrow{\mathrm{FC}} \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$.
