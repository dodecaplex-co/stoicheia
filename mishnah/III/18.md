**"The less than the greater."** Euclid shows that FC is perpendicular to DE by showing that no other straight line from F is. If FG were perpendicular, the right angle at G would make FC greater than FG (I.17, I.19); but FC is equal to FB, and FB is only part of FG.

**"Two partial converses."** Heath reads III.18 and III.19 together: "Just as III. 3 contains two *partial* converses of the Porism to III. 1, so the present proposition and the next give two partial converses of the corollary to III. 16" (vol. 2, p. 45). His three things are a straight line touching the circle, a straight line from the centre to the point of contact, and right angles there: the porism gives the first from the other two, III.18 the third from the first two, and III.19 the second from the first and third. Heath's note on the enunciation gives the Greek for "the tangent", ἡ ἐφαπτομένη (vol. 2, p. 45), a participle that names the straight line by what it does: touching.

**Where does B come from?** Euclid's figure puts B on FG between F and G, where FG leaves the circle, and so takes G to be outside it. We prove it. G cannot be within, since no point of a straight line that touches is within; and it cannot be on the circle, or DE would meet the circle at both C and G, and so cut it. With G outside, we find B with `line_circle_between`: a straight line from a point within a circle to a point outside crosses it between them.

**The modern way.** The foot of the perpendicular from F to the straight line C + tv (v ≠ 0) is the point of it nearest F, at t = −(C − F)·v/|v|². If the straight line touches, none of its points is within the circle, so none is nearer F than C; the foot is C itself, t = 0, and (C − F)·v = 0.
