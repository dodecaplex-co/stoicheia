**The less than the greater.** Euclid draws a perpendicular from F to DE and asks whether it could fall anywhere but C. If its foot were G, the right angle would make FC greater than FG. Yet FC equals FB, and FB is part of FG. The contradiction settles where the perpendicular falls. We keep Heath's figure that cannot be: DE touches at C, while FG stands for the supposed perpendicular without making a right angle at G.

**Two partial converses.** Heath writes: "Just as III. 3 contains two *partial* converses of the Porism to III. 1, so the present proposition and the next give two partial converses of the corollary to III. 16" (vol. 2, p. 45). The three conditions are touching the circle, passing through its centre, and meeting at right angles at the point of contact. Each pair supplies the third. Heath also singles out "the tangent, ἡ ἐφαπτομένη" (vol. 2, p. 45): the Greek participle names the straight line by what it does, touching the circle.

**Where does B come from?** Euclid's figure supplies B between F and G. Lean asks why G is outside: it cannot be within, since DE touches, and cannot be on the circle, since C and G would then be two points in common. `Touches` reads Definition 2 as Heath explains under III.17. We find B with `line_circle_between`, which puts a crossing between an inside point and an outside point. The Lean cites I.12 for drawing FG, a step Euclid leaves without a citation. Showing that its foot is C then gives perpendicularity to the whole straight line, on either side of C.

**The modern way.** For the circle |X − F| = r and the straight line X = C + tv through its point C, with v ≠ 0, |C + tv − F|² = r² + 2t(C − F)·v + t²|v|², which is never less than r² exactly when (C − F)·v = 0: the straight line touches the circle exactly when it is perpendicular to the radius FC.
