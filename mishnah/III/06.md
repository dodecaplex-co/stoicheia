**One point ties the radii together.** Euclid supposes that the touching circles have a common centre F. Their common point C makes FC a radius of both. The straight line FEB then makes the difficulty visible: it reaches the inner circle before the outer, yet the supposed common centre would make those distances equal. We see the two circles in the figure and follow an argument that makes their separation impossible.

**The case left to the reader.** Heath explains why the figure shows contact within: "in his usual manner, he chose for demonstration the more difficult case, and left the other case (that of *external* contact) to the intelligence of the reader. It is indeed sufficiently self-evident that circles touching externally cannot have the same centre; but Euclid's proof can really be used for this case too" (vol. 2, p. 13). We keep that breadth: the statement covers contact within, with either circle inside the other, and contact without.

**Must E and B be different?** Camerer notices that Euclid seems to assume what III.13 will prove: circles touching at C cannot meet again. Heath answers that "it is only necessary that *one* line drawn from the assumed common centre should meet the circles in different points" (vol. 2, p. 13). The distinction matters. Euclid needs a straight line on which the smaller circle ends before the larger; he need not yet know how many points the two circumferences share. His figure supplies the order, while the words leave its justification to the reader.

**One circle, not two.** We draw FEB and compare FC with FB and FE in Lean, but the last step changes. The rule `circle_eq` says that a centre and radius fix a circle. Thus FE equal to FB makes the two circles one, without first proving E different from B. Under the whole-circle reading of Definition III.3, contact within requires distinct circles; contact without forbids any point within both, which the supposed common centre would be. Lean therefore closes every case at the same contradiction, with no need to find a centre by III.1: F belongs to the supposition being refuted.

**The modern way.** A circle with centre O and radius r is the set of points X with |X − O| = r; two circles about one centre O through a common point C both have r = |C − O|, so they are one circle, and two different circles about one centre never meet at all.
