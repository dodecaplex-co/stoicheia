**One point ties the radii together.** Euclid supposes that the touching circles have a common centre F. Their common point C makes FC a radius of both. The straight line FEB then makes the difficulty visible: it reaches the inner circle before the outer, yet the supposed common centre would make those distances equal. Euclid needs no earlier proposition for it, and Lean cites none.

**The case left to the reader.** Heath explains why the figure shows contact within: "in his usual manner, he chose for demonstration the more difficult case, and left the other case (that of *external* contact) to the intelligence of the reader. It is indeed sufficiently self-evident that circles touching externally cannot have the same centre; but Euclid's proof can really be used for this case too" (vol. 2, p. 13). We keep that breadth: the statement covers contact within, with either circle inside the other, and contact without.

**Must E and B be different?** J. G. Camerer remarks that Euclid seems to assume what III.13 will prove: circles touching within at C cannot meet again. Heath answers that "it is only necessary that *one* line drawn from the assumed common centre should meet the circles in different points" (vol. 2, p. 13). The distinction matters. Euclid needs a straight line on which the smaller circle ends before the larger; he need not yet know how many points the two circumferences share. Heath finds that order in the very notion of internal contact.

**One circle, not two.** We draw FEB and compare FC with FB and FE in Lean, but the last step changes. The rule `circle_eq` (System E, Diagram-segment 2) says that a centre and a radius fix a circle. Thus FE equal to FB makes the two circles one, without first proving E different from B. Lean reads Definition III.3 of the whole circle, as III.11 explains, and the definition closes each case: contact within is between two circles, and in contact without no point is within both, as the supposed common centre would be. No centre is taken by III.1: F belongs to the supposition being refuted.

**The modern way.** A circle with centre O and radius r is the set of points X with |X − O| = r; two circles about one centre O through a common point C both have r = |C − O|, so they are one circle, and two different circles about one centre never meet at all.
