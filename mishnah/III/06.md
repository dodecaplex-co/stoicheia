**One look along one straight line.** Euclid supposes that two touching circles have one centre, F, and lets the point of contact, C, settle it. FC is a radius of both circles. A straight line drawn from F at random, FEB, reaches the inner circle at E before the outer at B, and yet FE and FB would each be equal to FC: "the less to the greater: which is impossible".

**"To the intelligence of the reader."** Euclid's figure shows the circles touching within, and the English editions say "internally" in the enunciation, a word Heath calls an interpolation. The fact is, he writes, that "in his usual manner, he chose for demonstration the more difficult case, and left the other case (that of *external* contact) to the intelligence of the reader. It is indeed sufficiently self-evident that circles touching externally cannot have the same centre; but Euclid's proof can really be used for this case too" (vol. 2, p. 13). We take him at his word: our statement covers circles touching within, whichever is the inner, and circles touching without.

**Must E and B be two points?** J. G. Camerer thought the proof takes for granted what Euclid proves only in III.13, that circles touching within at C meet nowhere else. Heath answers that "it is only necessary that *one* line drawn from the assumed common centre should meet the circles in different points" (vol. 2, p. 13), and the very idea of touching within gives one: before the inner circle meets the outer, it has passed through points within it.

**A centre and a radius make one circle.** In Lean the last step goes another way, and Camerer's worry never arises. System E's rule `circle_eq` (Diagram-segment 2) says that a centre and a radius fix a circle, so FE equal to FB makes the two circles one, whether or not E and B are two points. Lean reads Definition III.3 of the whole circle, as III.11 explains, and the definition closes each case: circles touching within are two circles, and circles touching without have no point within both, though the common centre would be such a point.

**The modern way.** Circles of radii R and r that touch have their centres R + r apart if they touch without, and |R − r| apart if they touch within, where R ≠ r, since two circles of one radius cannot touch within. Neither distance is zero, so the centres differ.
