**The centre has nowhere else to go.** Euclid turns III.18 into a way of locating the centre. CA is already drawn at right angles to DE, so any proposed centre F must lie on CA: joining it to C gives another right angle with DE. Two different straight lines drawn from C on one side of DE cannot both be at right angles to it. Our figure follows Heath in putting F off AC, inside the angle ACE. It draws the supposition to be refuted, not the centre the conclusion requires.

**Three conditions, taken two at a time.** Continuing his discussion under III.18, Heath writes: "We may also regard III. 19 as a partial converse of III. 18" (vol. 2, p. 45). The three conditions are passing through the centre, passing through the point of contact, and being perpendicular to the tangent. III.18 starts with the first two; III.19 starts with the last two. The remaining pairing gives Heath's other converse: "a straight line drawn through the centre perpendicular to the tangent passes through the point of contact" (vol. 2, p. 46). Contact keeps the meaning of Definition 2, discussed under III.17.

**Which side of DE?** Lean lets D and E be any two points naming the touching straight line. We choose E₁ different from C, using E unless C is E, when D will do. The statement also lets A lie across DE from F, whereas Heath puts A on the circle. In that case we produce AC beyond C to A₁ by Postulate 2; the adjacent angle is right too. Now F and A₁ are on the same side, and `onRay_of_angle_eq` puts them on one ray from C. This is the same impossibility of making a part equal to its whole that Euclid expresses as "the less to the greater".

**The modern way.** For a nonzero tangent direction v, III.18 gives (C − F)·v = 0. Through C there is one straight line perpendicular to v: X = C + s(C − F), which passes through the centre F at s = −1.
