**The centre has nowhere else to go.** Euclid turns III.18 into a way of finding where the centre lies. CA is drawn at right angles to DE at C; were the centre a point F off CA, the straight line FC would make a second right angle with DE, by III.18. But two straight lines from C on one side of DE cannot both be at right angles to it: the angle FCE would be equal to the angle ACE, "the less to the greater". Like Heath, we draw F off AC, inside the angle ACE, where the supposition puts it.

**A converse left over.** Heath goes on counting after III.18: "We may also regard III. 19 as a partial converse of III. 18" (vol. 2, p. 45). Now the three things are a straight line through the centre, one through the point of contact, and one perpendicular to the tangent. III.18 gives the third from the first two, and III.19 gives the first from the last two. The pairing left over is a converse Euclid does not state: "a straight line drawn through the centre perpendicular to the tangent passes through the point of contact" (vol. 2, p. 46).

**Which side of DE?** Our statement asks less of A than Heath's figure does. A is any point off DE with CA at right angles to it, not necessarily on the circle, and so it may lie on the other side of DE from the centre. Then we produce AC beyond C (Postulate 2), and the angle there is right too, so the straight line at right angles reaches F's side after all. On one side of DE only one ray from C makes a right angle with it (`onRay_of_angle_eq`), and that is Euclid's "the less to the greater": an angle cannot be equal to a part of itself.

**The modern way.** For a nonzero tangent direction v, III.18 gives (C − F)·v = 0. Through C there is one straight line perpendicular to v: X = C + s(C − F), which passes through the centre F at s = −1.
