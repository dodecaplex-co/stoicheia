**The centre has nowhere else to go.** Euclid turns III.18 into a way of finding where the centre lies. CA is drawn at right angles to DE at C; were the centre a point F off CA, the straight line FC would make a second right angle with DE, by III.18. But two straight lines from C on one side of DE cannot both be at right angles to it: the angle FCE would be equal to the angle ACE, "the less to the greater".

**A converse left over.** Heath goes on counting after III.18: "We may also regard III. 19 as a partial converse of III. 18" (vol. 2, p. 45). This time the three things are passing through the centre, passing through the point of contact, and standing at right angles to the tangent. A straight line with the first two has the third (III.18); one with the last two has the first (III.19). The pairing left over is a converse Euclid does not state: "a straight line drawn through the centre perpendicular to the tangent passes through the point of contact" (vol. 2, p. 46).

**Any A off DE will do.** Our statement asks less of A than Heath's figure does: CA must be at right angles to DE, but A need not be on the circle, and so it may lie on the other side of DE from the centre. Then we produce AC beyond C (Postulate 2), and the angle there is right too, so the straight line at right angles reaches F's side after all. On F's side of DE only one ray from C makes a right angle with it (`onRay_of_angle_eq`, as in I.4), and that is Euclid's "the less to the greater".

**The modern way.** For a nonzero tangent direction v, III.18 gives (C − F)·v = 0. Through C there is one straight line perpendicular to v: X = C + s(C − F), which passes through the centre F at s = −1.
