**Three points are enough.** Euclid supposes two circles meeting at four points, B, G, F and H, and uses three. He joins BH and BG, bisects them at K and L, and draws KC and LM at right angles to them. By the porism to III.1 the centre of each circle lies on both straight lines, and they meet only at P, so the two circles have one centre, which III.5 forbids. F never enters the argument, and our statement says it as I.7 does: two circles with three points in common are one circle. We keep Heath's oval for DEF, since no true circle can play the part the supposition asks of it.

**Must they cut?** Heath writes: "There is nothing in the demonstration of this proposition which assumes that the circles *cut* one another; it proves that two circles cannot *meet* at more than two points, whether they cut or meet without cutting, i.e. *touch* one another" (vol. 2, p. 23). Here, as his footnote says, "circle" means the circumference. Three common points are all the statement asks, and B and G give the meeting that III.5 needs. Heath also reports Heron's proof by III.9, which he calls incomplete: it assumes that the centre of the one circle is within the other (vol. 2, p. 24).

**Why do the perpendiculars meet?** Heath writes: "Euclid's proof contains an unproved assumption, namely that the lines bisecting *BG*, *BH* at right angles *will* meet in a point *P*" (vol. 2, p. 24). Our circles carry their centres, so the porism to III.1 puts the centre of each on both straight lines, and Heath's gap closes by itself. Lean asks only that AC and NO be two straight lines, and I.17 gives it, as in III.9 (`Prop10.meet_once`). Euclid gives no citation for the bisections or the right angles: they are I.10 and I.11, and the Lean cites them.

**The modern way.** The equations x² + y² + a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and x² + y² + a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0 of two different circles subtract to (a₁ − a₂)x + (b₁ − b₂)y + c₁ − c₂ = 0. That is a straight line (the radical axis) when the centres differ, and has no points at all when they are the same; and a straight line meets a circle in at most two points.
