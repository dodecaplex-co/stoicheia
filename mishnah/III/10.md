**Three points are enough.** Euclid joins BH and BG, bisects them at K and L, and draws the perpendiculars through those points. Their common point must be the centre of each circle, by III.1's porism, and III.5 forbids this for different circles that meet. We need only B, G and H; F never enters the argument. We keep Heath's oval for DEF: no true circle can play the part that the contradiction supposes. Heath also gives Heron's proof by III.9, which he calls incomplete because it assumes the first circle's centre lies within the second (vol. 2, p. 24).

**Must they cut?** Heath writes: "There is nothing in the demonstration of this proposition which assumes that the circles *cut* one another; it proves that two circles cannot *meet* at more than two points, whether they cut or meet without cutting, i.e. *touch* one another" (vol. 2, p. 23). As his footnote says, circle here means its circumference; his notes on meeting and touching are discussed under III.17 and III.11. Our statement accordingly asks only for three common points. B and G alone give the meeting that III.5 needs.

**Why do the perpendiculars meet?** Heath writes: "Euclid's proof contains an unproved assumption, namely that the lines bisecting BG, BH at right angles *will* meet in a point P" (vol. 2, p. 24). Our circles carry their centres, so III.1's porism puts a known point on both straight lines. Lean must still establish that AC and NO are different. If they coincided, BKL would have two right angles; `Prop10.meet_once` cites I.17 to rule that out. It also cites I.10 for the bisections and I.11 for the perpendiculars, which Euclid gives without citations.

**The modern way.** The equations x² + y² + a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and x² + y² + a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0 of two different circles subtract to (a₁ − a₂)x + (b₁ − b₂)y + c₁ − c₂ = 0. That is a straight line (the radical axis) when the centres differ, and has no points at all when they are the same; and a straight line meets a circle in at most two points.
