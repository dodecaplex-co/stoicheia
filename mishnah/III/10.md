Euclid supposes two circles meeting at four points, B, G, F and H, and uses three. He joins BH and BG, bisects them at K and L, and draws KC and LM at right angles to them. By the porism to III.1 the centre of each circle lies on both straight lines, and they meet only at P, so the two circles have one centre, which Euclid rules out by III.5. F never enters the argument, so our statement asks for three common points, and it says the proposition positively: two circles with three distinct points in common are one circle. Our figure keeps Heath's oval for DEF, since no second circle can meet ABC in the four points the supposition asks for.

**Must they cut?** Heath writes: "There is nothing in the demonstration of this proposition which assumes that the circles *cut* one another; it proves that two circles cannot *meet* at more than two points, whether they cut or meet without cutting, i.e. *touch* one another" (vol. 2, p. 23). Here, as his note on the enunciation says, "circle" means the circumference. Our statement keeps that scope: three distinct common points are all it asks. That matters at the last step, for our III.5 asks that the circles cross, each with a point within the other, and here they are given only common points. So we prove, after III.8, what the last step takes for granted: two different circles that meet at two points cut one another (`CirclesCut.of_meet`). Their centres are two points, since one centre and a common point would make the circles one (`circle_eq`), as Heath states under III.5 of two circles with the same centre and one point of their circumferences in common (vol. 2, p. 12). From the centre of either circle, the least straight line to the other circle lies along the straight line through the centres, by III.7 when that centre is within the other circle and by III.8 when it is outside; it ends nearer the centre than the common points are, and so within the first circle. A centre on the other circle is itself such a point. With B and G the circles cut, and III.5, Heath's reference here, forbids their one centre P. So two different circles cannot meet at two points without cutting, and III.5 serves for every pair of circles Heath's note admits. Heath also reports Heron's proof by III.9, which he calls incomplete: it assumes that the centre of the one circle is within the other (vol. 2, p. 24).

"Euclid's proof contains an unproved assumption," Heath observes, "namely that the lines bisecting BG, BH at right angles *will* meet in a point P" (vol. 2, p. 24). The rule `exists_centre` gives each circle a centre, and by the porism each centre is a point on both straight lines, so the rule closes Heath's gap. What remains is that AC and NO are two straight lines, and I.17 gives it (`Prop10.meet_once`), as in III.9: were they one, the triangle BKL would have two right angles. Since they meet only once, the two centres are one point.

**The modern way.** Two different circles have equations $x^2 + y^2 + a_{1}x + b_{1}y + c_{1} = 0$ and $x^2 + y^2 + a_{2}x + b_{2}y + c_{2} = 0$, and their common points satisfy the difference, $(a_{1} - a_{2})x + (b_{1} - b_{2})y + c_{1} - c_{2} = 0$. When the centres differ, that is a straight line, the radical axis; when they are one point, it has no points at all, for the circles are different. A straight line meets a circle in at most two points, so two different circles have at most two in common.
