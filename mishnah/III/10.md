**Three points suffice.** Euclid supposes two circles meeting at four points, B, G, F and H, and uses three. He joins BH and BG, bisects them at K and L, and draws KC and LM at right angles to them. By the porism to III.1 the centre of each circle lies on both straight lines. They meet only at P, so both circles have that centre, which Euclid rules out by III.5. F never enters the argument. We state the conclusion positively: two circles with three distinct common points are one circle. Our figure keeps Heath's oval for DEF, since no second circle can meet ABC in the four points the supposition asks for.

Heath observes that the demonstration needs only meeting, whether the circles cut or touch (vol. 2, p. 23). Our statement keeps that scope. This matters at the last step: our III.5 asks that the circles cross, with a point of each within the other, while III.10 is given only common points. Once both centres are P, the Lean uses `circle_eq` directly. One centre and the common point B make the radii equal and hence make the circles one. This is the converse Heath gives under III.5 (vol. 2, p. 12), and a change to Euclid's final inference. The construction and the argument locating the centres remain his.

**Why do the perpendiculars meet?** Heath points out that Euclid assumes their meeting at P without proof (vol. 2, p. 24). Our statement gives each circle a centre, and the porism puts each centre on both perpendiculars, supplying a meeting point. We must still show that AC and NO are different straight lines. Were they one, the triangle BKL would have two right angles, contrary to I.17 (`Prop10.meet_once`). Their unique meeting then identifies the two centres.

**The modern way.** Subtract the equations of two different circles. If their centres differ, their common points lie on a straight line, the radical axis, which meets either circle at at most two points. If their centres agree, their radii differ and they have no common point. Three distinct common points therefore force the circles to coincide.
