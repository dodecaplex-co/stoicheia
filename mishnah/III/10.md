Euclid supposes two circles meeting at four points, B, G, F and H, and uses three. He joins BH and BG, bisects them at K and L, and draws KC and LM at right angles to them. By the porism to III.1 the centre of each circle lies on both straight lines, and they meet only at P, so the two circles have one centre, which III.5 forbids. F never enters the argument, and so we state the proposition positively: two circles with three points in common are one circle.

**Must they cut?** Heath writes: "There is nothing in the demonstration of this proposition which assumes that the circles *cut* one another; it proves that two circles cannot *meet* at more than two points, whether they cut or meet without cutting, i.e. *touch* one another" (vol. 2, p. 23). Here, as his footnote says, "circle" means the circumference. Three common points are all the statement asks, and B and G give the meeting that III.5 needs. Heath also reports Heron's proof by III.9, which he calls incomplete: it assumes that the centre of the one circle is within the other (vol. 2, p. 24).

"Euclid's proof contains an unproved assumption," Heath observes, "namely that the lines bisecting BG, BH at right angles *will* meet in a point P" (vol. 2, p. 24). Our circles carry their centres, and each centre is a point on both straight lines, so Heath's gap closes by itself. What remains is that AC and NO are two straight lines, and I.17 gives it, as in III.9 (`Prop10.meet_once`).

**The modern way.** Two different circles have equations $x^2 + y^2 + a_{1}x + b_{1}y + c_{1} = 0$ and $x^2 + y^2 + a_{2}x + b_{2}y + c_{2} = 0$, and their common points satisfy the difference, $(a_{1} - a_{2})x + (b_{1} - b_{2})y + c_{1} - c_{2} = 0$. When the centres differ, that is a straight line, the radical axis; when they are one point, it has no points at all, for the circles are different. A straight line meets a circle in at most two points, so two different circles have at most two in common.
