**"The less to the greater."** Euclid tests a supposed common centre by drawing a straight line through it. In his figure F comes before G, so EF cannot equal EG; yet both would equal EC. The picture lets us follow the supposition until it breaks. We keep his construction and both comparisons of radii. No figure can show two different circles that really have the same centre and also cut one another.

**Must the circles cut?** Heath sees a single theorem behind this proposition and III.6: "The propositions III. 5, 6 could be combined in one. It makes no difference whether the circles cut, or meet without cutting, so long as they do not coincide altogether; in either case they cannot have the same centre" (vol. 2, p. 12). He then turns the statement round: "*If two circles have the same centre and one point in their circumferences common, they must coincide altogether*" (vol. 2, p. 12). Meeting at one point already settles the question.

**One common point fixes the circle.** Lean gives a circle a centre only through a hypothesis, `Centre`, and we must also say that ABC and CDG are different circles. Otherwise the same circle would satisfy the condition of meeting at two points. The System E rule `circle_eq`, that a centre and a radius fix a circle, does the work of Heath's last sentence. After Euclid's equalities give EF equal to EG, that rule makes ABC and CDG one circle, contradicting their being two. The proof uses C alone of the common points, so its reasoning also proves Heath's combined enunciation. III.6 takes the same route, and III.10 later cites III.5.

**The modern way.** A circle with centre E and radius r is the set of points at distance r from E. Two circles about E through C both have radius |EC| and are the same set: different circles about one centre never meet.
