**What does the figure show?** Euclid supposes that the circles ABC and CDG, which cut one another at B and C, have one centre E, and lets EFG be drawn through at random. Then EF and EG are each equal to EC, and so to one another; yet in his figure F comes before G, and EF is the less. No figure can show two circles that cut and share a centre, so his shows us the supposition at the moment it fails.

**"Could be combined in one."** So Heath says of III.5 and III.6, and gives his reason: "It makes no difference whether the circles cut, or meet without cutting, so long as they do not coincide altogether; in either case they cannot have the same centre" (vol. 2, p. 12). He gives the combined enunciation, "*If the circumferences of two circles meet at a point they cannot have the same centre*", and turns it round: "*If two circles have the same centre and one point in their circumferences common, they must coincide altogether*" (vol. 2, p. 12).

**One centre and one point make one circle.** Heath's last sentence is one of our tacit rules, `circle_eq` (System E, Diagram-segment 2): a circle is fixed by its centre and its radius. So where Euclid sees F before G, our proof concludes from EF equal to EG that ABC and CDG are one circle. We therefore state that they are two, as Euclid's figure takes for granted: one circle taken twice meets itself everywhere and shares its own centre. The proof uses only C of the common points, so its argument proves Heath's combined enunciation as it stands, and III.6 goes the same way.

**The modern way.** A circle with centre E and radius r is the set of points at distance r from E. Two circles about E through C both have radius |EC| and are the same set: different circles about one centre never meet.
