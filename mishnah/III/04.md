**A common middle point would force two right angles.** Euclid supposes that AC and BD bisect one another at E, takes the centre F, and joins FE. The work of III.3 now pays twice: FE would cut both straight lines at right angles. The exclusion of the centre matters. Two diameters do bisect one another there, since their halves are radii. A diameter is a straight line through the centre with both ends on the circumference, as Definition I.17 says. We also need two different straight lines: naming the same one twice would give no contradiction.

**The part cannot equal the whole.** In Heath's figure EA lies within the angle FEB. The angle FEA is therefore part of FEB, with the angle AEB still to be added. Yet the supposed bisections have made both FEA and FEB right. Euclid's closing words, "the less to the greater: which is impossible" (vol. 2, p. 12), bring Common Notion 5 to bear on angles: the whole is greater than the part. The drawing shows where the contradiction comes from; it cannot show the two bisections that the proof supposes.

**Which end lies on A's side?** We cannot require every configuration to put B where Heath draws it. Because E lies between B and D, FE separates those two points, and one of them lies on A's side. If that point is D, III.3 has already made FED right as well. Lean uses `onRay_of_angle_eq`: on the same side of FE, two equal angles with the arm EF must have their other arms along the same ray. Its proof is precisely the part-and-whole argument. Applied to B or D, it would put BD on AC, contradicting our two different straight lines.

**The modern way.** With the centre at the origin, a common midpoint would give e = (a + c)/2 = (b + d)/2, so e · (c − a) = (|c|² − |a|²)/2 = 0, and likewise e · (d − b) = 0; the two distinct intersecting lines have independent directions, hence e = 0, the centre, through which neither passes.
