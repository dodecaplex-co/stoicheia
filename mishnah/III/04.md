**Two right angles where there is room for one.** Euclid supposes that AC and BD bisect one another at E, takes the centre F, and joins FE. Now III.3 pays twice: FE would be at right angles to both straight lines, so the angles FEA and FEB would both be right. In Heath's figure EA lies within the angle FEB, so FEA is only a part of FEB, and Euclid ends on Common Notion 5, the whole greater than the part: "the less to the greater: which is impossible."

**Why "not through the centre"?** Without it the proposition is false. Two diameters, straight lines through the centre with their ends on the circle (Definition I.17), do bisect one another, at the centre, since their halves are radii. And the two straight lines must really be two: AC named twice bisects itself, and nothing goes wrong. Our statement says both, and the proof uses both.

**Which end lies on A's side?** Euclid draws B on A's side of FE. We cannot ask every figure to do the same, so Lean asks which of B and D is there. FE separates them, since E is between B and D, and III.3 has made the angle FED right as well as FEB. Whichever it is, `onRay_of_angle_eq` puts it on the straight line EA: on one side of FE, equal angles at E have their other arms along one ray. That lemma's proof is Euclid's part and whole, and here it would lay BD along AC.

**The modern way.** With the centre at the origin, a common midpoint would give e = (a + c)/2 = (b + d)/2, so e·(c − a) = (|c|² − |a|²)/2 = 0 and likewise e·(d − b) = 0. Two lines that cross have independent directions, so e = 0, the centre, through which neither passes.
