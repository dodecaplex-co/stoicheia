**II.6 comes back to the circle.** III.35 cut within the circle and called on II.5; here D lies on AC produced, and II.6 takes its place. When DCA passes through the centre F, II.6 makes the rectangle AD, DC together with the square on the radius FC equal to the square on FD, and I.47, at the right angle FBD (III.18), makes that the squares on FB, BD. Take the square on the radius from each, and the rectangle AD, DC equals the square on DB. When DCA misses the centre E, the perpendicular EF bisects AC instead (III.3), and adding the square on FE turns each side into a square on a straight line from the centre.

**Why give the tangent its own proof?** We would first show that two straight lines from D which cut the circle give equal rectangles, then turn one of them until its two points on the circle come together at the point of contact. Heath explains that "Euclid and the Greek geometers generally did not allow themselves to infer the truth of a proposition in a *limiting case* directly from the general case including it, but preferred a separate proof of the limiting case", and concludes, "This accounts for the form of III. 36" (vol. 2, p. 75).

**F has two jobs.** We keep Euclid's letters as Heath prints them: F is the centre in the first figure and the foot of the perpendicular in the second, whose centre is E. Taking the centre and drawing EF are III.1 and I.12, as in III.35, though Euclid cites neither. And FBD is a triangle for I.47 because the centre is never on a straight line that touches, as III.18 found.

**The modern way.** Put E at the origin, let the radius be r and u a unit vector from D towards A: the equation t² + 2(D·u)t + ED² − r² = 0 has the positive roots DC and DA, so DA·DC = ED² − r². Since EBD is a right angle, DB² = ED² − r² too: both are the power of D, found here by coordinates rather than by Euclid's squares.
