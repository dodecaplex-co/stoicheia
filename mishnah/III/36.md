**II.6 comes back to the circle.** When DCA passes through the centre F, AC is bisected at F and produced to D, and II.6 makes the rectangle AD, DC together with the square on FC equal to the square on FD. FC is a radius, and so is FB, at right angles to DB (III.18), so I.47 makes the square on FD equal to the squares on FB, BD as well. Take the square on the radius from each, and the rectangle AD, DC is left equal to the square on DB. When DCA misses the centre E, the perpendicular EF bisects AC instead (III.3), and adding the square on FE turns each side into a square on a straight line from the centre.

**Why give the tangent its own proof?** A modern reader would first show that two straight lines from D which cut the circle give equal rectangles, then turn one of them until its two points on the circle come together at the point of contact. Heath explains that "Euclid and the Greek geometers generally did not allow themselves to infer the truth of a proposition in a *limiting case* directly from the general case including it, but preferred a separate proof of the limiting case", and concludes, "This accounts for the form of III. 36" (vol. 2, p. 75).

**F has two jobs.** We keep Euclid's letters as Heath prints them: F is the centre in the first figure and the foot of the perpendicular in the second, whose centre is E. Euclid gives no citation for taking the centre or drawing EF: they are III.1 and I.12. Before I.47 can speak of the triangle FBD, Lean asks why F is not on DB, and the answer is short: the centre is within the circle, and no point of a straight line touching it is within. We answer it the same way for E in the second figure.

**The modern way.** Put E at the origin, let the radius be r and u a unit vector from D towards A: the equation t² + 2(D·u)t + ED² − r² = 0 has the positive roots DC and DA, so DA·DC = ED² − r². Since EBD is a right angle, DB² = ED² − r² too: both are the power of D, found here by coordinates rather than by Euclid's squares.
