**Two routes to the same square.** Euclid brings II.6 back to the circle. When DCA passes through the centre F, the two halves of AC are radii, and II.6 relates the rectangle contained by AD, DC to the squares on FC and FD. The right angle at B lets I.47 supply the same square on FD in another way. When DCA misses the centre, the perpendicular EF supplies a midpoint instead. We then add the square on FE to both sides: I.47 turns the two sums into squares on straight lines from the centre. Each case ends by taking away the square on a radius.

**Why give the tangent its own proof?** A modern reader would first show that two straight lines from D which cut the circle give equal rectangles, then turn one of them until the two points where it cuts come together at the point of contact. Heath explains that "Euclid and the Greek geometers generally did not allow themselves to infer the truth of a proposition in a *limiting case* directly from the general case including it, but preferred a separate proof of the limiting case" (vol. 2, p. 75). He concludes: "This accounts for the form of III. 36" (vol. 2, p. 75).

**F has two jobs.** We keep Heath's change of letters: F is the centre in the first figure and the foot of the perpendicular in the second, whose centre is E. The Lean takes each centre by III.1 and draws EF by I.12, constructions Euclid leaves without citations here. Before III.3 can bisect AC, Lean also needs F strictly between A and C, as in III.35, and `foot_between` gives it by I.17 and I.19. `BookI.Prop47.sq` describes the squares of I.47 by I.46 and names each by its side through I.4, I.28 and I.34.

**The modern way.** Put E at the origin, let the radius be r and u a unit vector from D towards A: the equation t² + 2(D·u)t + ED² − r² = 0 has positive roots DC and DA, so DA·DC = ED² − r². Since EBD is a right angle, DB² = ED² − r² too, the power of D, obtained here by coordinates rather than Euclid's additions and subtractions of squares.
