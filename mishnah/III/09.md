**Three equal straight lines find the centre.** Euclid turns equal lengths into right angles. Bisect AB and BC, and I.8 makes the adjacent angles at their middle points equal. The porism to III.1 then puts the centre on each cutting straight line. Both pass through D, so their meeting identifies D as the centre. We return to the construction that opened Book III, now using it to recognise a centre already given.

**The same fact, proved again.** Heath credits Augustus De Morgan with the observation: "III. 9 is, as De Morgan remarks, a *logical* equivalent of part of III. 7, where it is proved that every *non*-central point is *not* a point from which three equal straight lines can be drawn to the circle" (vol. 2, p. 22). The conclusion could follow just by turning that denial round. Yet, Heath continues, "Euclid does not allow himself these logical inferences, as we shall have occasion to observe elsewhere also" (vol. 2, p. 22). The return to III.1 is deliberate.

**What if AB passes through D?** Heath writes: "Camerer remarks that the genuine proof should also have contemplated the case in which one or other of the straight lines *AB*, *BC* passes through *D*" (vol. 2, p. 22). One of the middle points would then be D itself, and the joining straight line would disappear. Heath calls including this case "a departure from Euclid's manner of taking the most obscure case for proof and leaving others to the reader" (vol. 2, p. 22).

**Two cutting straight lines, not one.** Lean makes us finish the diagram's argument: if GK and HL coincided, BEF would have two right angles, contrary to I.17. It also cites I.10 for the bisections. For Camerer's case we join and bisect CA, then repeat Euclid's argument on the two straight lines D is off. Our statement leaves out the requirement that D be within the circle: ED and FD are drawn through from E and F, which are within, and the proof never uses D's position. This lets III.25 use the result before its proposed centre is known to be within.

**The modern way.** The equation |X − A| = |X − B| becomes 2X·(B − A) = |B|² − |A|², the straight line through the midpoint of AB perpendicular to it. For noncollinear A, B, C, this line and the corresponding line for BC meet in exactly one point, and the centre and D both lie on each.
