**Three equal straight lines find the centre.** Euclid turns equal lengths into right angles. He bisects AB and BC at E and F, and I.8 makes the two angles at E equal, and so right, and those at F too. By the porism to III.1 the centre lies on each of the straight lines GK and HL, and these have only D in common, so D is the centre. The construction that opened Book III comes back, now to recognise the centre in a given point.

**A logical equivalent, proved again.** Augustus De Morgan saw it already proved: "III. 9 is, as De Morgan remarks, a *logical* equivalent of part of III. 7, where it is proved that every *non*-central point is *not* a point from which three equal straight lines can be drawn to the circle" (vol. 2, p. 22). H. M. Taylor draws III.9 from his own III.7 as a corollary. Euclid proves it afresh, and Heath sees a habit in that: "Euclid does not allow himself these logical inferences, as we shall have occasion to observe elsewhere also" (vol. 2, p. 22).

**What if AB passes through D?** J. G. Camerer asks it: then one of the middle points is D itself, and ED or FD is no straight line at all. Heath answers that Euclid's manner is to take "the most obscure case for proof" and leave the others to the reader (vol. 2, p. 22). Lean leaves nothing to the reader. D is none of A, B, C, so at most one of AB, BC, CA passes through it; then we bisect CA as well and run Euclid's argument on the two that miss D.

**Within, or anywhere?** Euclid takes D within the circle, and our statement leaves "within" out. His proof never uses it: ED and FD are drawn through from E and F, which are within wherever D is, and III.25 needs the proposition so, for a point not yet known to be within. Lean asks for one thing the figure shows, that GK and HL are two straight lines: were they one, the triangle BEF would have two right angles, against I.17. Euclid gives no citation for the bisections: it is I.10, and the Lean cites it.

**The modern way.** The conditions |X − A| = |X − B| = |X − C| are two linear equations, 2X·(B − A) = |B|² − |A|² and 2X·(C − B) = |C|² − |B|², with B − A and C − B independent, since A, B, C are not on one straight line. So exactly one point is as far from all three, and the centre and D are both that point.
