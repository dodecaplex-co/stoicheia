**"The angles in the alternate segments."** Euclid's key is a diameter. BA, at right angles to the tangent at B, passes through the centre (III.19), so the angle ADB in the semicircle is right (III.31). The right angle ABF and the angles ABD, BAD, which make a right angle in the triangle, share ABD; take it away, and DBF is left equal to BAD. His A is the end of the diameter, but we may take any angle in the segment BAD, for they are all equal (III.21). He gives no citation for drawing BA or for DBF, DBE making two right angles: they are I.11 and I.13.

**A test for touching.** Heath states the converse (vol. 2, p. 66): a straight line through B making with BD angles equal to those in the alternate segments touches the circle. He gives Christopher Clavius' direct proof (p. 67), Euclid's run backwards: add ABD to the equal angles FBD, BAD, and ABF equals ABD and BAD together, a right angle, so EF touches the circle by the porism to III.16. The equal angles test for contact as well as follow from it.

**"If two circles touch."** Pappus, Heath reports, assumes a consequence of III.32: "*If two circles touch, any straight line drawn through the point of contact and terminated by both circles cuts off segments in each which are respectively similar*" (vol. 2, p. 67). Pappus proves it with the common tangent there: the straight line makes equal angles with it in the two circles, so by III.32 the angles in their alternate segments are equal, and the segments are similar (Definition III.11).

**Which side is the circle on?** Euclid's figure has the whole circle, but for B, on one side of EF, and he never says why. The reason is III.18's: a straight line that touches has no point within the circle, so it cannot part two points of it (`Prop32.sameSide`). The figure also puts D on F's side of BA; otherwise we exchange E and F. When BD is itself the diameter, every angle in either segment is right (III.31), as are the angles at B: a case Euclid never separates, for, as Heath remarks under III.33, it too would need III.31.

**The modern way.** On the unit circle |z| = 1, let D = Be^{iθ}, 0 < θ < 2π. Then D − B = 2 sin(θ/2)·iB·e^{iθ/2}, so BD turns through θ/2 from the tangent direction iB: half the counterclockwise arc BD, the angle at any point of the other arc; the opposite tangent ray gives its supplement.
