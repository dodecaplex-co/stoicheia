**Where must the centre lie?** Book III opens by finding the point so many of its proofs will need. Euclid bisects AB at D and draws DC at right angles to it, and the centre must lie on that straight line; bisecting CE then picks it out. Book I's two tools, the bisection and the right angle, are all it takes.

**"Nothing more to base the proof upon."** Why construct F and then argue that no other point G can be the centre? Robert Simson observed that the proof could only be by *reductio ad absurdum*, and Heath explains why: "At the beginning of Book III. we have nothing more to base the proof upon than the definition of a circle, and this cannot be made use of unless we assume some point to be the centre" (vol. 2, p. 8). Our foundations assume that every circle has a centre. This proposition proves that Euclid's construction finds it: bisecting CE gives the centre F. Our statement still supplies a centre G for the reductio.

**"Taken for granted rather than proved."** Isaac Todhunter observes that D is taken to be within the circle, which Euclid proves only in III.2, and Heath that the straight line through D must meet the circle twice, which nothing before it ensures (vol. 2, p. 7). And at the end, "It is however taken for granted rather than proved that the centre must be the middle point of CE" (Heath, vol. 2, p. 8). All three are settled here: `Prop1.within` puts D within the circle by Euclid's own argument for III.2, from a centre supplied by `exists_centre` within that proof, rather than by System E's Circle 2, which contains III.2; the rule System E calls Intersections 5 then finds C and E; and `Prop1.middle` proves that a straight line has one middle point.

**"The greater to the less."** In Euclid's figure the centre is off AB, the angle FDB is right, and GDB is a part of it. But if AB passes through the centre, F falls on D and there is no angle FDB at all. So we set the right angle CDB beside GDB, and `Prop1.perpendicular` says what "the greater to the less" stands for: at D only one straight line is at right angles to DB, wherever G lies. The Porism uses this same argument from its given centre G: if G is on AB, it is D; otherwise the equal adjacent angles put G on DC. It needs no appeal to the rule that a circle has only one centre.

**The modern way.** The points p as far from a as from b satisfy 2p·(b − a) = |b|² − |a|², the perpendicular bisector of AB, and the centre o is one of them. That line meets the circle of radius r at o ± ru, for a unit vector u along it, and their midpoint is o.
