**Where will the centre fall?** Given only a piece of a circle, Euclid finds its centre and completes it. III.1's way is not open: there the straight line at right angles is halved between two points of the circumference, and here DB meets the segment's circumference only at B. So Euclid constructs at A an angle equal to the angle ABD, and which of the angles ABD, BAD is the greater says where the centre falls: beyond AC, at D, or within the segment, as the segment is less than a semicircle, a semicircle, or greater.

**"Will also pass through the remaining points."** Heath explains why Euclid says it: "it is still desirable to state … that 'the circle drawn with centre *E* and distance one of the straight lines *AE, EB, EC will also pass through the remaining points of the segment*' [III. 9], in order to show that part of the circle described actually coincides with the given segment" (vol. 2, p. 56). Lean has no word for a segment: we say it with the circle ABC, the straight line AC in it, and a point to name the side. So our statement is handed the whole circle, and Euclid's E is its centre by III.9.

**"Produce BD, if necessary."** Robert Simson takes the semicircle first and the other two cases together, telling us to "produce *BD*, if necessary", which Heath finds "a little shorter than Euclid's procedure" (vol. 2, p. 56). H. M. Taylor bisects AC at right angles and then AB, and his two straight lines cross at Euclid's E.

**Why must AE meet DB?** Euclid takes the meeting for granted. Lean wants a reason, and in the first case Postulate 5 gives it: the angle ABD is less than a right angle (I.17), so the angles BAE, ABD together are less than two right angles. In the third case AE crosses BD between B and D. Even B needs a word: D is within the circle, so DB meets the circumference. Our III.9 does not ask that E be within, which in the first case nothing yet shows. Euclid gives no citation for the bisection, the right angle, the angle BAE or the equal bases: they are I.10, I.11, I.23 and I.4, and the Lean cites them.

**The modern way.** With D at the origin, A = (−a, 0), C = (a, 0) and B = (0, b), for positive a and b, the centre is E = (0, (b² − a²)/2b) and the radius (a² + b²)/2b. So E lies beyond AC, at D, or on B's side as b is less than, equal to or greater than a: Euclid's three cases.
