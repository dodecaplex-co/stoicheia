**Three places for the centre.** Euclid finds the whole circle from the part we can see. He bisects AC by I.10, draws DB at right angles by I.11, and compares the angles at A and B. Their inequality tells him which way to look along BD. I.23 copies the angle at B to A; I.6 then makes EA equal to EB. I.4 makes EA equal to EC by comparing the triangles on the equal halves AD, DC. Three equal straight lines lead him to III.9.

**The remaining points matter.** Finding a circle through A, B and C must recover the given circumference too. Heath insists: "If the problem is solved after the manner of IV. 5, it is still desirable to state, as Euclid does, after proving AE, EB, EC to be all equal, that 'the circle drawn with centre E and distance one of the straight lines AE, EB, EC *will also pass through the remaining points of the segment*' [III. 9], in order to show that part of the circle described actually coincides with the given segment" (vol. 2, p. 56). We express that identification by proving that E is the centre of the given circle.

**Two cases in one instruction.** Robert Simson takes the semicircle first and joins the other two cases with "produce BD, if necessary", as Heath reports (vol. 2, p. 56). H. M. Taylor instead bisects AB at right angles after bisecting AC at right angles. His intersection is Euclid's E, since EA equals EB. Both constructions find the same point by arranging that it is equally distant from the three ends.

**Why must AE meet DB?** Lean is given the circle; we must construct its centre. It also asks for the crossing Euclid draws. In the first case I.17 makes ABD less than a right angle; the copied angle and ABD together are therefore less than two right angles, so Postulate 5 supplies E. In the third case the copied straight line falls within BAD and crosses BD between its ends. We also justify DB reaching the circumference: D lies within the circle. Our III.9 does not require E to be known within the circle before identifying it as the centre.

**The modern way.** With D at the origin, A = (−a, 0), C = (a, 0), B = (0, b), and a, b positive, the centre is E = (0, (b² − a²)/2b) and the radius (a² + b²)/2b. Thus E lies on the far side of AC, at D, or on B's side as b is less than, equal to or greater than a: Euclid's three cases.
