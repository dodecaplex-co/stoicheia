**The exterior cannot equal the interior.** Euclid draws AC through C to D, then joins CB and DB. The angle ACB is exterior to the triangle DCB, while ADB is interior and opposite to it. Similarity would make them equal; I.16 says the exterior is greater. As Heath observes, his phrase "cannot be constructed" is the one used in I.7 (vol. 2, p. 53). Both impossibility proofs become equalities in our statements: here the two circles must be one.

**Similar before similarity.** Heath reports Augustus De Morgan's objection to Definition III.11: "De Morgan remarks that the use of the word *similar* in 'similar segments' is an anticipation, and that similarity *of form* is meant. He adds that the definition is a theorem, or would be if 'similar' had taken its final meaning" (vol. 2, p. 5). Euclid has not yet developed the theory of similar figures. We take his definition as he gives it: equal angles at one point of each segment. III.21 makes that equality hold at every point of each.

**Which segment lies within?** Heath explains Robert Simson's objection: "this remark is an equally necessary preliminary to III. 23, in order that we may be justified in drawing the segments as being one inside the other" (vol. 2, p. 53). The Lean cites III.10 to exclude a third common point and separates the cases where C lies within or outside the other circle. Our given D may be anywhere on its segment. III.21 carries its angle to Euclid's point on AC produced; when the segments lie the other way round, it carries both given angles to the constructed points. The short contradiction needs this longer account of the figure.

**Coinciding is the conclusion.** Simson replaces "unequal" with "not coinciding with one another" (Heath, vol. 2, p. 53), just what our equality of circles expresses. Christopher Clavius observes that similar segments on opposite sides must also be equal: turn one about the base to put it beside the other. We state the same-side case that Euclid proves; segments on opposite sides may belong to different circles.

**The modern way.** For a straight line AB of length c > 0 and 0 < θ < π, the points on one side that see AB at angle θ fill a single open arc of the circle of radius c/(2 sin θ), whose centre lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB at signed distance (c/2) cot θ toward that side. The angle and the side fix the circle.
