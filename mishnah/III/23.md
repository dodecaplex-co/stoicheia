**The exterior cannot equal the interior.** Euclid draws AC through to D and joins CB, DB. The angle ACB is exterior to the triangle DCB, and ADB is interior and opposite to it; similar segments would make them equal, and I.16 makes the exterior greater. Euclid's "cannot be constructed" is the phrase of I.7, Heath observes (vol. 2, p. 53), and both impossibilities become equalities in our statements: in I.7 the two points are one, and here the two circles are one.

**Similar before similarity.** Heath reports Augustus De Morgan's objection to Definition III.11: "De Morgan remarks that the use of the word *similar* in 'similar segments' is an anticipation, and that similarity *of form* is meant. He adds that the definition is a theorem, or would be if 'similar' had taken its final meaning" (vol. 2, p. 5). Similar figures wait for Book VI. We take the definition as Euclid gives it, equal angles at one point of each segment, and III.21 makes the angle the same at every point of each.

**Which segment lies within?** Euclid draws ACB inside ADB without a word. Robert Simson observes, and Heath agrees, that the remark Euclid makes in III.24, that the circles cannot meet again, "is an equally necessary preliminary to III. 23, in order that we may be justified in drawing the segments as being one inside the other" (vol. 2, p. 53). Our proof begins as Simson's does: the circles meet at A and B, so not at C (III.10), and C is within ADB or outside it. D may lie anywhere on its segment, and III.21 carries its angle to where AC drawn through meets ADB, or, with the segments the other way about, carries both angles. Then I.16 does Euclid's work.

**"Not coinciding with one another."** Simson puts these words for Euclid's "unequal" (Heath, vol. 2, p. 53). Christopher Clavius and the other early editors add that similar segments on opposite sides of one straight line are equal too: turn one about the base until it lies on the other's side. The statement keeps to the case Euclid proves, for on opposite sides the segments may lie in different circles.

**The modern way.** For a straight line AB of length c > 0 and 0 < θ < π, the points on one side of AB that see it under the angle θ fill a single open arc of one circle, whose centre lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB at signed distance (c/2) cot θ toward that side. The angle and the side fix the circle.
