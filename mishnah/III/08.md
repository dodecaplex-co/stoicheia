**The longest way through, the shortest way to.** Euclid draws from D to both sides of the circle: DA reaches farthest, DG stops soonest, and the other straight lines change length as they turn away from DA. We read the concave circumference as the second meeting with the circle, the convex as the first. This gives a meaning to words left undefined, as Heath observes under III.7 (vol. 2, p. 16). Our greatest and least comparisons hold against every point of the circle: I.20 needs no choice of circumference, and on DA itself the part is less than the whole.

**Two things the figure seems to settle.** Heath writes: "As De Morgan points out, there are here two assumptions similar to that tacitly made in the proof of III. 7, namely that *K* falls within the triangle *DLM* and *E* outside the triangle *DFM*." He says these can be proved as in III.7 (vol. 2, p. 20). Augustus De Morgan's question matters twice: the outside placement gives the greater angle for I.24; the inside placement lets I.21 compare the two broken straight lines. Yet, as Heath remarks under Definition III.3 (vol. 2, p. 3), III.7 and III.8 serve no genuine proof later in Book III.

**What the figure settles, proved.** Lean asks us to prove both placements. We do so in `Prop8.outside` and `Prop8.within`: a crossing within a radius is within the circle, and System E's Circle 1 puts it between the two meetings with the circumference, taking the place of Heath's perpendicular. The Lean cites I.23 for Euclid's unbracketed construction of the equal angle DMB. For "only two", Euclid's "which was proved impossible" calls on the comparison along the convex circumference; Lean uses I.24 at M again: different angles give different bases, while equal angles on one side give the same radius. This covers a possible third endpoint on either circumference, even when DK touches the circle.

**The modern way.** With M at the origin, MD = d and radius r, a point P at angle θ from MD has DP² = d² + r² − 2dr cos θ, which grows as θ runs from 0° to 180°: DG is least, DA greatest, and each intermediate length occurs at θ and −θ only, once on each side of DA. This orders the straight lines by the angle at the centre, as in H. M. Taylor's version under III.7, rather than by Euclid's angle at D.
