**Greatest through the centre, least outside it.** From D outside the circle, Euclid draws straight lines through to the far side: DA, through the centre, is the greatest, and the nearer to DA is always greater than the more remote. Their parts outside the circle go the other way: DG, between D and the diameter, is the least, and the nearer to DG is always less. Euclid never defines the concave and the convex circumference, as Heath remarks under III.7 (vol. 2, p. 16). We read the convex one as where a straight line from D first meets the circle, and the concave as where it meets it again.

**The gap of III.7, twice over.** Augustus De Morgan finds it here twice, as Heath reports: "As De Morgan points out, there are here two assumptions similar to that tacitly made in the proof of III. 7, namely that K falls within the triangle DLM and E outside the triangle DFM" (vol. 2, p. 20). The first lets I.21 compare MK, KD with ML, LD; the second gives the greater angle at M for I.24. Heath observes on Definition III.3 that no genuine proof in Book III uses III.7 or III.8, and thinks they are there to answer possible objections (vol. 2, p. 3).

**Within a radius, within the circle.** We prove both placements as in III.7 (`Prop8.outside`, `Prop8.within`): where DE crosses the radius MF, or DK crosses ML, the crossing is within the circle, so System E, Circle 1 puts it between the two points where that straight line meets the circle. Euclid gives no citation for making the angle DMB equal to KMD: it is I.23. For "only two", his "which was proved impossible" looks back to the convex circumference, but a third straight line DN might end on the concave one. So Lean compares the angles at M instead, and by I.24 unequal angles there give unequal straight lines from D.

**The modern way.** With M at the origin, MD = d and radius r, a point P at angle θ from MD has DP² = d² + r² − 2dr cos θ, which grows as θ runs from 0° to 180°: DG is least, DA greatest, and each intermediate length occurs at θ and −θ only, once on each side of DA. This orders the straight lines by the angle at the centre, as in H. M. Taylor's version under III.7, rather than by Euclid's angle at D.
