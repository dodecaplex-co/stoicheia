**Which part of I.26?** Joining EA and EB brings in all we know of a circle's centre, that its radii are equal, and the same two triangles then serve both ways: by I.8 when AF is equal to FB, and by I.26 when the angles at F are right. For the second Euclid chooses the part of I.26 where the equal side subtends one of the equal angles, since the common side EF lies opposite the angles at A and B, which the equal radii make equal.

**Any two give the third.** Heath writes: "This proposition asserts the two *partial* converses (cf. note on I. 6) of the Porism to III. 1" (vol. 2, p. 11), and Augustus De Morgan would place it next to III.1. Three things go together: a straight line in the circle bisected, cut at right angles, and cut by a straight line through the centre. The Porism to III.1 gets the third from the first two; III.3 assumes the third and either of the others, and gets the remaining one. Heath sets out the same pattern under III.18, for a straight line touching the circle.

**One right angle is enough.** For the converse our statement asks only that the angle AFE be right. Euclid, writing "the right angle AFE is equal to the right angle BFE", takes its neighbour to be right as well, and Lean finds that in a tacit rule: a right angle is equal to the angle adjacent to it (`adjacent_eq_of_right`, System E, Diagram-angle 3). We keep Euclid's "not through the centre", and add that F is between A and B, as bisecting a straight line means. Were AB through the centre, it would be bisected at E itself, and there would be no angle AFE to speak of.

**The modern way.** With the centre at the origin, |a| = |b| gives (b − a)·(a + b) = |b|² − |a|² = 0, so the straight line from the centre to the middle point (a + b)/2 is perpendicular to AB. Conversely, the foot a + t(b − a) of the perpendicular from the centre has t = a·(a − b)/|b − a|² = 1/2, since |b − a|² = 2(|a|² − a·b).
