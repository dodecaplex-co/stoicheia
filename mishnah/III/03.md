**One figure, read both ways.** Joining EA and EB brings in all we know of a circle's centre, that its radii are equal. Then the same two triangles serve twice. If AF is equal to FB, the triangles have their three sides equal, so the angles at F are equal, and each is right. If the angles at F are right, the equal radii make the angles at A and B equal too, and the common side EF gives AF equal to FB. Euclid chooses the part of I.26 where the equal side subtends one of the equal angles, since EF lies opposite the angles at A and B.

**Any two give the third.** Heath writes: "This proposition asserts the two *partial* converses (cf. note on I. 6) of the Porism to III. 1" (vol. 2, p. 11), and Augustus De Morgan would place it next to III.1. Three things go together: a straight line in the circle bisected, cut at right angles, and cut by a straight line through the centre. The Porism to III.1 gets the third from the first two; III.3 keeps the third and gets either of the others from the one remaining. Heath sets out the same pattern under III.18, for a straight line touching the circle.

**One right angle is enough.** For the converse our statement asks only that the angle AFE be right. Euclid, writing "the right angle AFE is equal to the right angle BFE", takes its neighbour to be right as well, and Lean finds that in a tacit rule: a right angle is equal to the angle adjacent to it (`adjacent_eq_of_right`, System E, Diagram-angle 3). We keep Euclid's "not through the centre", and add that F is between A and B, as bisecting a straight line means. Were AB through the centre, it would be bisected at E itself, and there would be no angle AFE to speak of.

**The modern way.** With the centre at the origin, |a| = |b| gives (b − a)·(a + b) = |b|² − |a|² = 0, so the line from the centre to the midpoint (a + b)/2 is perpendicular to AB. Conversely, the foot a + t(b − a) of the perpendicular from the centre has t = a·(a − b)/|b − a|² = 1/2, since |b − a|² = 2(|a|² − a·b).
