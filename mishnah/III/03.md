**The centre holds the two triangles together.** Joining EA and EB makes the circle's defining equality available: the two distances from E are equal. We can now use the same figure in both directions. Equal halves give equal angles at F; right angles give equal halves. Euclid chooses EF, the common side, for I.26 in the converse. It lies opposite the equal angles at A and B, so he uses the part of that proposition about a side subtending an equal angle.

**Three facts, any two.** Heath writes: "This proposition asserts the two *partial* converses (cf. note on I. 6) of the Porism to III. 1. De Morgan would place it next to III. 1." (vol. 2, p. 11). Here Augustus De Morgan's suggested order brings three facts together: bisection, right angles and passage through the centre. Our `Prop1.porism` assumes the first two and concludes the third; III.3 keeps the third and exchanges the other two. Heath explains partial converses under I.6, and returns to the pattern under III.18, where the three facts belong to a straight line touching the circle.

**One right angle gives its neighbour.** In the first half, `IsRightAngle` says what Definition I.10 says: equal adjacent angles are right; `Postulate4` makes each of them `∟`, the one size all right angles share. In the converse we assume just AFE right, and System E's Diagram-angle 3 (`adjacent_eq_of_right`) makes it equal to its neighbour BFE. Both steps need F between A and B, which the statement says; later uses in III.14, III.35 and III.36 get this order from `foot_between`. The centre must also lie off AB: otherwise AB would be bisected at E itself, and there would be no angle AFE.

**The modern way.** With the centre at the origin, |a| = |b| gives (b − a) · (a + b) = |b|² − |a|² = 0, so the straight line from the centre to the middle point (a + b)/2 is at right angles to AB; and the foot a + t(b − a) of the perpendicular from the centre has t = a · (a − b)/|b − a|² = 1/2, since |b − a|² = 2(|a|² − a · b).
