**Two equalities from one angle.** Euclid joins the ends of each equal straight line to its circle's centre. Equal circles give equal radii, as in III.26, so the triangles have their three sides equal. Their equal angles at the centres settle the lesser circumferences. The greater ones need no second pair of angles: they are what remains when equal parts are taken from equal wholes. Lean makes each whole circle four right angles (`Circumference.whole`), twice the opposite angles of a quadrilateral in the circle, by III.21 and III.22. We get both equalities by comparing one pair of triangles, then subtracting.

**What if AB passes through K?** Heath writes: "Euclid's proof does not in terms cover the particular case in which the chord in one circle passes through its centre; but indeed this was scarcely worth giving, as the proof can easily be supplied" (vol. 2, p. 60). He supplies it by observing that the other straight line must also be a diameter, since III.15 makes every other straight line in the circle less than its diameter. The four circumferences are then semicircles, two right angles each by I.5 and I.32 (`Circumference.semicircle`). Here greater and less no longer distinguish the two sides. Our statement allows equality, so this case belongs to the proposition too.

**Which side contains the centre?** The drawing puts K above AB and L above DE. Lean needs a reason: a circumference on the centre's side is greater than a semicircle, while the other is less (`Circumference.gt_semicircle`, `Circumference.lt_semicircle`, through I.32 and III.20–III.22). We use `Prop28.beyond` to exclude a centre on the side named as lesser. The remaining possibilities are Euclid's triangles or diameters. For the latter, `Prop28.diameter` goes straight to I.20: a straight line not through the centre is less than the two radii to its ends. This is how III.15 proves the diameter greatest. It also excludes a mixed case, with one diameter and one straight line not through its centre.

**The modern way.** Two points a distance c apart on a circle of radius r cut it into arcs of lengths 2r·arcsin(c/2r) and 2πr − 2r·arcsin(c/2r), which depend only on c and r. So in circles of equal radius, equal straight lines cut off equal arcs, the less equal to the less and the greater to the greater.
