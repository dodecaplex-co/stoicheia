**"The greater equal to the greater."** Euclid joins the ends of each equal straight line to its circle's centre. Equal circles give equal radii, so the triangles AKB, DLE have their three sides equal, and I.8 makes the angles at the centres equal. III.26 then settles the less circumferences, equal in measure and congruent, for we read equal circumferences as both, as there. The greater are what remains when equal parts are taken from equal wholes, the same subtraction that closed III.26. Common Notion 3 gives their equal measures. For congruence, the map of the lesser segments extends to the whole circles and restricts to the greater segments, so one application carries both the removed parts and the remainders.

**What if AB passes through K?** Heath writes: "Euclid's proof does not in terms cover the particular case in which the chord in one circle passes through its centre; but indeed this was scarcely worth giving, as the proof can easily be supplied" (vol. 2, p. 60). He supplies it: the other straight line must then be a diameter too, since every other straight line in a circle is less than its diameter (III.15), and the four circumferences are semicircles. Greater and less no longer tell the two sides apart, but our statement asks of "the greater" only that it be no less, so we count this case in. The angle at the centre of each semicircle is two right angles (`angleAtCentre.semicircle`); the proof obtains congruence from the right angle in each semicircle (`Prop28.right`) and III.24. So the Lean proves the semicircles congruent in the case Euclid's proof does not in terms cover.

Euclid's triangles need K on C's side of AB, and his figure puts it there without a word. The reason is in the circumferences: the one on the centre's side is greater than a semicircle, and the other less, so the centre cannot lie on the side of the less (`Prop28.beyond`). That leaves Euclid's triangles, or diameters. For diameters the Lean cites I.20 where Heath cites III.15: a straight line not through the centre is less than the two radii to its ends, which is how III.15 proves the diameter greatest (`Prop28.diameter`). The same step rules out a diameter in one circle with a straight line that is not one in the other.

**The modern way.** Two points a distance c apart on a circle of radius r cut it into arcs of lengths 2r·arcsin(c/(2r)) and 2πr − 2r·arcsin(c/(2r)), which depend only on c and r. So in circles of equal radius, equal straight lines cut off equal arcs, the less equal to the less and the greater to the greater, and a rigid motion carries each onto its fellow.
