**Two contacts would be too many.** Euclid gives the two kinds of touching different proofs. Within, the straight line of centres forces a comparison of lengths: BH would be both equal to HD and greater than it. Without, the straight line AC would fall within both circles, although circles touching externally have no point within both. The figure holds both impossible suppositions at once. Its inner oval and the thin overlap at AC show what the arguments rule out.

**Which way is the straight line produced?** Heath reports J. G. Camerer's objection: "it is assumed that the supposed second point of contact lies on the line of centres *produced beyond the centre of the 'outer' circle*, whereas all that is proved in III. 11 is that the line of centres *produced beyond the centre of the 'inner' circle* passes through a point of contact" (vol. 2, p. 33). The order BGHD is doing work; merely putting all four points on one straight line would not justify the comparison.

**The remaining direction.** Heath answers: "Hence the only hypothesis left is that the second point of contact may be *on* the line of centres but in the direction of the centre of the '*outer*' circle; and Euclid's proof disposes of this hypothesis" (vol. 2, p. 33). His answer explains why Euclid turns back towards the outer centre after producing the line through the inner one.

**A centre belongs between the ends.** Lean reads Definition III.3 of the whole circle, as discussed under III.11, so III.11 holds at every common point. We use it to put B and D on the straight line of centres, then keep Euclid's comparison of lengths. Since G is within the outer circle, it lies between B and D (`between_of_inside_chord`); H then gives the order BGHD. We draw BD as a diameter, with G at its middle, to show that step explicitly. The same argument serves with the circles' names exchanged. For external contact, Lean takes a point between A and C, which III.2 puts within both circles.

**The modern way.** Two circles with centres g ≠ h and radii r, s touch when |g − h| is r + s or |r − s|; then at a common point x one triangle inequality among g, h, x is an equality, so x lies on the straight line gh, where one point only is r from g and s from h.
