**One figure, two impossibilities.** Euclid gives the two kinds of touching two different proofs. Within, III.11 puts both points of contact on the line of centres, and BH comes out both equal to HD and much greater than it. Without, the straight line AC falls within both circles by III.2, though circles touching externally have no point within both. Our figure, like Heath's, holds both suppositions at once: its oval EBFD and the thin overlap of the circles at AC show what the two arguments rule out.

**Which way is the line of centres produced?** Heath reports J. G. Camerer's objection: "it is assumed that the supposed second point of contact lies on the line of centres *produced beyond the centre of the 'outer' circle*, whereas all that is proved in III. 11 is that the line of centres *produced beyond the centre of the 'inner' circle* passes through a point of contact" (vol. 2, p. 33).

**Back through the outer centre.** Heath answers that a common point off the line of centres would bring a third with it, so that "the only hypothesis left is that the second point of contact may be *on* the line of centres but in the direction of the centre of the '*outer*' circle; and Euclid's proof disposes of this hypothesis" (vol. 2, p. 33). So Euclid, having produced the line of centres through the inner centre to D, turns back through the outer centre to B. The order BGHD carries his comparison; four points merely on one straight line would not.

**Every common point is a point of contact.** That is how Lean reads Definition III.3, of the whole circles (see III.11): circles touching within at D touch within at B as well, and III.11 puts B, like D, on the line of centres. G, the centre of the outer circle, lies between B and D, two points of that circle on a straight line through it (`between_of_inside_chord`), and H then gives the order BGHD; our figure draws BD as a diameter, with G at its middle. One argument serves with the circles' names exchanged. Without, Lean takes a point between A and C, which III.2 puts within both circles.

**The modern way.** Two circles with centres g ≠ h and radii r, s touch when |g − h| is r + s or |r − s|, as in III.11 and III.12; then at a common point x one triangle inequality among g, h, x is an equality, so x lies on the straight line gh, where one point only is r from g and s from h.
