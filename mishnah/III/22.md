**Two corners become one.** Euclid takes the three angles of the triangle ABC, which make two right angles (I.32), and moves two of them to D. CAB is equal to BDC, and ACB to ADB, each pair in one segment (III.21), so the angle ADC is BAC and ACB together. Add ABC to each, and the opposite angles at B and D make two right angles. One of those segments may be less than a semicircle, which is why III.21 must hold in every segment, Robert Simson's case included.

**Can the angles give us the circle?** Heath takes up the converse after Isaac Todhunter: "As Todhunter remarks, the converse of this proposition is true and very important: *if two opposite angles of a quadrilateral be together equal to two right angles, a circle may be circumscribed about the quadrilateral*" (vol. 2, p. 52). Heath would circumscribe a circle about the triangle ABC, by the method of III.9 or by IV.5, and prove by *reductio ad absurdum* that it passes through D. So the angles alone tell when four points lie on one circle.

**"Let AC, BD be joined."** We say the order of the four corners by putting A and C on opposite sides of BD. Euclid's figure also has AC and BD cut one another, so that DB lies within the angle ADC and its two parts add up, and we prove it as in III.21: where AC crosses the straight line BD, the point lies between A and C, so within the circle, and so between B and D.

**The modern way.** In a quadrilateral ABCD in a circle, the angles at B and D stand on the two arcs into which A and C cut the circle, together 360°, and each is half its arc, so ∠ABC + ∠ADC = 180°; conversely, a convex quadrilateral whose opposite angles add to 180° has a circle through its corners. The first half is Heron's proof, which Heath gives under III.20, not Euclid's.
