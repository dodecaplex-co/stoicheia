**Two corners become one.** Euclid takes the angles at A and C of the triangle ABC and finds their equals together at D. The circle lets him move those two angles without changing their sum; the triangle supplies the two right angles. Either of the segments in which he compares angles may be less than a semicircle, so III.21 must cover every segment, including Robert Simson's case discussed by Heath under that proposition. Nothing here requires either of the straight lines AC, BD to pass through the centre.

**Can the angles give us the circle?** Heath takes up the converse after Isaac Todhunter: "As Todhunter remarks, the converse of this proposition is true and very important: *if two opposite angles of a quadrilateral be together equal to two right angles, a circle may be circumscribed about the quadrilateral*" (vol. 2, p. 52). Heath's proof circumscribes a circle about the triangle ABC, by the method of III.9 or by IV.5; a *reductio ad absurdum* then forces D onto that circle. Thus an equality of angles can establish the existence of a circle through four given points.

**The crossing does the quiet work.** We state the order of the four corners by putting A and C on opposite sides of BD. Lean must then show that AC and BD meet between their ends. The crossing lies within the circle because it lies between A and C (System E, Circle 2, `inside_of_between`), and therefore lies between B and D as well (Circle 1, `between_of_inside_chord`). That puts DB inside the angle ADC and justifies adding its two parts. We give Euclid's argument once and use it again with the corners renamed, making his final "Similarly" an application of the same proof.

**Four right angles round the circle.** Lean's `Circumference.whole` uses this result to show that the two circumferences cut off by a straight line together measure four right angles: doubling the two opposite angles gives their sum. The quadrilateral thus supplies the whole against which later propositions compare parts of a circumference.

**The modern way.** In a quadrilateral ABCD inscribed in a circle, the angles at B and D stand on the two arcs into which A and C cut the circle, together 360°; each is half its arc, so ∠ABC + ∠ADC = 180°. Conversely, a convex quadrilateral whose opposite angles add to 180° can be inscribed in a circle.
