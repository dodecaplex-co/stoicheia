**Two halves of one angle.** Euclid compares BAD and BED through a third angle. Each is half the angle BFD at the centre, by III.20, so they are equal. He takes for granted that the halves of equal things are equal, which Lean says once, as `Magnitude.eq_of_add_self_eq`. We can move the vertex from A to E along the circumference of the same segment without changing the angle. Definition III.8 gives the angle in a segment its name; this proposition says that the choice of vertex does not matter.

**Must the segment contain the centre?** Heath identifies the limit of Euclid's argument: "Under the restriction that the 'angle at the centre' used in III. 20 must be less than two right angles, Euclid's proof of this proposition only applies to the case of a segment greater than a semicircle, and the case of a segment equal to or less than a semicircle has to be considered separately. The simplest proof, of many, seems to be that of Simson" (vol. 2, p. 49).

**Robert Simson makes two greater segments.** Draw AF through the centre to C and join CE. Simson's C is now the end of a diameter, whereas Euclid's C merely names the lower circumference in our figure. Heath gives Simson's next step: "Therefore the segment *BADC* is greater than a semicircle, and the angles in it *BAC*, *BEC* are equal, by the first case" (vol. 2, p. 50). The same argument equates CAD and CED. Adding the pairs gives BAD equal to BED. We retain Euclid's proof and use this completion for the remaining segments.

**The crossings must fall between B and D.** Lean asks why AC and EC divide the two angles. Both cross BD within the circle, so each crossing lies between B and D (`between_of_inside_chord`). The straight lines therefore split BAD and BED into the pairs Simson compares. We state III.21 for every segment, including a semicircle: III.22 and the measure of circumferences (`Circumference.eq`) need that full result.

**The modern way.** Complex numbers give a different proof: with B = e^{iβ}, D = e^{iδ} and β < δ < β + 2π, every A = e^{iα} with δ < α < β + 2π makes (D − A)/(B − A) a positive multiple of e^{i(δ−β)/2}, so the angle BAD is (δ − β)/2 wherever A lies on that arc, in Euclid's case (δ − β < π) and in Simson's alike.
