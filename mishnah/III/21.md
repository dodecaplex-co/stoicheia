**Wherever the point is taken.** By III.20 the angle BFD at the centre is double of the angle BAD, and double of BED as well, so BAD and BED are equal, halves of one angle, by the little argument met under I.37. So the angle in a segment is the same wherever its point is taken, and that is what lets Definition III.11 call segments similar by their angles.

**Must the segment contain the centre?** Heath answers that Euclid's proof needs it: "Under the restriction that the 'angle at the centre' used in III. 20 must be less than two right angles, Euclid's proof of this proposition only applies to the case of a segment greater than a semicircle, and the case of a segment equal to or less than a semicircle has to be considered separately. The simplest proof, of many, seems to be that of Simson" (vol. 2, p. 49).

**Robert Simson makes two greater segments.** Draw AF through the centre to C, and join CE. Heath gives Simson's next step: "Therefore the segment BADC is greater than a semicircle, and the angles in it BAC, BEC are equal, by the first case" (vol. 2, p. 50). In the same way CAD is equal to CED, and the two pairs make up BAD and BED. This C is Simson's, the end of a diameter; in Euclid's figure C only names the circle.

**Euclid's case, then Simson's.** Under Euclid's sentences our proof runs his two steps where the centre F is on A's side of BD, the case his figure draws: there the angle BFD is less than two right angles, as his angles at the centre are, and III.20 doubles BAD and BED (`Prop20.special`). The semicircle and the less segment, where F is on BD or across it from A, follow his sentences by Simson's completion, with Euclid's case taken twice (`Prop21.greater`). What Simson reads off his figure, that the segment BADC is greater than a semicircle, we prove: AC and EC cross BD between B and D, which puts A and E on D's side of BC, and F, between A and C, on their side too. Our III.20 also has an angle at the centre of any size (`angleAtCentre`), with which Euclid's two steps would reach every segment; that reading is ours, not Euclid's, so this proof leaves it aside and argues each case as Euclid or Simson does.

**The modern way.** In complex numbers the angle BAD is the size of the argument of (D − A)/(B − A). So III.21 says that for A and E on one circle through B and D, on one side of BD, the cross-ratio ((D − A)/(B − A)) / ((D − E)/(B − E)) is a positive real number; four points lie on one circle or one straight line exactly when it is real.
