**A second circle finds the point of contact.** III.16's porism draws a straight line touching a circle at a point of its circumference. Here the given point A is outside, and the point of contact has to be found. Euclid makes the right angle where it is easy, at D on the straight line EA, and lets the larger circle carry it round: the triangles DEF and BEA are equal, so the angle at B is right too, and the porism makes AB touch.

**Meeting is not yet touching.** Heath points out that Euclid's Greek keeps two verbs apart, ἅπτεσθαι and ἐφάπτεσθαι, "the former meaning 'to *meet*' and the latter 'to *touch*.' The distinction was generally observed by Greek geometers from Euclid onwards" (vol. 2, p. 2). The construction puts it to work. EA meets the given circle at D and passes within it, while AB meets it at B and stays outside.

**"Let the centre E of the circle be taken."** Euclid takes the centre by III.1, which finds it from two points of the circle, and our statement names none. `Prop1.of_circle` takes two points and applies that construction to obtain E. We then join EA and take D where it meets the given circle between E and A, before drawing the larger circle.

**Two straight lines, one length.** Taking F on the other side of EA gives a second straight line touching from A, and Heath reports Heron's observation that the two are "equal in length and equally inclined to the straight line joining the external point to the centre" (vol. 2, p. 44). Euclid's construction needs A outside the circle, and `Prop17` asks for it. A point on the circumference Euclid leaves out, Heath explains, because the construction is "so directly indicated by III. 16, Por. as to be scarcely worth a separate statement" (vol. 2, p. 44); the companion `Prop17.general` takes it too, and there the straight line at right angles to the radius at A touches by that porism, A itself being the point of contact. From a point within, no straight line touches (`Prop17.inside`), so not being within the circle is the condition for a solution.

**The modern way.** With E at the origin, A = (d, 0) and radius 0 < r < d, Euclid's D is (r, 0), his F is (r, √(d² − r²)), and B = (r/d)F = (r²/d, (r/d)√(d² − r²)). Thus B·(B − A) = r² − r² = 0: EB is perpendicular to AB, whose length is √(d² − r²).
