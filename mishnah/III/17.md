**A second circle finds the point of contact.** III.16's porism draws a straight line touching a circle at a point of its circumference. Here the given point A is outside, and the point of contact has to be found. Euclid makes the right angle where it is easy, at D on the straight line EA, and lets the larger circle carry it round: the triangles DEF and BEA are equal, so the angle at B is right too, and the porism makes AB touch.

**Meeting is not yet touching.** Heath points out that Euclid's Greek keeps two verbs apart, ἅπτεσθαι and ἐφάπτεσθαι, "the former meaning 'to *meet*' and the latter 'to *touch*.' The distinction was generally observed by Greek geometers from Euclid onwards" (vol. 2, p. 2). The construction puts it to work. EA meets the given circle at D and passes within it, while AB meets it at B and stays outside. Our `Touches` says just this, in the words of Definition III.2: the straight line meets the circle and, produced either way, does not cut it.

**"Let the centre E of the circle be taken."** Euclid takes the centre by III.1, which finds it from two points of the circle. Our circles carry a centre only by hypothesis, and our statement names no point of the circle at all. So we first find two, where the straight line from that centre to A crosses the circle, once towards A and once beyond the centre; III.1's centre is then the one we began with, for a circle has one centre. The first crossing is Euclid's D. Euclid gives no citation for drawing DF at right angles to EA: it is I.11.

**Two straight lines, one length.** Taking F on the other side of EA gives a second straight line touching from A, and Heath reports Heron's observation that the two are "equal in length and equally inclined to the straight line joining the external point to the centre" (vol. 2, p. 44). Euclid's construction needs A outside the circle, and our statement asks for it. From a point within, no straight line touches; and a point on the circumference Euclid leaves out, Heath explains, because the construction is "so directly indicated by III. 16, Por. as to be scarcely worth a separate statement" (vol. 2, p. 44).

**The modern way.** With E at the origin, A = (d, 0) and radius 0 < r < d, Euclid's D is (r, 0), his F is (r, √(d² − r²)), and B = (r/d)F = (r²/d, (r/d)√(d² − r²)). Thus B·(B − A) = r² − r² = 0: EB is perpendicular to AB, whose length is √(d² − r²).
