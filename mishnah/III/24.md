**Two equal bases, one shape.** Euclid applies the segment AEB to CFD, placing A on C and AB on CD. Equal straight lines bring B onto D; equal angles in the segments must now bring the circumferences together. Similarity here means equality of those angles, the definition discussed by Augustus De Morgan under III.23. We are watching Euclid return to superposition, despite his usual avoidance of it, as Heath remarks under Definition 1.

**Three ways to miss.** Once the bases coincide, the applied segment might fall wholly within the other, wholly outside it, or across it. Heath separates the cases: "The first two hypotheses are disposed of by a *tacit* reference to the preceding proposition III. 23" (vol. 2, p. 54). That proposition has already ruled out two different similar segments on the same side of one straight line, the observation behind Robert Simson's shortened proof reported under III.23.

**"Fall awry."** Heath recognises the word from I.8: "The word implies that the applied figure will partly fall short of, and partly overlap, the figure to which it is applied" (vol. 2, p. 54). The thin curve CGD draws that impossible alternative. It shares C and D with CFD and crosses it again near the top. III.10 forbids three common points of different circles. The curve is deliberately not circular: the contradiction is what we are meant to see.

**Equal radii without moving the segments.** Our Lean proof has no rule for superposing segments, so we prove the radii equal directly. III.20 converts equal angles in the segments into equal angles at the centres; III.22 supplies equal angles in the other segments when the centres lie beyond the bases. Then `Prop24.isosceles` uses I.5 for the base angles, I.32 for the sum of the three angles, and I.26 to match the radii. The centres may also lie on the bases, making both bases diameters. These cases cover the greater segment and the semicircle too. Equality of the circumferences finishes the proof; III.10 is not needed.

**The modern way.** A segment on a chord of length c whose inscribed angle is θ lies in a circle of radius c/(2 sin θ), by the law of sines, and its arc subtends 2π − 2θ at the centre, so equal c and θ give equal radii and equal arcs. This trigonometric argument takes a different route from Euclid's superposition.
