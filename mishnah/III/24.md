**Two equal bases, one shape.** Euclid lays the segment AEB on CFD, A on C and AB along CD. The equal bases bring B onto D, and the equal angles in the segments must then bring the circumferences together. Superposition is a method Euclid uses as little as he can, Heath remarks in his note on Definition III.1, and here he needs it. Our statement says what equal segments come to: the circles AEB, CFD are equal, and so are the circumferences AEB, CFD.

**Three ways to miss.** If the bases fit and the segments do not, the one falls within the other, outside it, or across it. Heath puts a semicolon after "outside it", where the Greek has a comma, so that III.10 answers only the third: "The first two hypotheses are disposed of by a *tacit* reference to the preceding proposition III. 23" (vol. 2, p. 54). Robert Simson's version says no more than that: once AB lies on CD, the segments fit by III.23, as Heath reports under that proposition.

**"Fall awry."** It is the word of I.8, Heath notes: "The word implies that the applied figure will partly fall short of, and partly overlap, the figure to which it is applied" (vol. 2, p. 54). The thin curve CGD draws that impossible case. It meets CFD at C, at D and once more near the top, and a circle does not cut a circle at more points than two (III.10). As in Heath's figure, it is no circle's circumference, for no circle can be drawn so.

**Equal radii without moving the segments.** Lean cannot lay one segment on another, so we compare radii, from the centres the circles carry. The angle in a segment is acute, right or obtuse as the centre lies on its side of the base, on the base, or beyond it (`Prop24.angle_type`), so the equal angles at E and F put both centres alike. III.20 then makes the angles at the centres equal, with III.22 first when the centres lie beyond the bases, and isosceles triangles on equal bases with equal angles at the centres have equal sides (`Prop24.isosceles`, by I.5, I.32 and I.26). When the centres lie on the bases, the bases are diameters and the radii their halves. We never need III.10.

**The modern way.** A segment on a straight line of length c, with the angle θ in it, lies in a circle of radius c/(2 sin θ), by the law of sines, and its arc subtends 2π − 2θ at the centre, so equal c and θ give equal radii and equal arcs. It is not Euclid's route: he lays one segment on the other.
