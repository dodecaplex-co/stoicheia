**Two equal bases, one shape.** Euclid applies the segment AEB to CFD, placing A on C and AB on CD. Equal straight lines bring B onto D; equal angles in the segments must now bring the circumferences together. Similarity here means equality of those angles, the definition discussed by Augustus De Morgan under III.23. Euclid returns here to superposition, a method he avoided wherever he could, as Heath remarks under Definition 1.

**Three ways to miss.** Once the bases coincide, the applied segment might fall wholly within the other, wholly outside it, or across it. Heath separates the cases: "The first two hypotheses are disposed of by a *tacit* reference to the preceding proposition III. 23" (vol. 2, p. 54). That proposition has already ruled out two different similar segments on the same side of one straight line, the observation behind Robert Simson's shortened proof reported under III.23.

**"Fall awry."** It is the word of I.8, Heath notes: "The word implies that the applied figure will partly fall short of, and partly overlap, the figure to which it is applied" (vol. 2, p. 54). The thin curve CGD draws that impossible alternative. It shares C and D with CFD and crosses it again near the top, and III.10 forbids three common points of different circles. As in Heath's figure, the curve is not a circle's, since no circle could be drawn so.

**Equal radii without moving the segments.** Lean cannot apply one segment to another, so our proof compares the radii, from the centres our circles carry by hypothesis. The angle in a segment is less than, equal to or greater than a right angle as the centre lies on its side of the base, on the base, or beyond it (`Prop24.angle_type`), so the equal angles at E and F place both centres alike. On the sides of E and F, III.20 makes the angles at the centres equal; beyond the bases, III.22 first makes the angles in the other segments equal. Then `Prop24.isosceles` takes I.5 for the base angles, I.32 for the three angles together, and I.26 for the radii. On the bases, both bases are diameters. The circumferences follow through `Circumference.eq` and `Circumference.whole`, which bring in III.21. III.10 is not needed.

**The modern way.** A segment on a straight line of length c whose inscribed angle is θ lies in a circle of radius c/(2 sin θ), by the law of sines, and its arc subtends 2π − 2θ at the centre, so equal c and θ give equal radii and equal arcs. This trigonometric argument takes a different route from Euclid's superposition.
