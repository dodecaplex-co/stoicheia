**"If the segment AEB be applied to CFD."** Euclid lays the segment AEB on CFD, A on C and AB along CD. The equal bases bring B onto D, and the equal angles in the segments must then bring the circumferences together. Superposition is a method Euclid uses as little as he can, Heath remarks in his note on Definition III.1, and here he needs it. Our statement says less: the circles AEB, CFD are equal, and the circumferences AEB, CFD are equal in our measure, twice the angle that stands on each. It does not say that one segment can be laid on the other. Since the measure carries no radius, the equal angles alone give the equal circumferences; it is the equal circles that take work.

**Within, outside, or across?** If the bases fit and the segments do not, the one falls within the other, outside it, or across it. Heath puts a semicolon after "outside it", where the Greek has a comma, so that III.10 answers only the third: "The first two hypotheses are disposed of by a *tacit* reference to the preceding proposition III. 23" (vol. 2, p. 54). Robert Simson's version says no more than that: once AB lies on CD, the segments fit by III.23, as Heath reports under that proposition.

**"Fall awry."** It is the word of I.8, Heath notes: "The word implies that the applied figure will partly fall short of, and partly overlap, the figure to which it is applied" (vol. 2, p. 54). The thin curve CGD, Heath's as well, is that impossible case: it meets CFD at C, at D and once more, and a circle does not cut a circle at more points than two (III.10).

**Equal radii without moving the segments.** Our rules lay no segment on another, superposition being kept for I.4 and I.8, so we compare the circles by their radii instead. The angle in a segment tells where the centre lies, so the equal angles at E and F place both centres alike, and III.20 and III.22 then give equal angles at the centres, on equal bases, and so equal radii. Euclid needs III.10 to rule out a segment that falls awry; with the radii equal first, we never do.

**The modern way.** A segment on a straight line of length c, with the angle θ in it, lies in a circle of radius c/(2 sin θ), by the law of sines, and its arc subtends 2π − 2θ at the centre, so equal c and θ give equal radii and equal arcs. It is not Euclid's route: he lays one segment on the other.
