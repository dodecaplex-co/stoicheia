**The square on FE, added and taken away.** When E is the centre, AE, EC, DE and EB are radii, and Euclid calls the equality manifest. Otherwise he adds the square on FE to each rectangle and finds the square on a radius both times; take the square on FE away again, and the rectangles are equal. Everything rests on one fact about the isosceles triangle FAC: the rectangle AE, EC together with the square on FE is equal to the square on FC. Isaac Todhunter remarked that much of the proofs of III.35 and III.36 amounts to this fact, and Heath adds that it follows at once from I.47 with II.5 or II.6 (vol. 2, p. 73).

**Must every case be drawn?** Robert Simson, following Johannes Campanus, adds the cases where one straight line passes through the centre and the other does not; he thought Theon, or some other editor, had left them out as too long. Heath disagrees: "in giving the simplest and most difficult cases only, he seems to be following quite consistently his habit of avoiding *too great* multiplicity of cases, while not ignoring their existence" (vol. 2, p. 73). So Euclid draws the easiest figure and the hardest, and trusts us with the rest.

**What if E is G?** Then AC is cut equally at E, and II.5, which needs an unequal cut, has nothing to give. But the square on EG is then nothing, and the rectangle AE, EC is already the square on GC, so the argument holds all the same. Lean has no figure to choose from, so we take Simson's cases too. Euclid's argument, written for any straight line through E (`Prop35.cut`), covers a straight line through the centre and this case, and serves for DB as Euclid's "For the same reason".

**The modern way.** This is the power of a point: for a circle of centre F and radius r, the power of a point E is FE² − r², negative inside the circle, and AE · EC = DE · EB = r² − FE² is that power with its sign changed.
