**Two rectangles, one remainder.** Euclid first puts E at the centre, where all four parts are equal radii. Away from the centre, his idea is to add the same square to each rectangle and obtain equal squares on radii. The square on FE is the bridge between the two straight lines. We can see the argument already in the isosceles triangle FAC: the rectangle AE, EC together with the square on FE equals the square on FC. Isaac Todhunter singles out this triangle argument as the substance of much of III.35 and III.36, as Heath reports (vol. 2, p. 73).

**Must every case be drawn?** Robert Simson, following Campanus, supplies the cases where one straight line passes through the centre and the other does not. He suspects that Euclid's text has lost them. Heath disagrees: "in giving the simplest and most difficult cases only, he seems to be following quite consistently his habit of avoiding *too great* multiplicity of cases, while not ignoring their existence" (vol. 2, p. 73). The first figure settles the equal radii; the second carries the longer argument.

**What if E is G?** Then AC is cut equally at E, and II.5's unequal cut is unavailable. The square on EG is nothing, and the rectangle is already the square on GC. We include this case in `Prop35.cut`, which repeats the argument for DB and also covers a straight line through the centre. Lean asks for the perpendicular's foot between the ends before III.3 can bisect the straight line there. It also cites III.1 for taking the centre and I.12 for drawing the perpendiculars, constructions Euclid gives without citations here.

**The modern way.** This is the power of a point: for a circle of centre F and radius r, the power of an interior point E is FE² − r², so AE · EC = DE · EB = r² − FE² is its negative.
