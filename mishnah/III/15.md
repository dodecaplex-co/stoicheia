**Move BC beside FG.** Euclid draws a new straight line MN as far from E as BC, so III.14 makes them equal, and at right angles to EK, so it lies beside FG, parallel to it. Two triangles with equal radii for sides now stand at E, and the greater angle MEN gives the greater base by I.24. The same MN settles the diameter's claim: AD is two radii, and two radii together exceed MN by I.20.

**"Euclid himself assumes."** Heath sees the figure at work: "Euclid himself assumes that the angle subtended at the centre by MN is greater than the angle subtended by FG, or, in other words, that M, N both fall outside the triangle FEG" (vol. 2, p. 37), an assumption like those of III.7 and III.8. The reason is ready: "its truth is obvious because EM, EN, being radii of the circle, are greater than the distances from E to the points in which MN cuts EF, EG" (vol. 2, p. 37).

**Why can MN and FG never meet?** Heath's reason takes for granted that MN cuts EF and EG at all, and we prove it from one step back. Were MN and FG to meet, the triangle that point makes with L and K would have two right angles, against I.17; so F and G lie across MN from E. Each point where MN cuts EF or EG is then within the circle, and so between L and the end of MN on its side. `Prop15.half` makes one half of the angle comparison this way, and the two halves together are Euclid's MEN greater than FEG.

**Simson leaves MN out.** Robert Simson's proof compares instead the squares on the perpendiculars and on the halves of the straight lines, after a similar proof by Theodosius (*Sphaerica* I.6), and Simson adds the converse. Heath wonders whether Euclid "would have regarded this as too complicated an inference to make without explanation or without an increase in the number of his axioms" (vol. 2, p. 37). Euclid's MN keeps the comparison in the two triangles before us, but asks us to account for where their points fall.

**The modern way.** A straight line in a circle of radius r that subtends the angle θ at the centre has length 2r sin(θ/2), which grows with θ until θ is two right angles and the straight line is the diameter, 2r. That is Euclid's route: the nearer straight line subtends the greater angle, and I.24 does the rest.
