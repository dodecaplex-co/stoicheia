**Move BC beside FG.** Euclid introduces MN to put the comparison where the eye can follow it. It is as far from E as BC is, so III.14 makes the two equal; but MN runs parallel to FG. We can now compare two triangles with equal radii for their sides. The greater angle at E gives the greater base by I.24. The same new straight line settles the diameter's claim: two radii together exceed MN by I.20. Cutting EL equal to EH uses I.3, and drawing LM at right angles uses I.11, though Euclid gives neither a citation here.

**The greater angle is read from the figure.** Heath writes: "Euclid himself assumes that the angle subtended at the centre by MN is greater than the angle subtended by FG, or, in other words, that M, N both fall outside the triangle FEG" (vol. 2, p. 37). As in III.7 and III.8, the positions need a reason. He supplies it: "its truth is obvious because EM, EN, being radii of the circle, are greater than the distances from E to the points in which MN cuts EF, EG" (vol. 2, p. 37).

**Why can MN and FG never meet?** A meeting would make a triangle with right angles at both L and K, against I.17. This puts F and G across MN from E. We then locate each crossing of MN with EF or EG within the circle and between L and the corresponding end of MN. In Lean, `Prop15.half` proves each half of the angle comparison; adding them gives Euclid's whole. The centre E and the perpendiculars EH, EK are already given in our statement: there is no construction by III.1 or I.12 to repeat.

**Simson leaves MN out.** Robert Simson compares the squares on the perpendiculars and half the straight lines instead. Heath traces that proof to Theodosius, *Sphaerica* I.6, and observes that Simson also adds the converse (vol. 2, p. 37). Euclid's choice keeps the comparison in the two triangles before us, but asks us to justify what their positions show.

**The modern way.** For 0 ≤ d < r, a straight line in a circle of radius r at distance d from the centre has length 2√(r² − d²), which is 2r, the diameter, when d = 0 and less as d grows; that is Simson's route through I.47, not Euclid's.
