**"Equal to a given rectilineal angle."** Euclid copies the given angle at A as BAD, and wants the circle that AD touches at A and that passes through B. Its centre lies on the straight line from A at right angles to AD, and on the one from F, the middle point of AB, at right angles to AB; when the angle is right, the first is AB itself, and the centre is F. Robert Simson treats the acute and obtuse cases as one, and Christopher Clavius draws a semicircle on AB for the right angle, as Heath reports (vol. 2, p. 70).

**Why draw AD in the middle figure?** Heath writes: "A glance at Euclid's figure and proof will however show a more curious fact, namely that he does not, in the proof of the second case, use the *angle in the alternate segment*, as he does in the other two cases. He might have done so after proving that AD touches the circle; this would only have required his point E to be placed on the side of AB opposite to D" (vol. 2, p. 70). His reason, on the same page: III.32 never singles out the case where the alternate segments are semicircles. Our E stays on D's side, as in Euclid's figure.

**Will AE and FG meet?** Heath says: "It is assumed in Euclid's proof of the first and third cases that AE and FG will meet; but of course there is no difficulty in satisfying ourselves of this" (vol. 2, p. 70). Postulate 5 is how we satisfy ourselves: the angle EAF is less than a right angle and the angle AFG is right, so together they are less than two right angles. They meet across AB from D when the angle is acute, and on D's side when it is obtuse, and we prove each.

**The modern way.** Put A at 0 and B at a positive number c. The segment above AB that admits the angle θ has its centre on the perpendicular bisector of AB, at the height ½c·cot θ: above AB for an acute angle, at F, the middle point, for a right one, and below it for an obtuse one.
