**Three figures for one problem.** Euclid must build on a straight line AB a segment of a circle admitting the angle at C. He copies the angle at A as BAD, and wants the circle that AD touches at A and that passes through B. Its centre lies on the straight line from A at right angles to AD, and on the one from F, the middle point of AB, at right angles to AB; when the angle is right, the first is AB itself, and the centre is F. Euclid gives them no citation: they are I.23, I.10 and I.11. Robert Simson treats the acute and obtuse cases as one, and Christopher Clavius draws a semicircle on AB for the right angle, as Heath reports (vol. 2, p. 70).

**Why draw AD in the middle figure?** Euclid proves that AD touches the circle, then never uses it. Heath writes: "A glance at Euclid's figure and proof will however show a more curious fact, namely that he does not, in the proof of the second case, use the *angle in the alternate segment*, as he does in the other two cases. He might have done so after proving that AD touches the circle; this would only have required his point E to be placed on the side of AB opposite to D" (vol. 2, p. 70). His reason, on the same page: III.32 never singles out the case where the alternate segments are semicircles. Our E stays on D's side, as in Euclid's figure.

**Will AE and FG meet?** Heath says: "It is assumed in Euclid's proof of the first and third cases that AE and FG will meet; but of course there is no difficulty in satisfying ourselves of this" (vol. 2, p. 70). Postulate 5 satisfies Lean: the angle EAF is less than a right angle and the angle AFG is right, so together they are less than two right angles. Lean also asks on which side of AB they meet: across AB from D when the angle is acute, on D's side when it is obtuse. Our statement hands back the circle with its centre, for later proofs, and one point of the segment: by III.21 its angles are all equal.

**The modern way.** For an angle θ, with 0 < θ < π, the radius is AB/(2 sin θ), and the centre lies on the perpendicular bisector of AB at a signed distance (AB/2) cot θ from F, towards the segment: on its side for an acute angle, at F for a right one, beyond AB for an obtuse one.
