**Three figures for one problem.** Euclid starts with a straight line and an angle, and builds a circle whose segment admits that angle. In the acute and obtuse cases, the angle copied at A fixes a straight line that will touch the circle, and two perpendiculars locate its centre. Euclid gives no citation for these constructions: I.23 copies the angle BAD, I.10 bisects AB at F, and I.11 draws the two perpendiculars. Admitting an angle is the language of Definition III.11. Robert Simson treats the acute and obtuse cases as one, and Christopher Clavius draws a semicircle on AB for the right angle, as Heath reports (vol. 2, p. 70). We keep Euclid's three cases, because the position of the required segment changes between them.

**Why draw AD in the middle figure?** Euclid proves that AD touches the circle, then finishes without using that fact. Heath writes: "A glance at Euclid's figure and proof will however show a more curious fact, namely that he does not, in the proof of the second case, use the *angle in the alternate segment*, as he does in the other two cases. He might have done so after proving that AD touches the circle; this would only have required his point E to be placed on the side of AB opposite to D" (vol. 2, p. 70). Here E stays on D's side, and III.31 supplies the right angle.

**Will AE and FG meet?** Heath says: "It is assumed in Euclid's proof of the first and third cases that AE and FG will meet; but of course there is no difficulty in satisfying ourselves of this" (vol. 2, p. 70). We prove that meeting with Postulate 5: the angle between AE and AB is less than a right angle, while FG makes a right angle with AB. Lean also needs the side of AB on which they meet. It is opposite D in the acute case and on D's side in the obtuse case, leaving H in the alternate segment.

**The modern way.** For a given angle θ, with 0 < θ < π, the radius is AB/(2 sin θ), and the centre's signed offset from the middle point F along the perpendicular toward the chosen segment is (AB/2) cot θ. Thus the centre is on the segment's side for an acute angle, at F for a right angle, and on the other side for an obtuse angle.
