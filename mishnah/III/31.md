**Why carry BA through to F?** The radii divide the triangle into two isosceles triangles. Their equal base angles make BAC equal to the other two angles of ABC together. We can now see the purpose of F: the exterior angle FAC equals that same pair. BAC and FAC are therefore equal neighbours on a straight line, which is exactly how Definition I.10 recognises right angles. The circle has brought us back to Book I's definition.

**Must A be the middle point?** Heath finds in a passage of Aristotle's *Metaphysics* the trace of an older proof beginning at the middle point of the semicircle's circumference. There the two smaller triangles are themselves right-angled and isosceles. To reach every other point, that argument needs III.21, the equality of angles in the same segment, a theorem "which Euclid's more general proof does not need" (vol. 2, pp. 63–64). Euclid lets A be anywhere on the semicircle.

**An angle in, or an angle of?** The last clauses change the meaning of angle. An angle *in* a segment has two straight arms; the angle *of* it is bounded by its circumference and its straight base. Euclid compares these curved corners with the right angles just found at A. Under Definition III.7, Heath writes: "Euclid abandoned the actual use of the 'angle' in question, but no doubt thought it unnecessary to break with tradition so far as to strike the definition out also" (vol. 2, p. 4). The surviving definition preserves a way of thinking older than this proof.

**Every point of each segment.** Our statement makes the inequalities available at every point of the two circumferences, as Book IV will need them. Lean reaches any point on B's side of AC by pairing it with D on the other side: III.22 says their angles together make two right angles, and the angle at D is already greater than one. We also prove that both BAC and FAC are right. Our angles have two straight arms, so the final claims about angles *of* segments remain Euclid's sentences without Lean proofs beneath them.

**The modern way.** With the centre at the origin, put B at −c, C at c and A at a, where |a| = |c| and a ≠ ±c. Then (−c − a)·(c − a) = |a|² − |c|² = 0, so the two nonzero arms of BAC are perpendicular.
