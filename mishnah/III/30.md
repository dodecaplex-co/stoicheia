**Bisect the straight line first.** Euclid turns a problem about a circumference into two familiar constructions on AB: bisect it at C, then draw CD at right angles to it. The circle's centre need never be found. The two triangles on either side of CD have equal sides about equal right angles, so I.4 gives equal straight lines AD and DB. We can now use III.28 to pass from those straight lines to the circumferences they cut off.

**One circle is enough.** III.28 speaks of equal circles, but here both straight lines belong to the same circle, equal to itself (`EqualCircles.refl`), as Heath's remark under III.29 allows. The construction will matter again in IV.16: there Euclid finds a circumference equal to two fifteenths of the circle and bisects it by III.30. Joining the new division points gives the side needed for his equilateral and equiangular fifteen-angled figure.

**"Less than a semicircle."** Euclid says this of AD and DB without giving a reason (vol. 2, p. 61). We already have what is needed: I.4 gives equal angles at A and B as well as equal straight lines. By I.17 those two angles together are less than two right angles, so each is less than one right angle. Each circumference, measured by twice the angle standing on it (`Circumference.standing`, from III.21), is therefore less than a semicircle. The circumference that remains is greater, the two making up the whole circle (`Circumference.whole`, from III.21 and III.22). That distinction matters: III.28 pairs the less with the less, and equal straight lines alone do not say which circumferences to compare.

**Which meeting point?** Our statement names the side of AB containing the given circumference, and Lean must put D on that side. The Lean cites I.10 for bisecting AB and I.11 for setting up the perpendicular, where Euclid gives no citations. Since C lies between two points of the circle, it is within it. The perpendicular therefore meets the circumference in the required direction. We use System E's Circle 2, which is III.2, and Intersections 5 for these facts, making explicit the meeting that Euclid's figure supplies.

**The modern way.** With the circle as the unit circle in the complex plane, the two square roots of the product of A and B are the midpoints of the two arcs that A and B cut off, and the one on the given arc bisects it.
