**The same squares, taken away twice.** Euclid makes two right-angled triangles with equal radii for their greatest sides. The perpendiculars bisect AB and CD, so equal straight lines give equal halves. Take the squares on those halves away from the equal squares on the radii, and the squares on the perpendiculars remain equal. For the converse he changes what is taken away: equal perpendiculars leave equal halves, and doubling restores the whole straight lines. We can follow both directions in one figure. The construction stays the same; the subtraction changes.

**Must E lie between them?** Heath says Heron has "an elaborate addition to this proposition in which he proves, first by *reductio ad absurdum*, and then directly, that the centre of the circle falls between the two chords" (vol. 2, p. 36). Our proof never asks where E lies relative to the pair. It asks only that each foot falls between the ends of its own straight line. The name ABDC follows the order round the figure, a matter Heath remarks on under III.13 when he discusses Euclid's ABCD here too.

**The feet are already given.** To say that AB and CD are equally distant, Definition III.4 compares EF and EG. We therefore give the centre and both perpendiculars in the statement; there is no new construction by I.12. Euclid still takes the centre by III.1. Lean follows him, then identifies the centre found with E, since a circle has one centre. Before III.3 can bisect AB and CD, Lean also needs F and G between their ends. Neither straight line passes through E, as III.3 requires. These are the facts behind the two right-angled triangles, whichever direction of the argument we take.

**The modern way.** A straight line in a circle of radius r whose perpendicular from the centre has length d has length 2√(r² − d²), and d ↦ 2√(r² − d²) is one-to-one for 0 ≤ d < r.
