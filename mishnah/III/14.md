**The same squares, taken away twice.** Euclid makes two right-angled triangles, each with a radius for its greatest side. The perpendiculars from the centre bisect AB and CD, so equal straight lines have equal halves; take the squares on the halves from the equal squares on the radii, and the squares on the perpendiculars that remain are equal. For the converse he changes what is taken away: equal perpendiculars leave equal halves, and doubling them gives back the whole straight lines. One construction and one figure serve both ways; only the subtraction changes. Each time he passes from equal squares to equal straight lines without a word.

**Must E lie between them?** Heath reports that Heron has "an elaborate addition to this proposition in which he proves, first by *reductio ad absurdum*, and then directly, that the centre of the circle falls between the two chords" (vol. 2, p. 36). Euclid's proof never asks where E lies; it asks only that each foot fall between the ends of its own straight line. Our figure puts E between them all the same. Its circle is ABDC, in the order of the points round it, where Euclid writes ABCD, as Heath notes under III.13.

**"Let it be E."** Definition III.4 measures how far AB and CD stand from the centre by the perpendiculars EF and EG, so our statement names E and the feet F and G before it can say "equally distant", and nothing is left to construct by I.12. Euclid still takes the centre by III.1, and so does Lean: it finds III.1's centre and shows that it is E, since a circle has one centre (`centre_unique`). Then III.3, to bisect AB and CD, wants F between A and B and G between C and D, which Euclid reads off his figure; `foot_between` gives them, for neither straight line passes through E.

**The modern way.** A straight line in a circle of radius r whose perpendicular from the centre has length d has length 2√(r² − d²), and d ↦ 2√(r² − d²) is one-to-one for 0 ≤ d < r.
