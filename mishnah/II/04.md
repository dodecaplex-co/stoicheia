**"Shown in the figures."** Euclid cuts the square on AB into four pieces with the diameter BD, his word for a diagonal, and the parallels CF and HK: two squares, and two complements equal to one another. His HK goes "through G", a point he never introduces: it is where CF crosses BD, and Lean has to find it. Heath sees the whole book in this: "It is important however to bear in mind that the whole procedure of Book II. is *geometrical*; rectangles and squares are shown in the figures, and the equality of certain combinations to other combinations is proved by those figures" (vol. 1, p. 373).

**"I say next that it is also right-angled."** Euclid proves CGKB a square in full, and our proof follows him step by step. I.29 and I.5 make the angles CGB, GBC equal, so I.6 makes CG equal to CB, and I.34 gives the other sides. Then I.29 makes the angles KBC, GCB together two right angles, and Common Notion 3, unnamed in his text, takes away the right angle KBC and leaves BCG right. The proof attributed to Theon stops at the right angle at B, "a legitimate abbreviation of the genuine proof", Heath says (vol. 1, p. 381), since I.46 has already shown that when one angle of such a figure is right, all are.

**"For the same reason."** With these words Euclid makes HF a square too, without repeating the argument. The Greek text also has a Porism that says it in general, which Heath calls "undoubtedly interpolated": "From this it is manifest that in square areas the parallelograms about the diameter are squares" (vol. 1, p. 381). J. L. Heiberg suspected Theon, and a papyrus fragment lacks it. Euclid's reason does hold for any square, so we prove it once, as `Square.about_diameter`, and use it here for HF and again in II.5 to II.8.

**"Twice the rectangle."** Euclid gives no citation for "AG is equal to GE": it is I.43, on the complements, and the Lean cites it. Each complement is the rectangle AC, CB, so the two are twice it, and Heath notes: "By a curious idiom this is in Greek 'the rectangle *twice contained* by the segments'" (vol. 1, p. 380). The semi-algebraical proof, from II.2 and then II.3 twice, Heath finds in Christopher Clavius and most later editions (vol. 1, p. 381).

**The modern way.** With AC = a and CB = b, II.4 is (a + b)² = a² + b² + 2ab. For vectors, |u + v|² = |u|² + |v|² + 2u·v, and AC and CB point the same way, so for them u·v is ab.
