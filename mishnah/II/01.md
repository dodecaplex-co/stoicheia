**Contained by two straight lines.** Euclid names a rectangle, like an angle, by the two straight lines that contain it, and Heath explains how the Greek shortens both: "In both cases the substantive and participle can be omitted because the feminine or neuter of the article enables us to distinguish whether an angle or a rectangle is meant" (vol. 1, p. 370). Our `rect` is that rectangle, taken from the lengths of its sides. That a rectangle drawn is the rectangle contained by its sides Euclid takes for granted; Lean proves it once, from I.4 and I.34, and `Rectangle.eq_rect` cites it.

**Making room for the cut.** I.11 gives BF no length, and I.3 cuts off only from the greater of two straight lines. So Lean first sets out a straight line equal to A at B, by I.2, which Euclid does not cite, and `produce_longer` reaches along BF further than that. Heath's figure puts F beyond G; Lean needs only G on the ray from B through F. `exists_off_line` chooses the side of BC that BF stands on, with the two circles of I.1 (Postulates 1 and 3, Definition 15; System E, Intersection 5).

**Where the parallels meet.** Euclid names K, L, H, where DK, EL, CH meet GH, without asking whether they do. They do: a straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other, from I.30, as Heath reports Proclus drawing it under I.31 (`BookI.Prop31.parallelogram`). I.30 also makes the three parallel to one another (`BookI.Prop30.parallel`). Parallels keep order, so G, K, L, H lie on GH as B, D, E, C lie on BC (`Parallel.between`), and Book Zero's `quadArea_add_of_parallelograms` makes BK, DL, EH the whole BH.

**The three rectangles.** One right angle makes a parallelogram a rectangle (`Rectangle.of_right`, by I.29). BH and BK have the right angle at B, and DL and EH the angles at D and E, right because they lie beside right angles (`adjacent_eq_of_right`). DK is equal to BG by I.34, and EL likewise, so each piece is the rectangle contained by A and its part of BC. Euclid's enunciation cuts BC into "any number of segments whatever", his setting-out and our statement at D and E. He never cites II.1, as Heath remarks under II.2, though Heron, as Heath reports from an-Nairīzī, drew II.2–II.10 from it and held that II.1 itself cannot be proved without drawing its rectangles.

**The modern way.** Write a for A and b, c, d for BD, DE, EC: II.1 is the distributive law, a(b + c + d) = ab + ac + ad. For a sum times a sum the figure grows both ways, Heath notes: "a series of parallels to BC would have to be drawn as well as the series of parallels to BF" (vol. 1, p. 376).
