**The acute angles.** Euclid calls ABC acute-angled, then specifies that the angle at B is acute. Heath remarks that "the last words have no point if all the angles of the triangle are necessarily acute" (vol. 1, p. 407). Our statement asks for acute angles at B and C, leaving A unrestricted. These are exactly the conditions that put D within BC. If C were right, D would be C and the result would be I.47; if C were obtuse, D would fall beyond C and II.7 would no longer apply to this cut.

**The foot within.** Postulate 1 names the straight line BC. D cannot coincide with B or C, since the acute angle there would then be right. The helper `Prop13.not_beyond` excludes a foot beyond either end: I.13 makes the angle beside the acute angle obtuse, and I.17 forbids the resulting triangle to have both an obtuse and a right angle. Applied at B and again at C, this supplies what Euclid reads from his figure. Lean's `between_cases` then places D between B and C.

**Add the same square.** II.7 compares the squares on CB, BD with twice the rectangle contained by CB, BD and the square on DC. `CommonNotion2` adds the square on DA to each. I.47 replaces the squares on BD, DA by the square on AB, and the squares on AD, DC by the square on AC. Its second use has no bracket in Heath. Rearranging these sums completes Euclid's argument, an instance of the geometrical algebra discussed under II.4.

**The squares named.** `BookI.Prop47.sq` supplies I.47 for squares named by their sides. It describes the squares by I.46; `Square.described` makes them parallelograms by I.28 and identifies each as the square on its side. That identification rests on `Rectangle.contained`: rectangles contained by equal straight lines are equal, from I.4 and I.34. These shared steps explain the certificate's additional I.4, I.28, I.34 and I.46; I.13 and I.17 enter when we place the foot. The certificate also records Postulate 2 directly: `BookI.Prop28.square` produces straight lines to apply I.28. I.4 also brings in Postulate 1; I.13, I.17, I.28, I.34, I.46, I.47 and II.7 bring in both postulates.

**The modern way.** Put B = (0, 0), C = (a, 0), A = (d, h), and D = (d, 0), with 0 < d < a. Then AC² = (a − d)² + h² = a² + (d² + h²) − 2ad = CB² + BA² − 2·CB·BD, the law of cosines since BD = AB cos B. The identity in a, d, h holds wherever D falls, with d a signed coordinate; Euclid's use of II.7 needs D within CB.
