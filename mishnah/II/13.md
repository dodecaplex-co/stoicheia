**"Having the angle at B acute."** II.13 is II.12's partner. At an acute angle the square on the opposite side falls short of the squares on the other two sides, by twice the rectangle contained by CB and BD, the piece the perpendicular from A cuts off within. The enunciation speaks plainly of acute-angled triangles. Yet the setting-out adds that the angle at B is acute, and Heath remarks that "the last words have no point if all the angles of the triangle are necessarily acute" (vol. 1, p. 407).

**"One at least, if not all."** Readers saw early that the relation holds at an acute angle of any triangle, acute-angled, right-angled or obtuse-angled. Heath names Isaacus Monachus, Campanus, Christopher Clavius and others, and a Greek scholiast who explains Euclid's word away: Euclid "calls all triangles acute-angled because all have an acute angle, one at least, if not all" (vol. 1, p. 407). Heron's converse of this proposition speaks of any triangle, and Heath judges that Heron too would have left the word out. He closes on the pair: "It may be noted, lastly, that II. 12, 13 are supplementary to I. 47 and complete the theory of the relations between the squares on the sides of *any* triangle, whether right-angled or not" (vol. 1, p. 409).

**"Cut off within."** We keep Euclid's figure: our statement asks for acute angles at B and C, and nothing at A. Were the angle at C right, D would fall on C and the theorem would be I.47; were it obtuse, D would fall beyond C, and CB would not be cut at D as II.7 asks. Euclid reads D's place off his figure; we argue for it as in II.12. D is not B or C, for the acute angle there would be right. Nor does it fall beyond either end, for then the angle beside the acute one would be obtuse (I.13), and no triangle has an obtuse angle and a right angle (I.17). So the Lean cites both (`Prop13.not_beyond`).

**The modern way.** Put B = (0, 0), C = (a, 0) and A = (d, h), with d > 0 and h ≠ 0, so that D = (d, 0) and the angle at B is acute. Then AC² = (a − d)² + h² = a² + (d² + h²) − 2ad = CB² + BA² − 2·CB·BD, whether D falls within CB or not, as the scholiast reads the theorem; with II.12 this is the law of cosines, since BD = AB cos B.
