**"Having the angle at B acute."** Euclid's triangle is acute-angled, and yet he adds that the angle at B is acute; Heath remarks that "the last words have no point if all the angles of the triangle are necessarily acute" (vol. 1, p. 407). Our statement asks for acute angles at B and C, and nothing at A. Euclid's route needs the one at C: were it right, D would fall on C and the theorem would be I.47; were it obtuse, D would fall beyond C, and CB would not be cut at D as II.7 asks.

**The foot within.** Euclid reads D's place off his figure; Lean needs a reason. D is not B or C, for the acute angle there would be right. Nor does it fall beyond either end: `Prop13.not_beyond` shows that the angle beside the acute one would be obtuse (I.13), and a triangle with an obtuse angle and a right angle is what I.17 forbids. Used at B and at C, it leaves D within BC, since of three points on a straight line one lies between the other two (`between_cases`). So I.13 and I.17 are cited, though Euclid brackets neither.

**Adding the square on DA.** II.7 makes the squares on CB, BD equal to twice the rectangle CB, BD and the square on DC, and Common Notion 2 adds the square on DA to each. Then I.47 puts the square on AB for the squares on BD, DA, and the square on AC for those on AD, DC; Euclid brackets the first use and not the second. All that is left is to gather the sums, the geometrical algebra Heath describes under II.4.

**The squares named.** Lean cites I.47 as `BookI.Prop47.sq`, which describes the squares (I.46) and names each by its side through `Square.described`: a parallelogram by I.28, and the square on its side because rectangles with equal sides are equal (`Rectangle.contained`, from I.4 and I.34). Of the postulates, the certificate records 1 and 2 directly, the second for a straight line produced inside `BookI.Prop28.square`; the propositions cited bring in Postulates 3 and 4 and Definition 15 as well, and Postulate 5 comes in through I.34, I.46 and I.47.

**The modern way.** Put B = (0, 0), C = (a, 0) and A = (d, h), with d > 0 and h ≠ 0, so that D = (d, 0) and the angle at B is acute. Then AC² = (a − d)² + h² = a² + (d² + h²) − 2ad = CB² + BA² − 2·CB·BD, whether D falls within CB or not: the law of cosines, since BD = AB cos B.
