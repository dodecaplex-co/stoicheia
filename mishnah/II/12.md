**Where the perpendicular falls.** Euclid draws BD perpendicular to CA produced, and his figure puts D beyond A. Our statement gives the perpendicular, and Lean has to find its foot. It is not A, or the angle BAC would be right. Nor is it on the ray from A through C, or the triangle ABD would have an obtuse angle and a right angle, which I.17 forbids (`BookI.Prop17`). So C, A, D lie in that order, and I.17 is cited though Euclid gives no bracket for it.

**Adding the square on DB.** II.4 makes the square on DC equal to the squares on CA, AD and twice the rectangle CA, AD, and Common Notion 2 adds the square on DB to each. Then I.47, used twice, puts the square on CB for the squares on CD, DB, and the square on AB for those on AD, DB. Lean cites I.47 as `BookI.Prop47.sq`, which describes the squares (I.46) and names each by its side through `Square.described` (I.4, I.28, I.34). Of the postulates, the certificate records 1 and 2 directly, the second for a straight line produced inside `BookI.Prop28.square`; I.46 and I.47 bring in Postulates 3, 4 and 5 and Definition 15 as well.

**Two lines of proof.** Heath says that "only two lines of proof are possible, (1) by means of the results of certain earlier propositions in Book II. combined with the result of I. 47 and (2) by means of the procedure in Euclid's proof of I. 47 itself" (vol. 1, p. 404). Euclid takes the first, the geometrical algebra Heath describes under II.4. Heath gives the second as well, and finds it in Gregory of St. Vincent's work of 1647.

**Heron's converse.** Heath reports from an-Nairīzī a converse by Heron, related to II.12 as I.48 is to I.47: "In any triangle, if the square on one of the sides is greater than the squares on the other two sides, the angle contained by the latter is obtuse" (vol. 1, p. 405). Heron sets beside the triangle a right-angled one with the same two sides about the angle. By I.47 its base is the less, and by I.25 the angle BAC is greater than its right angle.

**The modern way.** Put A = (0, 0), C = (b, 0) and B = (−d, h), with b, d > 0 and h ≠ 0, so that D = (−d, 0). Then BC² = (b + d)² + h² = b² + (d² + h²) + 2bd = AC² + AB² + 2·CA·AD, the law of cosines, since AD = −AB cos A.
