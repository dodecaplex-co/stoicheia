**What if the angle is not right?** I.47 settles the right-angled triangle, and II.12 and II.13 take the other two kinds. When the angle BAC is obtuse, the square on BC is more than the squares on BA, AC, and Euclid says exactly how much more: twice the rectangle contained by CA and AD, the piece the perpendicular from B cuts off outside the triangle. Our figure at the top draws the excess as two black rectangles beside the square on CA.

**"Perpendicular to CA produced."** Euclid's figure puts D beyond A. Our statement gives the perpendicular, and Lean has to find its foot. It is not A, or the angle BAC would be right. Nor is it on the ray from A through C, or the triangle ABD would have an obtuse angle and a right angle, which I.17 forbids. So C, A, D lie in that order, and the Lean cites I.17 where Euclid gives no citation.

**"Alone worth giving."** Book II's pictures of areas do not help with squares on a triangle's sides, so, Heath writes, "only two lines of proof are possible, (1) by means of the *results* of certain earlier propositions in Book II. combined with the *result* of I. 47 and (2) by means of the procedure in Euclid's proof of I. 47 itself" (vol. 1, p. 404). Euclid takes the first, and we follow him. As alternatives, proofs of the second kind are "alone worth giving"; Heath traces them past Dionysius Lardner's Euclid of 1828 to Gregory of St. Vincent's work of 1647. His own proof of II.12 shows two rectangles equal on the way, "a particular case of the theorem in III. 35" (vol. 1, p. 405).

**Heron turns it round.** Heath reports from an-Nairīzī a converse by Heron, related to II.12 as I.48 is to I.47: "*In any triangle, if the square on one of the sides is greater than the squares on the other two sides, the angle contained by the latter is obtuse*" (vol. 1, p. 405). Heron sets beside the triangle a right-angled one with the same two sides about the angle. By I.47 its base is the less, and by I.25 the angle BAC is greater than its right angle.

**The modern way.** Put A = (0, 0), C = (b, 0) and B = (−d, h), with b, d > 0 and h ≠ 0, so that D = (−d, 0). Then BC² = (b + d)² + h² = b² + (d² + h²) + 2bd = AC² + AB² + 2·CA·AD, the law of cosines, since AD = −AB cos A.
