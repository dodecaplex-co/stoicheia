**Where the perpendicular falls.** Euclid places D on CA produced beyond A. Our statement gives the perpendicular and asks Lean to prove where its foot falls. D cannot be A, since the angle BAC would then be right. If D lay on the ray from A through C, the triangle ABD would have an obtuse angle at A and a right angle at D, contrary to `BookI.Prop17`. The order of the three points is therefore C, A, D. That is where I.17, which Euclid does not cite, comes in.

**Adding the square on DB.** II.4 gives the square on DC from the squares on CA, AD and twice the rectangle contained by CA, AD. Common Notion 2 adds the square on DB to each side; I.47 then identifies the squares on CB and AB. The shared step `BookI.Prop47.sq` describes squares by I.46, and `Square.described` makes them parallelograms by I.28 and the squares on their sides by I.4 and I.34. These explain the extra citations. The certificate records Postulates 1 and 2 directly: 1 names CA, and 2 enters through the straight lines produced inside `BookI.Prop28.square`. Through the cited I.46 and I.47 it also rests on Postulates 1–5 and Definition 15.

**Two lines of proof.** Heath says that "only two lines of proof are possible, (1) by means of the results of certain earlier propositions in Book II. combined with the result of I. 47 and (2) by means of the procedure in Euclid's proof of I. 47 itself" (vol. 1, p. 404). Euclid takes the first route here, using the geometrical algebra discussed under II.4. Heath traces the proof after the manner of I.47 to Gregory of St Vincent's work of 1647 (vol. 1, p. 404).

**Heron's converse.** Heath reports it from an-Nairīzī: "In any triangle, if the square on one of the sides is greater than the squares on the other two sides, the angle contained by the latter is obtuse" (vol. 1, p. 405). Heron compares the triangle with a right-angled triangle having the same two sides. I.47 compares the squares on their remaining sides, and I.25 makes the greater base subtend the greater angle.

**The modern way.** Put A = (0, 0), C = (b, 0) and B = (−d, h), with b, d > 0 and h ≠ 0, so D = (−d, 0). Then BC² = (b + d)² + h² = b² + (d² + h²) + 2bd = AC² + AB² + 2·CA·AD. This is the law of cosines, with AD = −AB cos A.
