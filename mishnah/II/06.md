**What if D lies beyond B?** Euclid adds BD to the bisected straight line, and II.5's figure now holds a rectangle that runs past its line. In Heath's words the rectangle AD, DB is "a rectangle applied to a given straight line (AB) but *exceeding* by a square (the side of which is equal to BD)" (vol. 1, p. 386): Proclus' exceeding, where II.5 gave his falling-short. The proof keeps step with II.5's. AL is equal to HF, CM is added to each, and the gnomon NOP is again three parallelograms, here CH, BM and HF. Our statement, like Euclid's setting-out, puts D beyond B, so we need only his one figure.

**Hippocrates' lunes.** Heath sees a problem here too: to apply to a given straight line a rectangle equal to a given square and exceeding by a square figure, the equation ax + x² = b² (vol. 1, p. 386). He finds a problem of this kind already presupposed in Hippocrates' *Quadrature of lunes*, in the fifth century B.C. (vol. 1, pp. 386–387): a straight line of given length must be fitted between a straight line and a circumference so as to point, produced, through a given point. Hippocrates could have solved it by the construction below, Heath says, but H. G. Zeuthen thinks it probable that here he simply slid the line along until it did (vol. 1, p. 387).

**Robert Simson's circle.** Heath gives Simson's construction, II.6 and I.47 working together. At B draw a straight line at right angles to AB and equal to b, and with centre C describe the circle through its far end, cutting AB produced at D. The rectangle AD, DB with the square on CB is the square on CD, by II.6; CD is a radius, so that square is the squares on CB and on b, by I.47. Take away the square on CB, and the rectangle AD, DB is the square on b: BD is the x sought. The other root is negative, and Heath writes, "From Euclid's point of view there would only be one solution in this case" (vol. 1, p. 388).

**The modern way.** Put C at 0, A at −h, B at h and D at d, with d > h > 0: then (d + h)(d − h) + h² = d². It is II.5's identity with CB and CD exchanged, as Heath shows under II.5.
