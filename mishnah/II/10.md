**"If produced in the direction B, D, will meet."** To find G, Euclid cites Postulate 5 itself, with its book: "[I. Post. 5]". It is the one postulate Book II cites, and his third citation of it, after I.29 and I.44. One step goes unsaid: EB falls within the angle CEF, so FEB is less than CEF, the part being less than the whole (Common Notion 5), and with EFD it makes less than the two right angles of I.29.

**Which way do they meet?** The postulate promises a meeting on the side where the angles are less than two right angles. Lean asks what that means here, and we answer from Book Zero: B lies between E and G, and D between F and G. The rest runs as in II.9, with no square drawn and the same halves of right angles. I.32 speaks of a side produced, so in the triangle EFG we produce FG beyond G, to a point Heath's figure does not name.

**A ladder to the square root of two.** H. G. Zeuthen saw here a proof of the Greek rule for side and diagonal numbers. Heath tells it from Theon of Smyrna: "The unit, being the beginning of all things, must be potentially both a *side* and a *diameter*" (vol. 1, p. 398), that is, a diagonal. From a side a and a diagonal d come the side a + d and the diagonal 2a + d: 1, 1 gives 2, 3, then 5, 7 and 12, 17. Each diagonal's square is one more or one less than twice its side's, and d/a comes ever nearer to √2. Plato's "rational diameter" of five is 7, whose square falls one short of twice 25 (vol. 1, p. 399).

**"Proved graphically."** In passages of his commentary on Plato's *Republic* first printed in 1901, Proclus says the property of these numbers "is proved graphically … in the second book of the Elements by '*him*'", and shows how II.10 proves it (vol. 1, pp. 399–400). He does not mean that it was proved there first, Heath says: the Pythagoreans had proved it, and this confirms "that the whole of the substance of that Book was Pythagorean" (vol. 1, p. 400), as the part the gnomon and I.47 play in Book II had suggested.

**The modern way.** Let AC = CB = a and BD = b; then (2a + b)² + b² = 2a² + 2(a + b)². With c = CD = a + b, this is II.9's identity (c + a)² + (c − a)² = 2a² + 2c²: the two propositions prove one result.
