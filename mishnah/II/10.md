**"If produced in the direction B, D, will meet."** The angles CEF, EFD make two right angles (I.29); EB falls within CEF, so FEB, EFD are less than two right angles, the part being less than the whole (Common Notion 5); and Postulate 5 gives the meeting G. It is the one postulate Book II brackets, printed with its book, "[I. Post. 5]", and I.29, I.30, I.32, I.34, I.46 and I.47 rest on it too. Lean then proves that B lies between E and G, and D between F and G, with Book Zero's `Between.of_inside` and `Between.of_sameSide`.

**What the figure asks us to say.** I.11 needs a side of AB to draw CE on, and `exists_off_line` gives one; I.3 needs a greater straight line to cut from, and `produce_longer` draws the perpendicular out far enough. Half a right angle is an angle which, added to itself, makes a right angle. I.5 and I.32 give such halves at A, E and B, Common Notion 3 taking the right angle at C from I.32's two, and I.15 carries the half at B across to DBG. I.29, I.34, I.32 and I.6 then make BD equal to GD and GF equal to EF. For I.32 in the triangle EFG we produce FG beyond G, to a point Heath leaves without a letter.

**The shared steps.** `BookI.Prop31.parallelogram` finds F where the parallel through D meets EF, and brings in I.30, by the argument Heath reports from Proclus under I.31. `BookI.Prop47.sq`, used four times, describes its squares by I.46, makes them parallelograms by I.28, and names each the square on its side through I.4 and I.34. As in II.9, no square is drawn, a change Heath notes there.

**Side and diagonal numbers.** Heath tells, after Zeuthen, how II.9 and II.10 prove the Pythagorean rule for side and diagonal numbers: side a and diagonal d give side a + d and diagonal 2a + d, so 1, 1 gives 2, 3, then 5, 7 and 12, 17, and d/a comes ever nearer to √2. Proclus says the property is proved in the second book of the Elements, and Heath takes it as confirming "that the whole of the substance of that Book was Pythagorean" (vol. 1, p. 400), as the part the gnomon and I.47 play there suggested.

**The modern way.** Let AC = CB = a and BD = b; then (2a + b)² + b² = 2a² + 2(a + b)². With c = CD = a + b, this is II.9's identity (c + a)² + (c − a)² = 2a² + 2c²: the two propositions prove one result, as Heath notes under II.9.
