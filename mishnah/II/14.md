**"Without the use of proportions."** I.45 turns any rectilineal figure into a parallelogram, and Euclid now turns a rectangle into a square, which, in Heath's words, "completes the theory of *transformation of areas* so far as it can be carried without the use of proportions" (vol. 1, p. 410). Our main theorem takes the figure of Euclid's I.45, two triangles sharing a side, with corners A₁ to A₄. Its companions take any figure given with its triangles, checked as in I.45, and draw a square equal to them (`Prop14.square`); that another cutting gives the same whole is I.45's tacit step, proved by David Hilbert for his measure of area.

**"The finding of the mean."** J. L. Heiberg points to Aristotle's remark that squaring "is better defined as the 'finding of the mean (proportional)' than as 'the making of an equilateral rectangle equal to a given oblong,' because the former definition states the *cause*, the latter the *conclusion* only" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 410). So Aristotle's text-books, Heiberg thinks, squared by proportions. Heath adds that the construction itself is that of VI.13, which finds a mean proportional; Euclid, he judges, changed only the proof, resting it on Books I and II.

**"Let BE be greater."** Robert Simson pointed out that the words are not needed: the construction works either way. Heath grants it, but "perhaps Euclid may have regarded it as conducive to clearness to have the points B, G, E, F in the same relative positions as the corresponding points … in the figure of II. 5 which he quotes in the proof" (vol. 1, p. 410). So we keep the words, and Simson is right too: one argument serves either way, and G, where BF is bisected, falls between B and E, as in II.5.

**A whole circle for a semicircle.** Where Euclid draws the semicircle BHF, Postulate 3 gives us the whole circle about G. Must DE produced meet it? E lies between the centre G and F, so it is inside the circle, and a straight line through a point inside cuts the circle whichever way it is produced (`line_circle_beyond`). We take the meeting beyond E, away from the rectangle, and the angle HEG is right, beside the rectangle's right angle DEB. In the end the rectangle BD, the rectangle BE, EF and the square on EH are one area under three names, and Euclid draws BD alone ([Book II's introduction](index.md#areas)).

**The modern way.** With BE = a and ED = EF = b, GH = (a + b)/2 and GE = (a − b)/2, so EH² = ((a + b)/2)² − ((a − b)/2)² = ab. Thus EH is √(ab), the mean proportional between BE and ED, and Heath calls Euclid's solution "the equivalent of the extraction of the square root, or of the solution of the pure quadratic equation" x² = ab (vol. 1, p. 410).
