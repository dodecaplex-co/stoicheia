**"Let the figure be drawn."** Here, for the first time, Euclid leaves the drawing to us. After the square ADEB, five words stand for the diameter BD, the parallel through C, the parallel through G where it crosses BD, and the points where they meet: II.4's figure, taken as known. Heath's figure also marks H and N, which his proof never names; our figure keeps them, and the Lean names them too, for `Parallelogram.figure`, the step that carries Euclid's five words, hands back every point of that figure at once.

**The square counted twice.** Euclid adds CF to the equal complements AG and GE, so that AF is equal to CE. The two overlap in CF: together they are the gnomon KLM and CF once more. Since BF is equal to BC, AF is the rectangle AB, BC, and the gnomon with CF is twice that rectangle. DG, the square on AC, fills out the square ADEB, and CF is left beside it: that is why the enunciation speaks of the square on the whole "and that on one of the segments both together". Euclid gives the small square two names, CF and BG. As in II.5, the gnomon's letters name an area, and our Lean writes the sum of AG, CF and GE.

**A difference in place of a sum.** Heath reads AC as the difference of AB and BC, so that "*the square on the difference of two straight lines is equal to the sum of the squares on the straight lines diminished by twice the rectangle contained by them*" (vol. 1, p. 389). II.4 gives the square on the sum of two straight lines, II.7 the square on their difference. Added and subtracted, he notes, the two identities give II.9 and II.10, and II.8, and so "a suggestion of alternative methods of proving those propositions" (vol. 1, p. 389). Euclid needs II.7 himself in II.13, for the pieces a perpendicular makes of a triangle's side.

**The modern way.** With AB = a and BC = b, so that AC = a − b, II.7 says a² + b² = 2ab + (a − b)², which Heath rearranges as (a − b)² = a² + b² − 2ab.
