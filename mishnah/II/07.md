**The square counted twice.** Euclid adds CF to the equal complements AG and GE, so that AF is equal to CE. The two overlap in CF: together they are the gnomon KLM and CF once more. Since BF is equal to BC, AF is the rectangle AB, BC, so the gnomon and CF make twice that rectangle; and DG, the square on AC, fills out the square ADEB, with CF beside it. As in II.5, Lean writes the gnomon as the sum of its parallelograms AG, CF and GE, and K, L and M name an area, not points.

**"Let the figure be drawn."** Euclid's five words hide two parallels and the points where they meet. `Parallelogram.figure` draws them by I.31 and finds the meetings by I.30, that a straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other, as Heath reports Proclus arguing under I.31; neither has a bracket here. Heath's figure also marks H and N, though his proof never names them, and so does ours. Before I.46 a point off AB, with no letter, chooses the side for the square (`exists_off_line`, with Postulates 1 and 3 and Definition I.15), and `Parallelogram.complements` and `Parallelogram.about_diameter` bring in I.30 again.

**The squares inside.** CF and DG are squares by the argument Euclid gives inside II.4, which Lean proves once as `Square.about_diameter` (I.5, I.6, I.29, I.34); I.34 then makes GH equal to CA, so DG is the square on AC. `Square.described` makes ADEB a parallelogram by I.28, and `Rectangle.of_right` makes AF a rectangle from the square's right angle at B, by I.29. That a rectangle drawn is the rectangle contained by its sides Lean proves once, from I.4 and I.34, and `Rectangle.eq_rect` and `Square.eq_sq` bring them in. These are all the citations beyond Euclid's two brackets.

**The difference of two straight lines.** Heath reads AC as the difference of AB and BC, so that "*the square on the difference of two straight lines is equal to the sum of the squares on the straight lines diminished by twice the rectangle contained by them*" (vol. 1, p. 389). II.4 gives the square on the sum of two straight lines, II.7 the square on their difference; the sum of the two identities, he notes, is II.9 and II.10 in algebra, and their difference II.8. II.13 uses II.7 for the pieces a perpendicular makes in a triangle.

**The modern way.** With AB = a and BC = b, so that AC = a − b, II.7 says a² + b² = 2ab + (a − b)², which Heath rearranges as (a − b)² = a² + b² − 2ab.
