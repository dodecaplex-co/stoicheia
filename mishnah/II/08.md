**"As on one straight line."** Euclid's enunciation ends on "the square described on the whole and the aforesaid segment as on one straight line": the whole and the segment laid end to end. So he produces AB to D, with BD equal to CB, and describes the square AEFD on AD; our statement says so for every D so placed. Then "let the figure be drawn double": II.7's figure twice, once through B and once through C. The diameter DE and the two pairs of parallels cut the square into nine figures. I.36 and I.43 make four of them equal to CK and four equal to AG; these eight make up the gnomon STU, four times AK, and OH completes the square.

**Where do the two figures cross?** Each drawing of the figure brings its own points, but G and R, where a parallel of one crosses a parallel of the other, belong to neither, so we prove that those parallels meet. Then we put every point of the double figure in order on its straight line, M, G, K on MN as A, C, B lie on AD, and so for each row and column; and `Prop8.cell`, made for this figure, says how Euclid reads its cells: four corners where two pairs of parallels meet make a parallelogram. With these, I.36 can compare two cells on equal bases, and I.43 two complements about one diameter. Euclid spends one sentence on all this, the one that draws the figure double: once it is drawn, its nine cells lie there in order, ready to be named, and our proof has to show that they do.

**"Of decided use."** Heath notes that Pappus quotes the proposition, that Euclid uses it in the *Data*, Prop. 86, and that "it is of decided use in proving the fundamental property of a parabola" (vol. 1, p. 391). He gives two more proofs. The first, given by Christopher Clavius and others, puts II.4 and II.7 together, as Heath's own note on II.7 suggests. The second is "after the manner of Euclid but with a difference": four equal rectangles set round a square (Heath, vol. 1, pp. 391–392).

**The modern way.** With AB = a and BC = b, so that AC = a − b and AD = a + b, II.8 says 4ab + (a − b)² = (a + b)²: II.4's identity less II.7's. It gives the parabola too: put the focus a distance b from the vertex, and a point a along the axis and y away from it is as far from the focus as from the directrix when (a − b)² + y² = (a + b)², that is, when y² = 4ab.
