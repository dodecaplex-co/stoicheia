**The figure drawn double.** Euclid produces AB to D, with BD equal to CB, and describes the square AEFD on AD: "the square described on AB, BC as on one straight line" is the square on AD, and our statement says so for every D so placed (`sq A D`). The diameter DE and two pairs of parallels cut the square into nine figures. I.36 and I.43 make four of them equal to CK and four equal to AG, and these eight, which make up the gnomon STU, are four times AK; OH completes the square. As in II.5, the gnomon's letters name an area, not points, and Lean writes the sum of its three parallelograms.

**What the drawing takes for granted.** "Let the square AEFD be described" has no bracket; Lean cites I.46 there (`BookI.Prop46`), on a side chosen by a point off AD with no letter in Heath. `exists_off_line` finds that point with Postulates 1 and 3, Definition I.15 and `circles_meet` (System E, Intersection 5). Drawing the figure double is `Parallelogram.figure` twice, which brings in I.30 and I.31. Where a parallel of one figure crosses a parallel of the other, at G and at R, `BookI.Prop30.meet` finds the point: a straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other, as Heath reports Proclus arguing under I.31. `Parallel.between` puts every point in its order, and `Prop8.cell` shows the cells to be parallelograms.

**The squares within the square.** KD, CP, GR and OH are squares by the argument Euclid gives inside II.4, `Square.about_diameter` (I.5, I.6, I.29, I.34). I.34, which Euclid uses here without a bracket, makes opposite sides equal: CB to GK, BD to KN, BK to CG and OQ to AC. `Rectangle.of_right` makes AK a rectangle by I.29. That AK is the rectangle AB, BD, and OH the square on AC, rests on rectangles with equal sides being equal, from I.4 and I.34 (`Rectangle.eq_rect`, `Square.eq_sq`); and `Square.described` brings in I.28 to make AEFD a parallelogram.

**Heath's other proofs.** Heath notes that Pappus quotes the proposition, that Euclid uses it in the *Data*, Prop. 86, and that "it is of decided use in proving the fundamental property of a parabola" (vol. 1, p. 391). He gives two more proofs: Clavius', from II.4 and II.7, as his note on II.7 suggests, and one in Euclid's manner, with four equal rectangles set round a square (pp. 391–392). We follow Euclid's own.

**The modern way.** With AB = a and BC = b, so that AC = a − b and AD = a + b, II.8 says 4ab + (a − b)² = (a + b)². It is II.4's identity less II.7's, as Heath notes under II.7.
