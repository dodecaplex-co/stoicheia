**"Here abandoned."** Euclid proves II.9 without drawing a single square. Heath sees two new departures. From II.9 on, every proposition of Book II is proved by means of I.47; and "the method of demonstration by showing in the figures the various rectangles and squares to which the theorems relate is here abandoned" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 394). Four right-angled triangles do the work instead. In ACE and EGF the sides about the right angle are equal, so each gives a double; AEF adds the two, and ADF turns the sum into the squares on AD and DF, where DF is equal to DB.

**"The sum and difference."** Heath pairs II.9 with II.10 as he paired II.5 with II.6: "they really prove the same result which can, as in the earlier case, be comprised in a single enunciation thus: *The sum of the squares on the sum and difference of two given straight lines is equal to twice the sum of the squares on the lines*" (vol. 1, p. 394). Two more proofs follow in Heath (vol. 1, pp. 394–395). One works from II.4 and II.7 on a single straight line with its points marked, the way Heron worked. The other draws the squares after all, to show that the method of II.1 to II.8 still serves.

**Half a right angle.** Lean has no word for it, so our proof says what Euclid means: an angle which, added to itself, makes a right angle. Two such halves are equal (`Magnitude.eq_of_add_self_eq`), and so the angle GEF equals the angle EFG, and the angle at B the angle DFB. "The angle at D" is two angles: FDB, which I.29 makes right, and ADF beside it, which the last use of I.47 needs; `adjacent_eq_of_right` passes the right angle across. As in II.5, we let D fall on either side of C.

**Sparing with citations.** Euclid cites nothing for drawing CE at right angles (I.11), for the equal angles at A and E (I.5), for the parallels (I.31), or for the square on EF (I.47 once more), though II.10, its twin, cites every one. The Lean cites them all, and proves what the figure simply shows: F falls between E and B, and G between E and C.

**The modern way.** Let AC = CB = a and CD = b, with 0 < b < a. Then AD and DB are a + b and a − b, in one order or the other, and the identity is (a + b)² + (a − b)² = 2a² + 2b².
