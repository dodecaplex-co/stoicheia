Euclid proves II.9 without drawing a single square, and Heath marks two new departures. The first eight propositions of Book II are proved without I.47, and every one from the ninth on is proved by means of it; and "the method of demonstration by showing in the figures the various rectangles and squares to which the theorems relate is here abandoned" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 394). Four right-angled triangles do the work instead, in our proof as in his. In ACE and EGF the sides about the right angle are equal, so the square on EA is double the square on AC, and the square on EF double the square on GF; AEF adds the two, and ADF turns the sum into the squares on AD and DF, where DF is equal to DB. Heath gives no reference here for drawing CE at right angles, the equal angles at A and E, the parallels or the square on EF, though II.10, its twin, has one for each.

Heath pairs II.9 with II.10 as he paired II.5 with II.6: "they really prove the same result which can, as in the earlier case, be comprised in a single enunciation thus: *The sum of the squares on the sum and difference of two given straight lines is equal to twice the sum of the squares on the lines*" (vol. 1, p. 394). He gives two more proofs, and we keep Euclid's. One works from II.4 and II.7 on a single straight line with its points marked, in Heron's manner. The other draws the squares after all, and shows that the method of II.1 to II.8 "is still effective for the purpose of proving II. 9, 10" (vol. 1, p. 394).

**The modern way.** Let $\mathrm{AC} = \mathrm{CB} = a$ and $\mathrm{CD} = b$, with $0 \lt b \lt a$. Then AD and DB are $a + b$ and $a - b$, in one order or the other, and II.9 is $(a + b)^2 + (a - b)^2 = 2a^2 + 2b^2$. For any two vectors the same identity reads $|\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}|^2 + |\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}|^2 = 2|\mathbf{u}|^2 + 2|\mathbf{v}|^2$, the parallelogram law: the squares on the two diagonals of a parallelogram are together equal to the squares on its four sides. II.9 is the case of two vectors along one straight line.
