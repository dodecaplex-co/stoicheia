**Squares without their outlines.** Heath sees two new departures here. From II.9 on, every proposition of Book II is proved by means of I.47; and "the method of demonstration by showing in the figures the various rectangles and squares to which the theorems relate is here abandoned" (vol. 1, p. 394). Four right-angled triangles do the work instead: ACE and EGF give the doubles, AEF puts them together, and ADF gives the squares sought. So our proof calls `BookI.Prop47.sq` four times, once where Euclid gives no bracket, for the square on EF.

**Where Euclid gives no bracket.** "Let CE be drawn from C at right angles to AB" is I.11, and `OnRay.exists_len_eq` makes CE equal to AC. "The angle EAC is also equal to the angle AEC" is I.5, in the isosceles triangle ACE, and again in BCE. I.31 draws DF and FG, through `BookI.Prop31.line` and `BookI.Prop31.parallelogram`. That they meet EB and CE rests on I.30, as Heath reports Proclus deducing under I.31: a straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other (`BookI.Prop30.meet` for F, the parallelogram step for G).

**What the figure shows.** Euclid's figure puts F between E and B, and G between E and C; Lean proves both with `Between.of_sameSide`, which carries order across a straight line. Lean has no word for half a right angle: it is an angle which, added to itself, makes a right angle, and two such halves are equal (`Magnitude.eq_of_add_self_eq`). At D, `adjacent_eq_of_right` makes the angle ADF right, since FDB beside it is. The statement lets D fall on either side of C: on A's side, we exchange A and B and are back in Euclid's figure.

**What the squares rest on.** `BookI.Prop47.sq` describes its squares by I.46, makes them parallelograms by I.28, and names each the square on its side through I.4 and I.34; so I.4, I.28 and I.46 join Euclid's citations, and Postulate 5 comes in through I.29, I.30, I.32, I.34, I.46 and I.47. Heath adds two proofs (pp. 394–395): one from II.4 and II.7 on a straight line with its points marked, as Heron, in an-Nairīzī, proved II.2–II.10; one with the squares drawn, as in II.1–II.8.

**The modern way.** Let AC = CB = a and CD = b, with 0 < b < a. Then AD and DB are a + b and a − b, in one order or the other, and the identity is (a + b)² + (a − b)² = 2a² + 2b².
