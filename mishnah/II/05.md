**The gnomon.** Heath quotes a scholium explaining its name: "from it the whole is known, whether of the whole area or of the remainder, when it is either placed round or taken away" (vol. 1, p. 371). Here NOP names CH, DM and HF together. N, O and P label this figure; they are not points. Heath remarks, "It will be observed that the gnomon is indicated in the figure by three separate letters and a dotted curve" (vol. 1, p. 383). The curve leaves LG outside its sweep.

**Adding the same figures.** I.43 makes CH equal to HF. Adding DM gives CM equal to DF; I.36 makes CM equal to AL because AC equals CB. Adding CH now makes AH equal to the gnomon. Lean proves that C lies between A and D and, by `Parallel.between`, that L lies between K and H. The shared steps `Parallelogram.figure`, `BookI.Prop31.parallelogram`, `Parallelogram.complements` and `Parallelogram.about_diameter` bring in I.30, as does `BookI.Prop30.parallel` for AK and DG. That KM, meeting CL, meets AK too is the argument Heath reports from Proclus under I.31. The last shared step makes the gnomon and LG the whole square.

**Either side of the middle.** Heath writes, "Euclid's figure corresponds to one only of the two solutions" (vol. 1, p. 384). Our statement allows D on either side of C. Lean gives Euclid's construction once, with D between C and B, and exchanges A and B for the other case. Equal distances from A would force D to be C, contrary to the unequal section. Before describing CEFB by I.46, `exists_off_line` supplies a side to stand on, using Postulates 1 and 3 and Definition 15.

**Naming the rectangles and squares.** Euclid takes for granted that a rectangle drawn is the rectangle contained by its sides. Lean proves it once from I.4 and I.34, and `Rectangle.eq_rect`, `Square.eq_sq` and `Square.described` bring them in to name AH, LG and CEFB by their sides. `Square.described` also uses I.28 to make CEFB a parallelogram. `Rectangle.of_right` uses I.29 to make AH a rectangle from its right angle. `Square.about_diameter` makes DM and LG squares through I.5, I.6, I.29 and I.34, the argument Euclid gives in II.4. Thus DH equals DB. I.34 also gives LH equal to CD, so LG is the square on CD.

**The modern way.** In the algebraic language discussed under II.4, put C at 0, A at −h, B at h and D at d, with 0 < |d| < h. Then (h + d)(h − d) + d² = h², Heath's difference of squares identity with the unequal parts h + d and h − d (vol. 1, p. 385).
