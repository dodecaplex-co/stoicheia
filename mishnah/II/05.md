**The gnomon.** Heath quotes a scholium on its name: "from it the whole is known, whether of the whole area or of the remainder, when it is either placed round or taken away" (vol. 1, p. 371). Here the gnomon NOP is CH, DM and HF together, and Heath remarks, "It will be observed that the gnomon is indicated in the figure by three separate letters and a dotted curve" (vol. 1, p. 383). The curve runs round H and leaves out LG. So N, O and P name an area, not points, and Lean writes the sum of three parallelograms.

**"One only of the two solutions."** Heath reads II.5 as solving a quadratic equation, whose two roots put the point of section on either side of C. "Euclid's figure corresponds to one only of the two solutions," he writes (vol. 1, p. 384), though he has no doubt that Euclid knew both. Euclid's setting-out names no side, so our statement lets D fall on either: Lean proves Euclid's case, D between C and B, and gets the other by exchanging A and B. Comparing AD with AC tells the cases apart; were they equal, D would be C.

**Where the parallels meet.** Before I.46, a point off CB with no letter in Heath chooses the square's side; `exists_off_line` finds one with Postulates 1 and 3 and Definition I.15. `Parallelogram.figure` draws DG and KM, and `BookI.Prop31.parallelogram` draws AK and finds K on KM: KM meets CL, so it meets AK too, by I.30, as Heath reports Proclus arguing under I.31. I.30 comes in again through `Parallelogram.complements`, `Parallelogram.about_diameter` and `BookI.Prop30.parallel`, which makes AK parallel to DG; and `Parallel.between` puts L between K and H, so that AH is AL and CH together.

**Naming the rectangles and squares.** Euclid takes for granted that a rectangle drawn is the rectangle contained by its sides. Lean proves it once, from I.4 and I.34, and `Rectangle.eq_rect`, `Square.eq_sq` and `Square.described` bring them in when they name AH, LG and CEFB. `Square.described` makes CEFB a parallelogram by I.28, and `Rectangle.of_right` makes AH a rectangle from its right angle at D, by I.29. That DM and LG are squares, so DH is equal to DB, is Euclid's argument inside II.4, `Square.about_diameter` (I.5, I.6, I.29, I.34); I.34 makes LH equal to CD, so LG is the square on CD.

**The modern way.** Put C at 0, A at −h, B at h and D at d, with 0 < |d| < h: then (h + d)(h − d) + d² = h², or h² − d² = (h + d)(h − d), the difference of two squares that Heath finds in II.5 and II.6 alike (vol. 1, p. 383).
