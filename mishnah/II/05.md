**"From it the whole is known."** The gnomon of Definition II.2 enters Euclid's proofs here, and stays for four propositions. In a square it is what is left when a smaller square is cut from one corner: a carpenter's square. A scholium explains the name: "from it the whole is known, whether of the whole area or of the remainder, when it is either placed round or taken away" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 371).

**Three letters for one area.** Here the gnomon NOP is CH, DM and HF together, and Heath remarks, "It will be observed that the gnomon is indicated in the figure by three separate letters and a dotted curve" (vol. 1, p. 383); the curve runs round H. Heath relettered the manuscripts' figure, which had two M's, and we keep his letters. They name an area, not points, so our Lean writes the sum of three parallelograms.

**"Their exceeding and their falling-short."** Proclus, writing on I.44, calls such propositions ancient, "the discoveries of the Muse of the Pythagoreans, the application of areas, their exceeding and their falling-short" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 384). To apply a rectangle to a straight line is to build it along that line. AH is the rectangle AD, DB, for DM is a square, as in II.4; applied to AB, it falls short by the square DM. Heath reads II.5 as the problem this suggests, to apply to a given straight line a rectangle equal to a given square and falling short by a square figure: the equation ax − x² = b² (vol. 1, p. 383).

**"One only of the two solutions."** That equation has two roots, which put the point of section on either side of C. "Euclid's figure corresponds to one only of the two solutions," Heath writes (vol. 1, p. 384), though he has no doubt that Euclid knew both. Euclid's setting-out names no side, so our statement lets D fall on either: we prove Euclid's case, D between C and B, and get the other by exchanging A and B. Lean must be told which case it is in, and comparing AD with AC tells; were they equal, D would be C, and the segments would not be unequal.

**The modern way.** Put C at 0, A at −h, B at h and D at d, with 0 < |d| < h: then (h + d)(h − d) + d² = h², or h² − d² = (h + d)(h − d), the difference of two squares that Heath finds in II.5 and II.6 alike (vol. 1, p. 383).
