The gnomon of Definition II.2 enters Euclid's proofs here and stays for four propositions. In a square it is what is left when a smaller square is cut from one corner, the shape of a carpenter's square, and a scholium explains the name: "from it the whole is known, whether of the whole area or of the remainder, when it is either placed round or taken away" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 371). Euclid's gnomon NOP is CH, DM and HF together, marked "by three separate letters and a dotted curve" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 383), the curve running round H. The manuscripts' figure had two M's, and Heath relettered it; we keep his letters. NOP names an area, not three points, so our Lean writes it as the sum of its three parallelograms, as [Book II's definitions](front.md) do for Definition II.2.

Heath recalls here Proclus' account of "the application of areas, their exceeding and their falling-short", met under [I.44](../I/44.md) (vol. 1, p. 384). II.5 is the falling-short: AH, the rectangle AD, DB, applied to AB, falls short of it by the square DM. So Heath reads II.5 as a problem, to apply to a given straight line a rectangle equal to a given square and falling short by a square figure, and when the given square is less than the square on half the line, the problem has two solutions, with D on either side of C. "Euclid's figure corresponds to one only of the two solutions," he writes (vol. 1, p. 384), though he has no doubt that Euclid knew both. Euclid's setting-out names no side, so our statement lets D fall on either: we prove his case, D between C and B, and get the other by exchanging A and B.

**The modern way.** Heath's problem, with $\mathrm{AB} = a$ and $\mathrm{DB} = x$, is the quadratic $ax - x^2 = b^2$ (vol. 1, p. 383). Its two roots put D at equal distances from C, one on each side, and they are real only when $b \le a/2$: by II.5 the rectangle AD, DB is the square on CB less the square on CD, so it is greatest when D is at C. At $b = a/2$ the two roots meet and D falls on C, where the segments are no longer unequal, so II.5's own case is $b \lt a/2$. Heath also writes II.5 in algebra (vol. 1, p. 385); with $\mathrm{AD} = p$ and $\mathrm{DB} = q$ (his $a$ and $b$, renamed to keep our earlier $a$ and $b$ free), it is $((p + q)/2)^2 - ((p - q)/2)^2 = pq$, and from it he draws the two rules, attributed to the Pythagoreans and to Plato, for a square number equal to two square numbers: $p = n^2$ and $q = 1$, with $n$ odd, give $((n^2 + 1)/2)^2 = ((n^2 - 1)/2)^2 + n^2$, and $p = 2n^2$ and $q = 2$ give $(n^2 + 1)^2 = (n^2 - 1)^2 + (2n)^2$.
