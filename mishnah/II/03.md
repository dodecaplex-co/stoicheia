**The whole and its two pieces.** Euclid describes the square CDEB on CB, produces ED to F, and draws AF through A parallel to CD. The rectangle AE is then the rectangle AD together with the square CE. These names use opposite corners: AE is AFEB, AD is AFDC, and CE and DB name the same square. Naming a rectangle by the two straight lines that contain it is Definition 1 of Book II; Heath's note on the phrase is quoted under II.1.

**Where F falls.** Euclid leaves the position of F to the figure. Lean proves that D lies between F and E, since C lies between A and B and parallels keep order (`Parallel.between`). Then `quadArea_add_of_parallelograms` says that the two pieces make the whole. A Lean straight line already extends both ways, so naming DE leaves F to be found by the parallel through A. The construction uses Postulate 1 to name straight lines. To choose a side for the square, `exists_off_line` supplies an unlettered point, using Postulates 1 and 3, Definition 15 and System E's rules.

**What the rectangle names require.** `Square.described` uses I.28 to make the square a parallelogram. It and `Rectangle.eq_rect` use I.4 and I.34 to identify drawn figures with the rectangles contained by their sides. `Rectangle.of_right` uses I.29 to make all four angles right. The angle DCA beside the square's right angle BCD is right by `adjacent_eq_of_right`, System E, Diagram-angle 3. `BookI.Prop31.parallelogram` uses I.30 to find F, following the argument Heath reports Proclus giving under I.31. `BookI.Prop30.parallel` supplies I.30 again: AF is parallel to BE as well as CD.

**Heath's wider use.** Heath writes that "this proposition and the preceding are, as already indicated, of great importance, and particularly so to the semi-algebraical method just described, which seems to have found its first exponents in Heron and Pappus" (vol. 1, p. 378). He describes that method under II.2. His note gives Lardner's deduction from II.1–II.3: II.2 divides the greater square into two rectangles; II.3 separates the lesser square from one of them; II.1 combines the remaining rectangles. Thus the difference of the squares is the rectangle contained by the sum and difference of their sides (pp. 378–379). Clavius's proof of II.4 from II.2 and II.3, which Heath gives under II.4, is another use.

**The modern way.** With CB = a and AC = b, the identity is (a + b)a = ab + a², the third in Heath's account of geometrical algebra, quoted under II.4. Euclid obtains it by cutting the rectangle along CD and naming its two pieces.
