Euclid describes the square CDEB on CB, and then calls it by two other names: CE, when it is added to AD to make AE, and DB, when it is the square on CB. He names a figure by two opposite corners, so AE is AFEB and AD is AFDC. "Let ED be drawn through to F" names F before saying where it lies. In our foundations a straight line is already the whole straight line, so when we name DE it is drawn through, and the parallel through A meets it at F.

Leave Book II itself aside and look to general use, Heath says, and this proposition and II.2 are "of great importance, and particularly so to the semi-algebraical method just described, which seems to have found its first exponents in Heron and Pappus" (vol. 1, p. 378). Euclid uses II.3 again in IX.15, and Pappus often quotes it by its book and number (Heath, under II.2). Heath's example of the method is Dionysius Lardner's proof, by II.1 to II.3 alone, that "*the difference of the squares on two straight lines is equal to the rectangle contained by the sum and the difference of the straight lines*" (vol. 1, pp. 378–379), a theorem generally given as equivalent to II.5 and II.6. II.2 makes the square on AB the rectangles AB, BC and AB, AC; II.3 takes the square on BC out of the first; and II.1 joins the two rectangles left over into one, contained by AC, the difference, and the sum of AB and BC.

**The modern way.** Euclid's figure draws $(a + b)a = ab + a^2$, with $\mathrm{CB} = a$ and $\mathrm{AC} = b$: the rectangle AE cut along CD into the rectangle AD and the square. It is the third of the identities Heath lists for Book II. Lardner's theorem, with $p = \mathrm{AB}$ and $q = \mathrm{BC}$, is $p^2 - q^2 = (p - q)(p + q)$.
