**One square, two names.** Euclid describes the square CDEB on CB, draws ED through to F, and draws AF parallel to CD. He names each figure by two opposite corners: AE is AFEB, and AD is AFDC. The square is CE when it is added to AD, and DB when it is called the square on CB; Lean reads it as one figure, CDEB, and `sq_symm` makes the square on CB the square on BC of the enunciation.

**Where F falls.** "Let ED be drawn through to F" names F before saying where it is. A straight line in Lean is already the whole straight line, so naming DE draws it through, and the parallel through A meets it at F, from I.30, as Heath reports Proclus drawing it under I.31 (`BookI.Prop31.parallelogram`). I.30 also makes AF parallel to BE as well as CD (`BookI.Prop30.parallel`). The parallels put D between F and E, as C lies between A and B (`Parallel.between`), and Book Zero's `quadArea_add_of_parallelograms` makes AD and CE the whole AE.

**The right angles.** A point off CB, with no letter in Euclid's figure, chooses the side of the square (`exists_off_line`), and `Square.described` makes CDEB a parallelogram by I.28, and the square on CB. One right angle makes a parallelogram a rectangle (`Rectangle.of_right`, by I.29): AE has the square's right angle at B, and AD has the angle DCA, right because it lies beside the square's right angle BCD (System E, Diagram-angle 3). `Rectangle.eq_rect`, which rests on I.4 and I.34, then names each by its sides, as Euclid does.

**"Of great importance."** Heath writes that "this proposition and the preceding are, as already indicated, of great importance, and particularly so to the semi-algebraical method just described, which seems to have found its first exponents in Heron and Pappus" (vol. 1, p. 378). His example is Lardner's proof, from II.1–II.3, that the difference of the squares on two straight lines is the rectangle contained by their sum and difference (pp. 378–379): II.2 splits the square on AB into the rectangles AB, BC and AB, AC, II.3 takes the square on BC out of the first, and II.1 joins the two rectangles left over. Heath finds the same kind of proof of II.4, from II.2 and II.3, in Clavius.

**The modern way.** With CB = a and AC = b, II.3 is (a + b)a = ab + a², the third of the identities Heath lists for Book II. Euclid's figure draws it: the rectangle AE, cut along CD.
