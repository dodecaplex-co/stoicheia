**A familiar division.** Heath writes: "The *fact* asserted in the enunciation of this proposition has already been used in the proof of I. 47" (vol. 1, p. 377). There the two rectangles make up the square on the hypotenuse. Here Euclid names each rectangle by the straight lines containing it, following Definition II.1, whose wording Heath explains in his note on that definition. Heath also calls II.2 and II.3 particular cases of II.1. Our II.1 takes two points of section, so its statement cannot supply this single cut directly. Euclid's own square and parallel give the proof.

**Where the cut ends.** C lies strictly between A and B. Postulate 1 names AB; `exists_off_line` supplies a point off it to choose the side for the square described by I.46. Euclid leaves this point unlettered. `BookI.Prop31.parallelogram` draws CF and finds F on DE, using I.30 for the meeting, as Heath reports Proclus doing under I.31. `BookI.Prop30.parallel` makes CF parallel to BE as well as AD, giving Euclid's "either". Lean then needs F between D and E: `Parallel.between` carries the order A, C, B across the three parallels. Book Zero's `quadArea_add_of_parallelograms` now makes the two pieces the whole.

**Naming the pieces.** `Square.described` makes ADEB a parallelogram by I.28, since its right angles make opposite sides parallel, and identifies it with the square on AB. `Rectangle.of_right` uses I.29 to make each smaller parallelogram a rectangle from one right angle. That the rectangle drawn is the rectangle contained by its sides rests on rectangles with equal sides being equal, proved once from I.4 and I.34 in `Rectangle.contained`; both `Rectangle.eq_rect` and `Square.described` use this step. These shared steps account for every citation beyond Euclid's brackets. The square's equal sides let AF be named by BA, AC and CE by AB, BC. Their sum is the square, and exchanging the summands gives the enunciation's order.

**The modern way.** With AC = a and CB = b, the identity is (a + b)b + (a + b)a = (a + b)². This is distributivity applied to a square, expressed geometrically by its division into two rectangles, in the sense of Heath's account of geometrical algebra discussed under II.4.
