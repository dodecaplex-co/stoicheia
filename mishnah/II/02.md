**Already in I.47.** Euclid has cut a square this way before. In I.47 the perpendicular from the right angle divides the square on the side subtending it into two rectangles, but I.47 had no need to say that they are the rectangles contained by that side and its two parts. II.2 says just that. Heath writes: "The *fact* asserted in the enunciation of this proposition has already been used in the proof of I. 47" (vol. 1, p. 377).

**Why prove it afresh?** II.2 and II.3 are particular cases of II.1, Heath notes, stated apart so as to be ready for later use: Euclid uses II.2 again in XIII.10. Yet he proves each from its own figure, and keeps the proofs of the first ten all but independent of one another. Heath's reason: "Surely the object was to show the power of the *method* of geometrical algebra as much as to arrive at results" (vol. 1, p. 377). He even counts the words. Euclid's proof runs to 126 in Heath's translation, 22 of them for the construction, and those are "much more than atoned for by the advantage of picturing the result in the figure" (vol. 1, p. 378).

**"Parallel to either AD or BE."** Euclid's "either" carries a step of its own. A straight line through C parallel to AD is parallel to BE too, because AD and BE are parallel: that is I.30, and the Lean cites it beside his I.31. AD and BE are each equal to AB, so AF is the rectangle BA, AC, and CE the rectangle AB, BC. Our statement follows Euclid's "I say"; his conclusion names the two rectangles in the other order, so the last line of our proof turns the sum round to match.

**The modern way.** With AC = a and CB = b, II.2 is (a + b)b + (a + b)a = (a + b)²: the whole times each part, added together, is the whole times itself.
