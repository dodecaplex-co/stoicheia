**Already in I.47.** Heath writes: "The *fact* asserted in the enunciation of this proposition has already been used in the proof of I. 47" (vol. 1, p. 377). There the perpendicular from the right angle divides the square on the side subtending it into two rectangles, but I.47 had no need to say that they are the rectangles contained by that side and its two parts; II.2 says just that. Heath calls II.2 and II.3 particular cases of II.1, stated apart so as to be ready for later use, and asks why Euclid makes the proofs of the first ten all but independent of one another. His answer: "Surely the object was to show the power of the *method* of geometrical algebra as much as to arrive at results" (vol. 1, p. 377).

**Which side, and where F falls.** Every point so far lies on AB, so the square needs a side to stand on: `exists_off_line` finds a point off AB, with no letter in Euclid's figure, and I.46 describes ADEB on its side. "Parallel to either AD or BE" asks for I.30 as well as I.31. `BookI.Prop31.parallelogram` draws CF parallel to AD and finds F where it meets DE, for a straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other, from I.30, as Heath reports Proclus drawing it under I.31; `BookI.Prop30.parallel` makes CF parallel to BE too. The three parallels keep the order of A, C, B, so F lies between D and E (`Parallel.between`), and Book Zero's `quadArea_add_of_parallelograms` makes AF and CE the whole AE.

**Naming the pieces.** `Square.described` makes ADEB a parallelogram, its right angles making its opposite sides parallel by I.28, and the square on AB. One right angle makes each smaller parallelogram a rectangle (`Rectangle.of_right`, by I.29), and `Rectangle.eq_rect` makes it the rectangle contained by its sides, which Lean proves once from I.4 and I.34 (`Rectangle.contained`). AD and BE are equal to AB, so AF is the rectangle BA, AC, and CE the rectangle AB, BC. Euclid's conclusion names the two rectangles in the other order from his "I say", and Lean turns the sum round to match.

**The modern way.** With AC = a and CB = b, II.2 is (a + b)b + (a + b)a = (a + b)²: the whole times each part, added together, is the whole times itself.
