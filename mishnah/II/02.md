Euclid has cut a square this way before. In I.47 the perpendicular from the right angle divides the square on the side subtending it into two rectangles, though that proof had no need to say that they are the rectangles contained by the side and each of its parts. II.2 says it, and Heath points back: "The *fact* asserted in the enunciation of this proposition has already been used in the proof of I. 47" (vol. 1, p. 377).

Why prove it at all, when it is II.1 with the uncut straight line taken equal to the whole? Heath has no doubt that Euclid stated II.2 and II.3 separately so that they could be quoted later, as II.2 is in XIII.10; and he thinks Euclid proved them, like all of the first ten, from figures of their own to show the method at work: "Surely the object was to show the power of the *method* of geometrical algebra as much as to arrive at results" (vol. 1, p. 377). Heath even counts the words. Euclid's proof has 126 in his translation, 22 of them for the construction, and H. M. Taylor's 120, with 8 for its construction; so Euclid's proof proper is the shorter by 8, and his extra words of construction are "much more than atoned for by the advantage of picturing the result in the figure" (vol. 1, p. 378).
