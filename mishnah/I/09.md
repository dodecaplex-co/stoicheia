**Which side of DE?** Heath: "It will be observed from the translation of this proposition that Euclid does not say, in his description of the construction, that the equilateral triangle should be constructed on the side of DE opposite to A; he leaves this to be inferred from his figure" (vol. 1, p. 264). On A's side, the corner of the triangle can land on A itself, or behind A, and then AF is no straight line at all, or points away from the angle. Proclus worked through those cases for a reader who might claim not to see any room below DE, and Heath finds "no particular value in Proclus' explanation as to how we should proceed in case any one should assert that he could not recognise the existence of any space below DE" (vol. 1, p. 264). Lean infers nothing from a figure, so the side has to be asked for. Book Zero's `exists_oppositeSidesOf` (System E, Points 6) gives a point Q on the far side of DE from A, I.1 as stated here builds the triangle on Q's side, and Book Zero's [`SameSideOf.trans_opposite`](../../Elements/Figure/Sides.lean) notes that F is then on the far side from A, since a straight line has only two sides.

**A chance point, again.** As in I.5, D is taken "at random", and in the very next breath AE is cut off from AC equal to AD [I. 3], which needs AD to be the less. So D must not lie too far out along AB. Book Zero's [`exists_between_nearer`](../../Elements/Figure/Circles.lean), the step I.5 takes too, keeps it inside a circle about A through C: anywhere between A and B if B is not outside that circle, or else between A and the point where AB leaves it (System E, Circle 2 and Intersections 4). It is a circle Euclid never draws.

**Does F fall within the angle?** Heath: "Proclus also answers the possible objection that might be raised to Euclid's proof on the ground that it assumes that, if the equilateral triangle be described on the side of DE opposite to A, its vertex F will lie within the angle BAC. The objector is supposed to argue that this is not necessary, but that F might fall either on one of the lines forming the angle or outside it altogether" (vol. 1, p. 265). In Lean we are that objector, twice over. I.8 wants ADF and AEF to be triangles, so F must be off AB and off AC; and "bisected by the straight line AF", as our statement reads it, says that AF falls within the angle, which I.10 will need. [`Prop9.crossing`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop09.lean) finds the point M where AF crosses the straight line DE (Intersection 1 and Pasch 4) and shows that it lies between D and E. If M were D itself, F would lie on AB, and here the Lean follows Proclus with the second part of I.5: the angles under the base of the isosceles triangle ADE are equal, and so are the angles at the base of DEF, so the angle DEF would be equal to the angle DEC, and F would lie on AC as well as AB (`Prop9.not_on_arm`, with Book Zero's `onRay_of_angle_eq`). Heath: "Here then is the second case in which, in Proclus' view, the second part of I. 5 is useful for refuting objections" (vol. 1, p. 265). If M fell beyond D or beyond E, F would lie outside the angle. Proclus answers that case with I.5 again; the Lean takes the road I.8 has just travelled instead: D and E would be on the same side of AF, each as far from A and from F as the other, and I.7 makes them one point. From M, `Prop9.side` then reads off the sides of AB and AC with Pasch 2.

**What else Lean made us say.** Euclid's conclusion is about the angles DAF and EAF; the angle he set out to bisect is BAC. The angle DAF is the angle BAF because D lies on the ray from A through B, and EAF is FAC because E lies on AC and an angle may be read either way round (`angle_onRay_left` and `angle_symm`, System E's Diagram-angle 4 and Metric 4). That the whole angle BAC is made up of BAF and FAC needs F within it once more (`angle_add_of_inside`, Diagram-angle 2). So the certificate cites I.1, I.5 and I.7 as well as the I.3 and I.8 that Euclid brackets, and the proof itself uses Postulate 2, because our I.5 is stated with its sides produced (I.7 and I.8 bring it in as well). Every rule cited is one of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma's; LeanEuclid, by Logan Murphy, Kaiyu Yang, Jialiang Sun, Zhaoyu Li, Anima Anandkumar and Xujie Si, was open alongside the whole time; and Michael Beeson, Julien Narboux and Freek Wiedijk's *Proof-checking Euclid* is a generous guide to the facts of position that hide in figures like this one.

**Only rectilineal angles.** Proclus, Heath says, takes the chance to insist that the angle be rectilineal, since bisecting curved angles is no matter for an elementary treatise and may not even be possible: "Thus it is difficult to say whether it is possible to bisect the so-called horn-like angle" (vol. 1, p. 265), the angle between a circle and a straight line touching it. Here, too, Proclus gives "his valuable historical note about the trisection of any acute angle, which (as well as the division of an angle in any given ratio) requires resort to other curves than circles" (vol. 1, p. 265): the conchoid of Nicomedes, the quadratrix of Hippias, the spiral of Archimedes. In 1837 Pierre Wantzel proved that straight lines and circles alone cannot trisect every angle: for an angle in general, halving and halving again is all that Euclid's tools can do.

**The modern way.** Put A at the origin, with unit vectors u and v along AB and AC: then u + v, the diagonal of the rhombus on u and v, points along the bisector, just as the diagonal AF of Euclid's kite ADFE does. With complex numbers, arms along e^{iβ} and e^{iγ} have their bisector along e^{i(β+γ)/2}.
