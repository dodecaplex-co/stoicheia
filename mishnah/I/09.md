Euclid builds the equilateral triangle on DE by I.1, though Heath gives no reference for it here. On which side? "Euclid does not say, in his description of the construction, that the equilateral triangle should be constructed on the side of DE opposite to A; he leaves this to be inferred from his figure" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 264). Proclus even provides for a reader who "could not recognise the existence of any space below DE": he builds on A's side, and the vertex may then fall on A, above A or below it (Heath, vol. 1, p. 264). We grant that space by a rule of System E (Points 6), which gives a point beyond DE from A, and our I.1 builds on that point's side.

Proclus also imagined an objector for whom F might fall on an arm of the angle BAC, or outside it (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265). Our proof has to answer that objector, because I.10 will want AF within the angle. Were F on AB, the angles under the base of I.5 would put it on AC as well: "Here then is the second case in which, in Proclus' view, the second part of I. 5 is useful for refuting objections" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265). Were AF to cross DE beyond D or E, I.7 would make D and E one point. So AF crosses DE between them, and falls within the angle.

Proclus stresses the word "rectilineal", for "it is difficult to say whether it is possible to bisect the so-called *horn-like* angle" between a circle and a straight line touching it (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265), the angle that III.16 meets. Here too he gives "his valuable historical note about the *trisection* of any acute angle, which (as well as the division of an angle in any given ratio) requires resort to other curves than circles" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265): the conchoid of Nicomedes, the quadratrix of Hippias, the spiral of Archimedes. In 1837 Pierre Wantzel proved that straight lines and circles alone cannot trisect every angle.

**The modern way.** Put A at the origin, with unit vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ along AB and AC. Their sum $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$, the diagonal of the rhombus on them, points along the bisector, as the diagonal AF of Euclid's kite ADFE does. Read as complex numbers $u$ and $v$, the argument of $u + v$ is the average of theirs, taken less than $\pi$ apart.
