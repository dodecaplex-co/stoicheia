**Which side of DE?** Euclid builds the equilateral triangle on DE by I.1, without a citation. Heath: "Euclid does not say, in his description of the construction, that the equilateral triangle should be constructed on the side of DE opposite to A; he leaves this to be inferred from his figure" (vol. 1, p. 264). Proclus even provides for a reader who "could not recognise the existence of any space below DE": he builds on A's side, and the vertex may then fall on A, above A or below it (Heath, vol. 1, p. 264). Lean grants that space by a rule: `exists_oppositeSidesOf` (System E, Points 6) gives a point beyond DE from A, and our I.1 builds on that point's side.

**Does F fall within the angle?** Proclus also imagined an objector for whom F might fall on an arm of the angle BAC, or outside it (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265). We are that objector, for I.10 will want AF within the angle. [`Prop9.crossing`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop09.lean) finds where AF crosses DE, between D and E. At D, F would lie on AB, and Proclus' answer puts it on AC too (`Prop9.not_on_arm`): "Here then is the second case in which, in Proclus' view, the second part of I. 5 is useful for refuting objections" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265). Beyond D or E, D and E would lie on one side of AF, each as far from A and from F as the other, and I.7 makes them one point.

**Why "rectilineal"?** Proclus stresses the word, for "it is difficult to say whether it is possible to bisect the so-called *horn-like* angle" between a circle and its tangent (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265), the angle that III.16 meets. Here too he gives "his valuable historical note about the *trisection* of any acute angle, which (as well as the division of an angle in any given ratio) requires resort to other curves than circles" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265): the conchoid of Nicomedes, the quadratrix of Hippias, the spiral of Archimedes. In 1837 Pierre Wantzel proved that straight lines and circles alone cannot trisect every angle.

**The modern way.** Put A at the origin, with unit vectors u and v along AB and AC: then u + v, the diagonal of the rhombus on u and v, points along the bisector, as the diagonal AF of Euclid's kite ADFE does. As complex numbers, the argument of u + v is the average of those of u and v, taken less than π apart.
