**Which side of DE?** Heath: "It will be observed from the translation of this proposition that Euclid does not say, in his description of the construction, that the equilateral triangle should be constructed on the side of DE opposite to A; he leaves this to be inferred from his figure" (vol. 1, p. 264). On A's side, F could land on A itself, or behind it. Lean infers nothing from a figure, so the side is asked for: Book Zero's `exists_oppositeSidesOf` (System E, Points 6) gives a point on the far side of DE from A, and our I.1 builds the triangle on that point's side.

**Does F fall within the angle?** Proclus imagined an objector for whom F might fall on an arm of the angle BAC, or outside it (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265). We are that objector, since I.8 wants ADF and AEF to be triangles and I.10 wants AF within the angle. [`Prop9.crossing`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop09.lean) finds where AF crosses DE, between D and E. If it crossed at D, F would lie on AB, and Proclus' argument with the angles under the base of I.5 would put it on AC too (`Prop9.not_on_arm`). Heath: "Here then is the second case in which, in Proclus' view, the second part of I. 5 is useful for refuting objections" (vol. 1, p. 265). If it crossed beyond D or E, D and E would be on one side of AF, each as far from A and from F as the other, and I.7 makes them one point.

**Five propositions, not two.** Euclid brackets I.3 and I.8. The proof also cites I.1, for the equilateral triangle he constructs without a bracket, and I.5 and I.7, to answer Proclus' objector. Our I.5 is stated with its sides produced, so the proof uses Postulate 2 (I.7 and I.8 bring it in as well).

**And trisection?** It is here that Proclus gives "his valuable historical note about the trisection of any acute angle, which (as well as the division of an angle in any given ratio) requires resort to other curves than circles" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 265): the conchoid of Nicomedes, the quadratrix of Hippias, the spiral of Archimedes. In 1837 Pierre Wantzel proved that straight lines and circles alone cannot trisect every angle.

**The modern way.** Put A at the origin, with unit vectors u and v along AB and AC: then u + v, the diagonal of the rhombus on u and v, points along the bisector, as the diagonal AF of Euclid's kite ADFE does. With complex numbers, arms along e^{iβ} and e^{iγ}, with β and γ less than π apart, have their bisector along e^{i(β+γ)/2}.
