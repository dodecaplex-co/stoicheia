**Turning I.47 round.** Book I ends with the converse of its great theorem, and Euclid proves it by building a right angle to compare with. He draws AD at right angles to AC, makes it equal to BA, and joins DC; then I.47 and the hypothesis make DC equal to BC, and I.8 makes the angle BAC equal to the right angle DAC. The right angle is I.11, though Heath gives no bracket. We put D on the other side of AC from B, as Heath draws it.

**Three lengths, one right angle.** Under I.47 Heath cites Vitruvius for Pythagoras' way of setting out a right angle: three lengths, of 3, 4 and 5. The recipe rests on this proposition: 9 and 16 make 25, so the angle between the two shorter lengths is right. That is the converse the figure at the top shows.

**"So that the side DC is also equal to BC."** Twice Euclid passes between equal sides and equal squares in a clause: from DA and AB to their squares at the start, and from the squares on DC and BC back to their sides here. Our proof cites each direction as a theorem of its own, both proved after I.46, where Heath reports Proclus proving them. Euclid also speaks of the squares on DA, AC and DC without describing them. Lean describes all three by I.46, outward from the triangle DAC as I.47 asks, beside the three our statement gives, which may stand on either side of their sides.

**"An appearance of avoiding indirect demonstration."** Proclus gives another proof, the one an-Nairīzī attributes to Heron: draw the perpendicular on B's side of AC instead, and I.7 will not let it fall anywhere but on AB. Augustus De Morgan sees in Euclid's own proof "an appearance of avoiding indirect demonstration by drawing the triangles on different sides of the base and appealing to I. 8, because drawing them on the same side would make the appeal to I. 7 (on which, however, I. 8 is founded)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 369). Our I.8 cites I.7 as well, so his parenthesis holds in Lean too.

**The modern way.** Put u = B − A and v = C − A: since |u − v|² = |u|² + |v|² − 2u·v, the hypothesis says that u·v = 0, so BA and AC are at right angles. That is not Euclid's route: he builds a right-angled triangle and compares the given one with it by I.8.
