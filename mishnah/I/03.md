**Where the circle meets AB.** Euclid draws the circle DEF about A and simply calls E the point where it meets AB. Why should it meet AB at all, and between A and B? Because A, the centre, is inside the circle, and B, being farther from A than the radius, is outside, and a straight line going from inside a circle to outside crosses it (System E, Intersections 4). Lean has to say so, and needs the straight line AB itself in order to say it, so the proof uses Postulate 1 although Euclid doesn't cite it (it is on the certificate through I.2 as well).

**Heath on cases.** Proclus again finds many cases, eight figures in all. Heath remarks that Proclus gets them only "by practically incorporating the construction of the preceding proposition, instead of assuming it as we are entitled to do" (vol. 1, p. 247). In Lean, citing I.2 is exactly assuming it: `Prop2` is used as a finished result, whatever its own figure looked like. Heath also sets aside Potts' two cases, cutting from one end of AB or the other, and Lean agrees: when I.6 cuts from the end B, it simply cites `Prop3` with the letters exchanged.

**What I.3 gives.** Together, I.2 and I.3 finish the work De Morgan describes under I.2: a compass that closes can now do all that an ordinary one does. Our `Prop3` also says where the cut falls, with E between A and B, since that is what cutting off from AB means, and I.5 and I.6 rely on it. Euclid names the less straight line by a single letter, C; Lean, which knows a finite straight line only by its two ends, gives C a pair of them.

**The modern way.** E is the point a known fraction of the way along AB: E = A + (|C| / |AB|)(B − A), where |C| is the length of C.
