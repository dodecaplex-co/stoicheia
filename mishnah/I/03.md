**The compass restored.** Euclid now puts I.2 to work. To cut off from AB a straight line equal to C, he places AD equal to C at the point A, by I.2, and lets the circle about A through D do the cutting. With that, the compasses that close of themselves, which Augustus De Morgan pictures under I.2, can do all that an ordinary pair does: carry a length and lay it off along a straight line.

**Eight figures, or one?** Proclus again finds many cases, eight figures in all. Heath remarks that he gets them only "by practically incorporating the construction of the preceding proposition, instead of assuming it as we are entitled to do. If Prop. 2 is assumed, there is really only one 'case' of the present proposition" (vol. 1, p. 247). In Lean, citing I.2 is exactly assuming it: we use `Prop2` as a finished result, whatever its own figure looked like. Heath also sets aside Robert Potts' two cases, cutting from one end of AB or the other, and so do our proofs: when I.6 cuts from the end B, it simply cites `Prop3` with the letters exchanged.

**Why should the circle cut AB?** Euclid simply takes E where the circle DEF meets AB. Why should it meet AB at all, and between A and B? The centre A is inside the circle and B, farther from A than the radius, is outside; a straight line from inside a circle to outside crosses it (System E, Intersections 4). Our proof says so, and first names the straight line AB, as in I.1. Our `Prop3` keeps E between A and B, as cutting off from AB means, for I.5 and I.6 rely on it. Euclid names the less straight line by one letter, C; our representation, which knows a finite straight line only by its two ends, gives C a pair of them.

**The modern way.** E is the point a known fraction of the way along AB: E = A + (|C| / |AB|)(B − A), where |C| is the length of C.
