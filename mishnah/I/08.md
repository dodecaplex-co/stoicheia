Heath calls I.8 "a *partial* converse of I. 4" (vol. 1, p. 262): the equal bases, which I.4 proves, are given here, and the angle is proved instead. Euclid goes no further than the angles at the vertices A and D, because, as Heath explains after Proclus and Henry Savile, "when once the vertical angles are proved equal, the rest follows from I. 4, and there is no object in proving again what has been proved already" (vol. 1, p. 262).

For the second time Euclid picks up a triangle and lays it on another. Our rule [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean) permits this as a hypothetical placement in System E's style, within a checked derivation. We suppose ABC laid down with B on E, C on the ray from E through F, and A on D's side of EF, keeping every side and angle. Euclid never says which side A comes down on, but I.7 needs to know, since D and its mirror image in EF are each as far from E and from F as the other; we read "applied" as putting A on D's side. C lands on F "because BC is equal to EF", and because BC was laid along EF, as in I.4. Then Euclid's reduction to the impossible, in which BA, AC "fall beside" ED, DF, becomes a citation of `Prop7` inside the placement: G, the hypothetical place where A comes down, is D. The Greek verb, Heath notes, means "to pass by without touching," "to miss" or "to deviate" (vol. 1, p. 262).

I.7's statement asserts no existence, though its earlier proof draws straight lines, produces them and uses circles through I.5 and I.3. Our reading allows that already proved statement to be cited inside the placement; the citation itself introduces no object. No step of the represented derivation constructs anything, and its hypothetical corners cannot be returned as constructed points. What leaves the placement is the equality of the angles BAC and EDF, naming only the given figure. Only I.4 and I.8 may use the rule directly. The former rule returned placed points; restricting its use to those two propositions left it stronger as a construction principle than System E's inference rule.

I.8 may once have said more. Aristotle, speaking of the rainbow, concludes that "*the triangles are equal*", and J. L. Heiberg took this for a trace of an older I.8: "we see here therefore, in a clear example, how the stones of the ancient fabric were recut for the rigid structure of his *Elements*" (Heath, vol. 1, pp. 262–263). Heath doubts the inference is safe. Proclus also preserves a proof by Philo that lays ABC on the far side of EF and uses I.5 in place of I.7, and Heath finds that "it is elegant; but it is inconvenient in one respect, since three cases have to be distinguished" (vol. 1, p. 263). David Hilbert, who made side, angle, side an axiom, proves side, side, side from it with no moving of figures.
