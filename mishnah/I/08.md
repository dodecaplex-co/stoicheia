Heath calls I.8 "a *partial* converse of I. 4" (vol. 1, p. 262): the equal bases, which I.4 proves, are given here, and the angle is proved instead. Euclid goes no further than the angles at the vertices A and D, because, as Heath explains after Proclus and Henry Savile, "when once the vertical angles are proved equal, the rest follows from I. 4, and there is no object in proving again what has been proved already" (vol. 1, p. 262).

For the second time Euclid picks up a triangle and lays it on another. Our rule [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean) returns newly placed points: a copy of ABC with B on E, C on the ray from E through F, and A on the chosen side of EF. We permit its direct use only in I.4 and I.8. That policy limits its use; as a construction principle it is stronger than System E's restricted inference rule. Euclid never says which side A comes down on, but I.7 needs to know, since D and its mirror image in EF are each as far from E and from F as the other; so we ask for D's side, which is what "applied" means. C lands on F "because BC is equal to EF", and because BC was laid along EF, as in I.4. Then Euclid's reduction to the impossible, in which BA, AC "fall beside" ED, DF, becomes one citation of `Prop7`: G, where A comes down, is D. The Greek verb, Heath notes, means "to pass by without touching," "to miss" or "to deviate" (vol. 1, p. 262).

I.8 may once have said more. Aristotle, speaking of the rainbow, concludes that "*the triangles are equal*", and J. L. Heiberg took this for a trace of an older I.8: "we see here therefore, in a clear example, how the stones of the ancient fabric were recut for the rigid structure of his *Elements*" (Heath, vol. 1, pp. 262–263). Heath doubts the inference is safe. Proclus also preserves a proof by Philo that lays ABC on the far side of EF and uses I.5 in place of I.7, and Heath finds that "it is elegant; but it is inconvenient in one respect, since three cases have to be distinguished" (vol. 1, p. 263). David Hilbert, who made side, angle, side an axiom, proves side, side, side from it with no moving of figures.
