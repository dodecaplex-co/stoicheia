**Picking up a triangle.** As in I.4, Euclid lifts the triangle ABC and lays it on DEF, something none of his postulates allows. Here that is one named rule, [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean), in the form LeanEuclid gives it, which folds System E's two superposition rules into one. Nothing actually moves: the rule hands us a fresh copy of ABC, with B on E, C somewhere on the ray from E through F, and A on whichever side of EF we ask for, every side and angle kept. Avigad, Dean and Mumma allow superposition only for proving facts about a figure already drawn, never for building new ones, and to respect that, the certificate's checks let only I.4 and I.8 cite it.

**"The point C will also coincide with F."** Euclid's reason, "because BC is equal to EF", is only half of it. The other half is the ray: C lands on F's side of E, not beyond E the other way, and that is what "the straight line BC on EF" is there to secure. `OnRay.eq_of_len_eq` says it out loud. On the straight line EF, on F's side of E, the one point as far from E as F is, is F. Any other would make EF equal to EF together with something, and the whole is greater than the part.

**Which side A comes down on.** Euclid never says that A lands on the same side of EF as D, but I.7 needs exactly that. On opposite sides, two points can be at the same distances from E and F without being the same point: think of D and its mirror image in EF. So we ask the superposition rule for D's side, which is what "applied to the triangle DEF" means. `SameSideOf.of_line` then checks that "the same side of EF" doesn't depend on which straight line through E and F we name, since two points fix one straight line (System E, Generalities 1). As in I.4, Lean needs that straight line before it can lay anything along it, so Postulate 1 is on the certificate although Euclid doesn't cite it.

**Falling beside.** Euclid supposes BA, AC "do not coincide with ED, DF but fall beside them as EG, GF". Heath notes that the Greek word means "to pass by without touching," "to miss" or "to deviate" (vol. 1, p. 262). Our I.7 is stated positively, so that two such points are one and the same point, and Euclid's reduction to the impossible becomes a single citation of `Prop7`: G is D.

**Why only the angle.** Heath: "It is to be observed that in I. 8 Euclid is satisfied with proving the equality of the vertical angles and does not, as in I. 4, add that the triangles are equal, and the remaining angles are equal respectively. The reason is no doubt (as pointed out by Proclus and by Savile after him) that, when once the vertical angles are proved equal, the rest follows from I. 4, and there is no object in proving again what has been proved already" (vol. 1, p. 262).

**Other roads.** Proclus preserves a proof by Philo that lays ABC on the far side of EF and uses I.5 in place of I.7. Heath: "This alternative proof avoids the use of I. 7, and it is elegant; but it is inconvenient in one respect, since three cases have to be distinguished" (vol. 1, p. 263). He prizes I.7 for its own sake, quoting Charles Dodgson: "of all plane figures that can be made by hingeing rods together, the three-sided ones (and these only) are rigid" (vol. 1, p. 261). Hilbert, who made side–angle–side an axiom, proves side–side–side from it as a theorem, with no moving of figures at all, and Beeson, Narboux and Wiedijk, checking Book I from axioms in the style of Tarski's, had no superposition to call on either. And Heath finds an allusion to the theorem in Aristotle, who, "speaking of the rainbow", observes "that, if equal rays be reflected from one and the same point to one and the same point, the points at which reflection takes place are on the circumference of a circle" (vol. 1, p. 262).

**The modern way.** With vectors, |B − C|² = |A − B|² + |A − C|² − 2 (B − A)·(C − A), so the three sides fix the dot product, and with it the angle at A: this is the law of cosines, cos A = (b² + c² − a²) / 2bc. Superposition has a modern name too: a rigid motion of the plane, and any two triangles with the same three sides are carried one onto the other, corner to corner, by exactly one of them.
