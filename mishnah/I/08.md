**"Applied to the triangle DEF."** For the second time Euclid picks up a triangle and lays it on another. Heath calls I.8 "a *partial* converse of I. 4" (vol. 1, p. 262): the equal bases, which I.4 proves, are given here, and the angle is proved instead. As in I.4, the moving is our rule [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean). It hands us a copy of ABC with B on E, C on the ray from E through F, and A on whichever side of EF we ask for.

**Which side does A come down on?** Euclid never says, but I.7 needs D's side, since D and its mirror image in EF are each as far from E and from F as the other. So we ask for D's side, which is what "applied" means. C lands on F "because BC is equal to EF", and because BC was laid along EF, as in I.4. Euclid's reduction to the impossible, in which BA, AC "fall beside" ED, DF, becomes one citation of `Prop7`: G, where A comes down, is D. The Greek verb, Heath notes, means "to pass by without touching," "to miss" or "to deviate" (vol. 1, p. 262).

**Why stop at the angle?** Euclid proves the angles at the vertices A and D equal and goes no further, because, as Proclus and Henry Savile pointed out, "when once the vertical angles are proved equal, the rest follows from I. 4, and there is no object in proving again what has been proved already" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 262). Aristotle, speaking of the rainbow, concludes that "*the triangles are equal*", and J. L. Heiberg took this for a trace of an older I.8: "we see here therefore, in a clear example, how the stones of the ancient fabric were recut for the rigid structure of his *Elements*" (Heath, vol. 1, pp. 262–263). Heath doubts the inference is safe.

**Philo's three cases.** Proclus preserves a proof by Philo that lays ABC on the far side of EF and uses I.5 in place of I.7. Heath's verdict: "it is elegant; but it is inconvenient in one respect, since three cases have to be distinguished" (vol. 1, p. 263). David Hilbert, who made side, angle, side an axiom, proves side, side, side from it with no moving of figures.

**The modern way.** With vectors, |B − C|² = |A − B|² + |A − C|² − 2 (B − A)·(C − A), so the three sides fix the dot product, and with it the angle at A: the law of cosines. And exactly one rigid motion of the plane, turning it over if need be, carries ABC onto DEF, corner to corner.
