**Superposition again.** As in I.4, Euclid lifts the triangle ABC and lays it on DEF, and here too that is the named rule [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean), which only I.4 and I.8 may cite. It hands us a fresh copy of ABC, with B on E, C on the ray from E through F, and A on whichever side of EF we ask for, every side and angle kept.

**"The point C will also coincide with F."** Euclid's reason, "because BC is equal to EF", is only half of it. The other half is the ray, which "the straight line BC on EF" is there to secure: on the ray from E through F, the one point as far from E as F is, is F (`OnRay.eq_of_len_eq`). Any other would make EF equal to EF together with something, and the whole is greater than the part.

**Which side A comes down on.** Euclid never says that A lands on the same side of EF as D, but I.7 needs exactly that, since D and its mirror image in EF, on opposite sides of it, are each as far from E and from F as the other. So we ask the rule for D's side, which is what "applied to the triangle DEF" means. As in I.4, Lean needs the straight line EF before it can lay anything along it, so the proof uses Postulate 1 (I.7 brings it in as well). Euclid's reduction to the impossible, in which BA, AC "fall beside" ED, DF, then becomes one citation of `Prop7`: G is D.

**Why only the angle.** Euclid proves the angles at A equal and stops, because, as Proclus and Savile pointed out, "when once the vertical angles are proved equal, the rest follows from I. 4, and there is no object in proving again what has been proved already" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 262). Proclus also preserves a proof by Philo that lays ABC on the far side of EF and uses I.5 in place of I.7: "it is elegant; but it is inconvenient in one respect, since three cases have to be distinguished" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 263). David Hilbert, who made side–angle–side an axiom, proves side–side–side from it with no moving of figures at all.

**The modern way.** With vectors, |B − C|² = |A − B|² + |A − C|² − 2 (B − A)·(C − A), so the three sides fix the dot product, and with it the angle at A: the law of cosines. Superposition's modern name is a rigid motion of the plane, turning it over if need be, and exactly one carries ABC onto DEF, corner to corner.
