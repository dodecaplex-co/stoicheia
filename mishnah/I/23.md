**Oenopides' angle.** This is the problem of copying an angle, and Heath traces it back past Euclid: "This problem was, according to Eudemus (see Proclus, p. 333, 5), 'rather the discovery of Oenopides,' from which we must apparently infer, not that Oenopides was the first to find any solution of it, but that it was he who discovered the particular solution given by Euclid" (vol. 1, p. 295). It is the same Oenopides of Chios who, Proclus says, first investigated the perpendicular of I.12. His solution is a small marvel of economy: an angle is hard to carry about, but a triangle is only three lengths, and I.22 can rebuild a triangle from its lengths anywhere we like. Carry the triangle DCE across, and I.8 brings the angle with it.

**"Not exactly the construction given in I. 22."** Heath noticed that Euclid asks more of I.22 here than I.22 gives: "The editors do not seem to have noticed the fact that the construction of the triangle assumed in this proposition is not exactly the construction given in I. 22. We have here to construct a triangle on a certain finite straight line AG as base; in I. 22 we have only to construct a triangle with sides of given length without any restriction as to how it is to be placed." In I.22 the triangle stands wherever its construction happens to put it; "Here the base is a given straight line abutting at a given point. Thus the construction has to be modified somewhat from that of the preceding proposition." He shows how: measure AG along AB, and GH along GB beyond it, and "produce BA, in the opposite direction," to F for the third length (vol. 1, p. 295). He suspects Proclus felt the same difficulty, which would explain why Proclus gives the construction over again, with the remark that "you may obtain the construction of the triangle in a more instructive manner as follows" (vol. 1, p. 295). Lean would not have let the gap pass, and our I.22 is stated to close it: its triangle has a corner at a given point, a side along a given ray from that point, and its third corner on the side of a given point, and its proof is to set out Euclid's straight line through the given point, just as Heath advises. With that statement in hand, I.23 goes through as Euclid writes it.

**What Lean made us say.** Very little, and none of it new. I.22 asks first that two of its three straight lines taken together in any manner be greater than the remaining one; Euclid does not repeat it here, but the three are the sides of the triangle DCE, so I.20 says it, once each sum is turned round into I.22's order (`add_comm`, `len_symm`). The bracket in I.22's own enunciation points to I.20 for exactly this. Then I.8 wants two triangles, so we must check that AFG is one: F lies on the straight line AB and is not A, and G, being on one side of AB, is off it (`OnRay.onLine`, `SameSideOf.triangle`, `Triangle.off_line` and `Triangle.of_line` in Book Zero). That is the only reason Lean names the straight line AB at all. No helper theorems were needed, and no new rule: the proof is I.20, I.22 and I.8, in Euclid's order. As with I.10, it does not wait for those proofs to be finished, since a proof needs only the statements it cites.

**Points taken at random.** "On the straight lines CD, CE respectively let the points D, E be taken at random." In Lean the given angle comes as the triangle DCE, three points not on one straight line, so D and E are there from the start and we take those. That the choice does not matter is a fact Euclid uses without a word: an angle depends only on its two rays, so any other points on CD and CE would name the same angle (`angle_eq_of_onRay` in Book Zero, from System E's Diagram-angle 4). That the given angle is a triangle's angle is something Euclid takes for granted too. His plane angle is the inclination of two lines "which meet one another and do not lie in a straight line" (Definition 8), and a flat angle could not be copied this way in any case, since there would be no triangle to carry.

**Which side, and which way.** "On the given straight line AB, and at the point A on it" leaves two choices open: the angle may open along the ray from A through B or the ray away from B, and above AB or below. Euclid's figure settles both, and later propositions depend on the settlement. I.24 builds its angle EDG on F's side of DE, and I.31 and I.42 need their angles on a named side as well. So our statement puts F on the ray from A through B and G on the side of AB where a given point P lies; I.22 already places its triangle that way, so I.23 inherits the choice for nothing. Our figure follows the proof, and so its letters F and G trade places with Heath's, whose figure stands G on AB and F above it. The angle FAG is the same either way, and the triangle AFG in our figure is DCE turned over, which I.8 does not mind.

**Apollonius' short cut.** Proclus reports a quicker construction by Apollonius: an arc about the vertex of the given angle, another about A, and on the second an arc cut off equal to the arc on the first, the angles "standing on equal circumferences" then being equal. Heath, with Proclus, finds fault: the two circles should have been made equal, and "the use of Book III. for such an elementary construction is objectionable." But he is kind to Apollonius: "It seems to me probable that his construction was only given from the point of view of *practical*, not theoretical, geometry. It really comes to the same thing as Euclid's except that DC is taken equal to DE" (in Proclus' letters the given angle is CDE, with its vertex at D). To cut off an equal arc is in practice to measure the chord with a pair of compasses, so his direction "was therefore probably intended as a practical short cut, avoiding the actual drawing of the chords," which, with a proof that the two triangles are equal in all respects, "would be required to establish *theoretically* the correctness of the construction" (vol. 1, p. 296). It is the way an angle is copied with ruler and compass today.

**The modern way.** In the complex plane, multiplying by a number w turns every direction through the angle arg w. So the point A + (B − A)(E − C)/(D − C) makes with AB, at A, an angle equal to DCE, and its mirror image in the straight line AB makes the same angle on the other side; which of the two lies on P's side is a matter of one sign.
