**Oenopides' angle.** An angle is hard to carry about, but a triangle is only three lengths, which I.22 can set down anywhere: so Euclid copies the triangle DCE at A, and I.8 brings the angle with it. Heath: "This problem was, according to Eudemus (see Proclus, p. 333, 5), 'rather the discovery of Oenopides,' from which we must apparently infer, not that Oenopides was the first to find any solution of it, but that it was he who discovered the particular solution given by Euclid" (vol. 1, p. 295).

**"Not exactly the construction given in I. 22."** The editors, Heath notes, seem to have missed it: "We have here to construct a triangle on a certain finite straight line AG as base; in I. 22 we have only to construct a triangle with sides of given length without any restriction as to how it is to be placed" (vol. 1, p. 295). He suspects Proclus felt it too, and so gave the construction over again "in a more instructive manner" (vol. 1, p. 295). Our I.22 is stated to close the gap.

**Apollonius' short cut.** Proclus objects to Apollonius' way, which leans on Book III: circles of one radius about the vertex and about A, equal circumferences cut off on them, and the angles equal as standing on equal circumferences. As under I.11, Heath reads him kindly: "It seems to me probable that his construction was only given from the point of view of *practical*, not theoretical, geometry" (vol. 1, p. 296). Done with compasses, by the chord, it is Euclid's construction with an isosceles triangle: the way an angle is copied to this day.

**Which side, and which way?** Euclid's "on the given straight line AB" leaves open which way along it the angle opens, and on which side, and I.24, I.31 and I.42 will care. So our statement settles both: one arm runs along the ray from A through B, and the other lies on the side of AB where a given point P lies. D and E, "taken at random", may be any points of the arms, since an angle depends only on its rays. Nor does Euclid check I.22's condition: the three lengths are the sides of the triangle DCE, and I.20 grants it.

**The modern way.** In the complex plane, multiplying by w turns every direction through the angle arg w. So the point A + (B − A)(E − C)/(D − C) makes with AB, at A, an angle equal to DCE, and its mirror image in the straight line AB makes the same angle on the other side; which of the two lies on P's side is a matter of one sign.
