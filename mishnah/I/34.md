**Parallelogrammic areas.** Heath observes that "when parallelograms have to be mentioned for the first time, Euclid calls them 'parallelogrammic areas'" (vol. 1, p. 325). He reports Proclus' account that Euclid introduced the word, suggested by I.33. Euclid gives no definition of a parallelogram; our `Parallelogram` is a phrase saying that both pairs of opposite sides are parallel. ACDB names the corners in order around the figure.

**Two triangles, then their areas.** BC falls on each pair of parallel sides. Twice I.29 gives equal alternate angles; I.26, with BC common, gives the remaining sides and angle. Common Notion 2 adds the angles at B and C to give the other pair of equal opposite angles. Euclid then returns to I.4 for equality of the triangles' areas, which I.26 does not assert. Our I.26 takes ABC and DCB in Euclid's stated order, preserving the correspondence of their corners, as Heath notes under I.33.

**What Lean made us say.** `Parallelogram.sameSideOf` puts the far corners on one side of each side; `AreParallel.oppositeSidesOf` then puts A and D on opposite sides of BC. Naming the parallelogram from each corner also places BC within the angles ABD and ACD, so `angle_add_of_within` justifies their addition. The conclusion says that the triangles ABC and DCB have equal areas; later proofs use `Prop34.bisect` to say that the whole is twice either half. The certificate cites I.4, I.26 and I.29, exactly Euclid's brackets, and records Common Notion 2. `Prop29.alternate` uses Postulates 1 and 2 to set out I.29's figure; Postulate 5 enters through I.29.

**What a diameter bisects.** Proclus distinguishes the two parts by their generality: circles and ellipses also have their areas bisected by a diameter. He recalls how "the ancients, after investigating the fact that the diameter bisects an ellipse, a circle, and a parallelogram respectively, proceeded to investigate what was common to these cases" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 325). Heath doubts the distinction, since both parts concern quadrilaterals. Proclus also distinguishes squares and rhombi, whose diameters bisect the angles, from other parallelograms (p. 325). An-Nairīzī uses I.33 and I.34 to divide a straight line into equal parts (p. 326).

**The modern way.** In vectors, D = B + C − A. The half-turn X ↦ B + C − X about the midpoint of BC exchanges B and C and takes A to D. It carries the triangle ABC onto DCB, preserving lengths, angles and areas; adding the two corresponding angles at B and C gives the remaining opposite angles.
