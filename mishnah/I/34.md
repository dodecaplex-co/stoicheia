**Parallelogrammic areas.** Heath observes that "when parallelograms have to be mentioned for the first time, Euclid calls them 'parallelogrammic areas'" (vol. 1, p. 325), and gathers from Proclus that the word was Euclid's own, suggested by I.33. Euclid never defines the figure. Our `Parallelogram` says that both pairs of opposite sides are parallel, the corners taken in order round the figure: here A, C, D, B.

**Two triangles, then their areas.** The diameter BC falls on each pair of parallel sides, and I.29 makes two pairs of alternate angles equal. With BC common, I.26 gives the remaining sides and the remaining angle, and Common Notion 2 adds the angles at B and at C into the other pair of opposite angles. For the areas Euclid goes back to I.4, since I.26 says nothing of them. Heath's DCB, for the Greek's BCD, keeps the corners in order, as he explains under I.33, and our I.26 takes them so.

**What Lean made us say.** `Parallelogram.sameSideOf` puts the far corners on one side of each side, and from that `AreParallel.oppositeSidesOf` puts A and D on opposite sides of BC, so that the angles at B and C are alternate. Named from each corner in turn, the parallelogram also puts BC within the angles ABD and ACD, and `angle_add_of_within` adds the angles there. The conclusion speaks of the triangles ABC and DCB; later proofs cite `Prop34.bisect`, which makes the parallelogram twice either half. The Lean cites just Euclid's three brackets. Postulates 1 and 2 come in where `Prop29.alternate` sets out I.29's figure; I.26 and I.29 bring in both as well, I.4 Postulate 1, and I.29 Postulate 5.

**What a diameter bisects.** Proclus finds the first part of the enunciation general, true of parallelograms alone, and the second not, for a diameter bisects circles and ellipses too: "the ancients, after investigating the fact that the diameter bisects an ellipse, a circle, and a parallelogram respectively, proceeded to investigate what was common to these cases" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 325). Heath doubts it: Proclus must take the figure to be four-sided in the first part, since some regular polygons have their opposite sides and angles equal too, and then he may in the second. Heath places here an-Nairīzī's way of dividing a straight line into equal parts, which uses I.33 and I.34 (p. 326).

**The modern way.** As vectors, D = B + C − A, and the half-turn X ↦ B + C − X about the middle of BC exchanges B and C and takes A to D. It carries the triangle ABC onto DCB and the angle ABD onto DCA, keeping every length, angle and area: the whole of I.34 at once.
