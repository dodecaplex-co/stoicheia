**"Parallelogrammic areas."** Euclid now names the figure I.33 built, and never defines it. Heath observes that "when parallelograms have to be mentioned for the first time, Euclid calls them 'parallelogrammic areas'", and gathers from Proclus that "parallelogram", in Greek "parallel-lined", was Euclid's own word, suggested by I.33 and formed like "rectilineal" (vol. 1, p. 325). Our `Parallelogram` says just that: both pairs of opposite sides are parallel, the corners taken in order round the figure, here A, C, D, B.

**"And BC its diameter."** The diameter, Euclid's word for a diagonal, falls on both pairs of parallels, and I.29 makes two pairs of alternate angles equal. With BC common, I.26 gives the remaining sides and angle. Common Notion 2 adds the angles at B and at C, once Lean knows that BC runs within the angles ABD and ACD, as the parallelogram, named from each corner, says (`angle_add_of_within`). I.26 says nothing of areas, so Euclid goes back to I.4. Later proofs want the halving as an area, so we add `Prop34.bisect`: the parallelogram is twice either triangle.

**Which part is general?** Proclus, after Aristotle, calls the first part general, since only parallelograms have their opposite sides and angles equal, and the second not, since a diameter bisects circles and ellipses too: "the ancients, after investigating the fact that the diameter bisects an ellipse, a circle, and a parallelogram respectively, proceeded to investigate what was common to these cases" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 325). Heath doubts it. Proclus himself admits that the proposition presumes a four-sided figure, and "if we are entitled to the tacit limitation of the theorem to quadrilaterals in one part, we are equally entitled to it in the other" (vol. 1, p. 325).

**A diameter in the widest sense.** Heath suspects Proclus was alluding to Greek work on the diameters of all sorts of figures, which may have led to Apollonius' definition: a diameter of a bent line bisects all its chords parallel to some straight line. A parallelogram's diameter is one: "either diagonal of a parallelogram bisects all lines drawn within the parallelogram parallel to the other diagonal" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 326). Here Heath places an-Nairīzī's "neat construction for dividing a straight line into any number of equal parts", which uses I.33 and I.34 (vol. 1, p. 326).

**The modern way.** As vectors, D = B + C − A, and the half-turn X ↦ B + C − X about the middle of BC exchanges B and C and takes A to D. It carries the triangle ABC onto DCB and the angle ABD onto DCA, keeping every length, angle and area: the whole of I.34 at once.
