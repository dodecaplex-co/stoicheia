**Any two.** "Taken together in any manner" is, Heath notes, "πάντῃ μεταλαμβανόμεναι, i.e. any pair added together" (vol. 1, p. 282). A triangle has three pairs of angles, so the Lean statement says three things, in Euclid's own letters: the angles ABC, BCA; the angles BAC, ACB; and the angles CAB, ABC. Euclid argues for the first pair and says "similarly" for the other two. As in I.6 and I.16, the Lean gives his argument once, for every triangle, and uses it three times: in Euclid's figure, in the triangle BAC with AC produced, and in the triangle CAB with AB produced.

**Equals added to unequals.** "Let the angle ACB be added to each" carries no citation, and none of the Common Notions fits, since the second adds equals to equals. The manuscripts and editions carried another, "If equals be added to unequals, the wholes are unequal", which Heiberg prints in brackets and Heath rejects: "(a) stands self-condemned because 'unequal' tells us nothing. It is easy to see what is wanted if we refer to I. 17, where the same angle is added to a greater and a less, and it is inferred that the first sum is greater than the second" (vol. 1, p. 223). What is wanted is System E's magnitude law `add_lt_add_right` (§3.5): adding the same thing to both sides keeps "less than". It is one of the tacit laws from which our Common Notions 3 and 5 are themselves proved, and here it is cited on its own, at the sentence where Euclid adds.

**What else Lean made us say.** Very little, and no helper theorems: Book Zero already says everything Euclid reads off this figure. I.16 hands back the interior angle as CBA, where Euclid says ABC; the angle ACB is added, and is called BCA in the sum; and I.13 gives the angles BCA, ACD, where Euclid writes ACD, ACB. Each time it is one angle read the other way round (`angle_symm`, System E's Metric 4) or two angles added in the other order (`add_comm`). I.13 also wants AC to stand on BD with A off it. D is on BC produced, so BD is the straight line BC, and A is off that because ABC is a triangle (`Triangle.off_line`, `Triangle.of_line`). To say so, Lean names the straight line BC (Post. 1) before Postulate 2 produces it. Every rule here is from the System E of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma.

**Without producing a side.** Proclus would not have the exterior angle as the cause of the property, Heath reports, "for the odd reason that 'it is not necessary that a side should be produced, or that there should be any exterior angle constructed... and how can what is not necessary be the cause of what is necessary?'" So "Proclus then sets himself to prove the theorem without producing a side of the triangle" (vol. 1, p. 282). He takes any point D on BC and joins AD. The exterior angle ADC of the triangle ABD is greater than the angle ABD, the exterior angle ADB of the triangle ADC is greater than the angle ACD, and the angles ADB, ADC together are two right angles. The proof may be Heron's. Heron, Heath tells us, sometimes "avoids producing a particular straight line, where Euclid produces it, in order to meet the objection of any one who should deny our right to assume that there is any space available"; and van Pesch, finding notes in Proclus aimed at the same sort of point, attributes to Heron "alternative proofs of I. 5, I. 17, and I. 32, which avoid the producing of certain straight lines" (vol. 1, p. 23). In Lean, Proclus' route would take its point by System E's rule that a point may be taken between two points (Points 3) instead of by Postulate 2, and cite I.16 twice instead of once.

**One perpendicular.** On I.12, Heath wrote that "for a proof that in Euclidean space there is only one perpendicular from a point to a straight line, we have to wait until I. 16" (vol. 1, p. 273). One proposition later, Proclus gives it: "Lastly, Proclus proves (what is obvious from this proposition) that there cannot be more than one perpendicular to a straight line from a point without it. For, if this were possible, two of such perpendiculars would form a triangle in which two angles would be right angles: which is impossible, since any two angles of a triangle are together less than two right angles" (vol. 1, p. 282). In Lean it would be a line or two from our statement: two right angles make ∟ + ∟, and nothing is less than itself (`lt_irrefl`).

**The converse is a postulate.** Two straight lines that meet a third and make a triangle with it make the interior angles on that side less than two right angles; Postulate 5 says the converse. Proclus used this against the postulate: "This ought even to be struck out of the Postulates altogether; for it is a theorem involving many difficulties … And the converse of it is actually proved by Euclid himself as a theorem" (vol. 1, p. 202). On I.17 itself he finds the cause of the property in "the convergence of the straight lines towards one another …, the lessening of the two right angles, which produces the triangle" (vol. 1, p. 282). The Lean puts the two side by side. Postulate 5 assumes `∠ A B C + ∠ B C D < ∟ + ∟` and concludes that the straight lines meet; I.17 starts from a triangle and concludes `∠ A B C + ∠ B C A < ∟ + ∟`. I.17 rests nowhere on Postulate 5 (CI checks that), and it holds as well in the geometry of János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky. Under Heath's "Riemann hypothesis" it fails: a triangle with one corner at the pole of a straight line and the other two on that line has two right angles.

**The modern way.** Put B at 0 and C at 1 in the complex plane, and A at a, above them. The angle ABC is arg a, the exterior angle ACD is arg(a − 1), and the angle BCA is π − arg(a − 1). Since a − 1 lies straight to the left of a, its argument is the larger, so ABC + BCA = π − (arg(a − 1) − arg a) < π. The shortfall arg(a − 1) − arg a is the angle BAC, and saying so is I.32, which needs the parallel postulate.
