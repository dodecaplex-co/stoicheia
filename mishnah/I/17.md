**Any two.** "Taken together in any manner" is, Heath notes, "πάντῃ μεταλαμβανόμεναι, i.e. any pair added together" (vol. 1, p. 282). So the Lean statement says three things, in Euclid's letters: the angles ABC, BCA; the angles BAC, ACB; and the angles CAB, ABC. Euclid argues for the first pair and says "similarly" for the others. As in I.16, the Lean gives his argument once, for every triangle, and uses it three times, producing BC, AC and AB in turn.

**Equals added to unequals.** "Let the angle ACB be added to each" carries no citation, and no Common Notion fits: the second adds equals to equals. The manuscripts and editions add others, among them "If equals be added to unequals, the wholes are unequal", which Heiberg prints in brackets and Heath rejects: "(a) stands self-condemned because 'unequal' tells us nothing. It is easy to see what is wanted if we refer to I. 17, where the same angle is added to a *greater* and a *less*, and it is inferred that the first sum is greater than the second" (vol. 1, p. 223). What is wanted is the tacit law of magnitudes `add_lt_add_right` (System E §3.5): adding the same thing to both sides keeps "less than". Our Common Notions 3 and 5 are proved from it, and here it is cited on its own.

**The converse is a postulate.** Postulate 5 is the converse of I.17, and Proclus held that against it: "This ought even to be struck out of the Postulates altogether; for it is a theorem involving many difficulties … And the converse of it is actually proved by Euclid himself as a theorem" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 202). In Lean, Postulate 5 assumes `∠ A B C + ∠ B C D < ∟ + ∟` and concludes that the straight lines meet, while I.17 starts from a triangle and concludes `∠ A B C + ∠ B C A < ∟ + ∟`. I.17 needs no Postulate 5 and holds in the geometry of János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky too, but not in Bernhard Riemann's (see I.16), where a triangle with a corner at the pole of a straight line and the other two on it has two right angles.

**The modern way.** Put B at 0, C at 1 and A at a, above them, in the complex plane: the angle ABC is arg a, and the angle BCA is π − arg(a − 1). Since a − 1 lies straight to the left of a, its argument is the larger, so the two angles together fall short of π by arg(a − 1) − arg a. That shortfall is the angle BAC, but saying so is I.32, which needs the parallel postulate.
