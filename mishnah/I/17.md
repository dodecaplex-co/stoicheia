Behind Euclid's "taken together in any manner", Heath notes, is the Greek "πάντῃ μεταλαμβανόμεναι, i.e. any pair added together" (vol. 1, p. 282). So our statement names all three pairs, in Euclid's letters. Euclid argues for the first pair and says "similarly" for the others, so we prove his case for any triangle and call on it three times, producing BC, AC and AB in turn. Proclus draws from I.17 at once that there cannot be two perpendiculars from one point to a straight line: they would make a triangle with two right angles (Heath, vol. 1, p. 282).

Heath gives no reference for "Let the angle ACB be added to each", and no Common Notion fits: Common Notion 2 adds equals to equals, not to unequals. The manuscripts and editions add "*If equals be added to unequals, the wholes are unequal*", which J. L. Heiberg prints in brackets and Heath rejects: "(a) stands self-condemned because 'unequal' tells us nothing. It is easy to see what is wanted if we refer to I. 17, where the same angle is added to a *greater* and a *less*, and it is inferred that the first sum is greater than the second" (vol. 1, p. 223). Our proof cites instead the tacit rule from which our Common Notions 3 and 5 are proved, `add_lt_add_right` (System E §3.5): adding the same thing to both sides keeps "less than".

Postulate 5 says that straight lines making angles less than two right angles with a third will meet; I.17, that straight lines which meet make such angles. Proclus held this against the postulate: "This ought even to be struck out of the Postulates altogether; for it is a theorem involving many difficulties … And the converse of it is actually proved by Euclid himself as a theorem" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 202). But I.17 is proved without Postulate 5, and holds in the geometry of János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky, where the postulate fails. In Bernhard Riemann's (see I.16) even I.17 fails: a triangle with a corner at the pole of a straight line and the other two on it has two right angles.
