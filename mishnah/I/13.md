**Two right angles, or equal to two?** Two right angles enter the propositions here, and stay: I.14, I.15 and I.32 all turn on this one. Euclid offers a choice, "either two right angles or angles equal to two right angles"; the first is Definition I.10's case, where the two angles are equal and so each is right. Since every right angle in Lean is the one `∟`, as in I.11, our statement keeps only the second form, and Euclid's first case needs a step he leaves unsaid: Postulate 4.

**Which way does AB lean?** Euclid draws AB leaning towards C, with BA within the right angle CBE and BE within the angle DBA, and both his sums of angles read this off the figure; we prove each. AB cannot lie along BE, or the angles CBA, ABD would be the right angles CBE, EBD over again, and equal; so it leans one way or the other. Leaning towards D, it gives his figure with C and D trading places, and his one argument serves for both. BE falls within DBA because BA has C on one side and both E and D on the other; and our I.11 drew BE on A's side of CD.

**"Not common before it is added."** Euclid's "let the angle EBD be added to each" is, Heath explains, "literally 'let the angle EBD be added (so as to be) common,' κοινὴ προσκείσθω ἡ ὑπὸ ΕΒΔ", and "'Let the common angle EBD be added' is clearly an inaccurate translation, for the angle is not common before it is added, i.e. the κοινή is proleptic" (vol. 1, p. 276): it calls the angle common before it has become so. So Heath says simply "to each", and "from each" for taking away. Our Lean says it the Greek way: `CommonNotion2` adds the angle EBD to both sides, and `rfl`, which proves a thing equal to itself, says that it is one angle, common to both.

**The modern way.** Put B at 0 in the complex plane, D at 1, C at −1 and A at cos θ + i sin θ, with 0 < θ < π: the angle ABD is the argument of A, which is θ, and the angle CBA is the argument of −1/A = −cos θ + i sin θ, which is π − θ. Together they make π, two right angles.
