**Picking up a triangle.** Euclid proves side–angle–side by lifting the triangle ABC and laying it down on DEF, and Heath thinks he did it unwillingly: "Euclid obviously used the method of superposition with reluctance" (vol. 1, p. 249). Here the moving is one named rule, [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean): the rule of System E §3.7, in the combined form LeanEuclid gives it, which only I.4 and I.8 may cite. Because the triangle is laid along DE, Lean needs that straight line by name, so the proof uses Postulate 1, which Euclid doesn't cite.

**Where B falls.** Heath points to something "most editors seem to have failed to observe": at the very start Euclid assumes that "if A be placed on D, and AB on DE, the point B will coincide with E, because AB is equal to DE. That is, the converse of Common Notion 4 is assumed for straight lines" (vol. 1, p. 250). Sir Henry Savile saw the gap and closed it: "let B fall somewhere short of E or beyond E; and in either case it will follow that the less is equal to the greater, which is impossible" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 250). That is Book Zero's `OnRay.eq_of_len_eq`, step for step.

**Where AC falls.** Next, AC "will also coincide with DF, because the angle BAC is equal to the angle EDF". This is a second unspoken fact: on one side of DE, only one ray from D makes a given angle with DE. Lean proves it as `onRay_of_angle_eq`: were the point where C lands off DF, one of two equal angles would be a part of the other.

**Equal triangles.** The superposition rule keeps every side and angle, but says nothing about area. That the triangle laid down has the area of ABC is [`area_congruent`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Metric.lean) (System E, Metric 9): triangles that agree in all their sides and angles are equal. Common Notion 4 then finishes the job, as Euclid says. Heiberg's bracketed sentence, that otherwise "two straight lines will enclose a space", Lean doesn't need: once B lands on E and C on F, the base simply is EF.

**The modern way.** With vectors, BC² = AB² + AC² − 2·AB·AC·cos A, so the two sides and the angle between them fix the base, and also the area, ½·AB·AC·sin A. Superposition itself is a rigid motion: in the complex plane, z ↦ az + b or z ↦ a z̄ + b with |a| = 1, carrying A to D, B to E and C to F.
