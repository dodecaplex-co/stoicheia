**Picking up a triangle.** Euclid proves side–angle–side by lifting the triangle ABC and laying it down on DEF, and Heath thinks he did it unwillingly: "Euclid obviously used the method of superposition with reluctance" (vol. 1, p. 249). Jacques Peletier saw the danger in 1557: if superposition were allowed as a proof, Heath reports, it "could equally well have been used in I. 2, 3 (thus in I. 2 we could simply have supposed the line taken up and *placed* at the point)" (vol. 1, p. 249). So we let it in as one named rule, [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean): the rule of System E §3.7, in the combined form LeanEuclid gives it, which only I.4 and I.8 may cite.

**Why must B land on E?** Heath points to something "most editors seem to have failed to observe": Euclid assumes that "if A be placed on D, and AB on DE, the point B will coincide with E, because AB is equal to DE. That is, the *converse* of *Common Notion* 4 is assumed for straight lines" (vol. 1, p. 250). Henry Savile closed the gap: "let B fall somewhere short of E or beyond E; and in either case it will follow that the less is equal to the greater, which is impossible" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 250). That is `OnRay.eq_of_len_eq`, step for step, in [Book Zero](../../Elements/Figure), our small library of facts every figure needs.

**What the moving does not say.** AC falls along DF "because the angle BAC is equal to the angle EDF", which takes for granted that on one side of DE only one ray from D makes that angle. Lean proves it as `onRay_of_angle_eq`: otherwise one of two equal angles would be part of the other. The moving keeps every side and angle but says nothing of area, so that the triangle laid down is equal to ABC needs a rule of its own, [`area_congruent`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Metric.lean) (System E, Metric 9).

**Why is the base EF?** Euclid's text says that otherwise "two straight lines will enclose a space", but J. L. Heiberg brackets the passage: an-Nairīzī gives it after the Q. E. D., so it is "a *scholium* only" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 249). Lean does without it: once B lands on E and C on F, the base is EF.

**The modern way.** BC² = AB² + AC² − 2·AB·AC·cos A, so the two sides and the angle between them fix the base, and also the area, ½·AB·AC·sin A. Superposition itself is a rigid motion: in the complex plane, z ↦ az + b or z ↦ a z̄ + b with |a| = 1, carrying A to D, B to E and C to F.
