**Picking up a triangle.** Euclid proves side–angle–side by lifting the triangle ABC and laying it down on DEF, and Heath thinks he did it unwillingly: "Euclid obviously used the method of superposition with reluctance" (vol. 1, p. 249). Jacques Peletier (Peletarius) objected as early as 1557 that if figures could be moved about, "the whole of geometry would be full of such proofs", and ended his note: *cogitabimusque figuras figuris superponere, Mechanicum quippiam esse: intelligere vero, id demum esse Mathematicum*, laying figures on figures is mechanical, understanding is mathematics. Bertrand Russell thought Euclid "would have done better to assume I. 4 as an axiom, as is practically done by Hilbert" (both reported by Heath, vol. 1, p. 249); Hilbert takes the equality of the remaining angles as an axiom and proves the equality of the bases (vol. 1, p. 250). Michael Beeson, Julien Narboux and Freek Wiedijk, checking Book I in Coq and HOL Light, derive I.4 from their five-line axiom, which comes from Tarski's geometry. Here superposition is one named rule, [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean): the rule of §3.7 of Avigad, Dean and Mumma's System E, in the combined form LeanEuclid gives it. The certificate lets only I.4 and I.8 cite it.

**Where B falls.** Heath points to something "most editors seem to have failed to observe": at the very start Euclid assumes that "if A be placed on D, and AB on DE, the point B will coincide with E, because AB is equal to DE. That is, the converse of Common Notion 4 is assumed for straight lines" (vol. 1, p. 250). Sir Henry Savile saw the gap and closed it: "let B fall somewhere short of E or beyond E; and in either case it will follow that the less is equal to the greater, which is impossible" (vol. 1, p. 250). That is the Lean lemma `OnRay.eq_of_len_eq`, step for step: of three points on a straight line one lies between the other two (System E, Between rule 6), the pieces add up to the whole (Diagram-segment rule 1), and the whole is greater than the part (Common Notion 5). Heath's further worry, why B should land on DE at all, is answered by the superposition rule itself, which lays AB along the ray from D through E.

**Where AC falls.** Next, AC "will also coincide with DF, because the angle BAC is equal to the angle EDF." This is a second unspoken fact: on one side of DE, only one ray from D makes a given angle with DE. Heath lists the matching existence statement among the things Euclid "really assumes": "On either side of the ray DE there is a ray DF such that the angle EDF is equal to the angle BAC" (vol. 1, p. 250). Lean proves the uniqueness as `onRay_of_angle_eq`. If the point C′ where C lands were off DF, then either C′ would lie within the angle EDF or F within the angle EDC′ (Triple-incidence rule 2 says which), and either way an angle would be part of an angle equal to it (Diagram-angle rules 1 and 2, with Common Notion 5 again). Pasch rule 3 puts C′ on F's side of D rather than behind it.

**The words in brackets.** Heiberg brackets the sentence about two straight lines enclosing a space, because the Arabic text of an-Nairīzī gives it only after the conclusion, "which shows that they constitute a scholium only". It was added, Heath says, to explain "an inference which really follows from the definition of a straight line and Post. 1" (vol. 1, p. 249). Lean doesn't need it either: a length is the length between two points, so once B′ is E and C′ is F the base B′C′ simply is EF, and Common Notion 4 says no more than that.

**Equal triangles.** The superposition rule promises that every side and angle is kept, but it says nothing about area. That the triangle laid down has the area of ABC is System E's Metric rule 9, [`area_congruent`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Metric.lean): triangles that agree in all their sides and angles are equal. After that, Common Notion 4 finishes the job, as Euclid says. And because Euclid lays the triangle along DE, Lean needs that straight line by name, which puts Postulate 1 on the certificate.

**The modern way.** With vectors, BC² = AB² + AC² − 2·AB·AC·cos A, so the two sides and the angle between them fix the base, and also the area, ½·AB·AC·sin A. Superposition itself is a rigid motion: in the complex plane, z ↦ az + b or z ↦ a z̄ + b with |a| = 1, carrying A to D, B to E and C to F.
