Euclid proves side–angle–side by picking up the triangle ABC and laying it down on DEF, and Heath thinks he did it unwillingly: "Euclid obviously used the method of superposition with reluctance" (vol. 1, p. 249). Jacques Peletier saw the danger in 1557. If superposition were allowed as a proof, Heath reports, it "could equally well have been used in I. 2, 3 (thus in I. 2 we could simply have supposed the line taken up and *placed* at the point)" (vol. 1, p. 249). So we let it in as one named rule, [`superposition`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean), in LeanEuclid's form of System E §3.7, and Peletier's danger is real for it. A proof may build on the corners it places, so as a construction principle it is stronger than System E's rule, and only our policy keeps it to I.4 and I.8.

**Why must B land on E?** Heath points to something "most editors seem to have failed to observe": Euclid assumes that "if A be placed on D, and AB on DE, the point B will coincide with E, because AB is equal to DE. That is, the *converse* of *Common Notion* 4 is assumed for straight lines" (vol. 1, p. 250). Henry Savile saw the difficulty and met it with this argument: "let B fall somewhere short of E or beyond E; and in either case it will follow that the less is equal to the greater, which is impossible" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 250). His argument assumes that B falls on DE or DE produced, which Heath says needs Postulates 1 and 2 together. Our superposition rule supplies this by putting B on the ray from D through E. [Book Zero](../../Elements/Figure) has his argument, step for step, as `OnRay.eq_of_len_eq`.

AC falls along DF "because the angle BAC is equal to the angle EDF", which assumes that on one side of DE only one ray from D makes that angle; Book Zero proves it as `onRay_of_angle_eq`, since otherwise one of two equal angles would be part of the other. The moving keeps every side and angle but says nothing of area, so that the triangle laid down is equal to ABC takes a rule of its own, [`area_congruent`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Metric.lean) (System E, Metric 9). The bases need no rule: Euclid's text says that otherwise "two straight lines will enclose a space", but J. L. Heiberg brackets the passage, since an-Nairīzī gives it after the Q. E. D., so it is "a *scholium* only" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 249). Once B lands on E and C on F, our base is EF.

**The modern way.** Superposition is a rigid motion: in the complex plane, $z \mapsto az + b$ or $z \mapsto a\bar{z} + b$ with $|a| = 1$, carrying A to D, B to E and C to F. What it carries is fixed by the two sides and the angle: the law of cosines, $\mathrm{BC}^2 = \mathrm{AB}^2 + \mathrm{AC}^2 - 2 \thinspace \mathrm{AB} \thinspace \mathrm{AC} \cos \mathrm{A}$, gives the base, and $\frac{1}{2} \thinspace \mathrm{AB} \thinspace \mathrm{AC} \sin \mathrm{A}$ the area.
