**One parallel does it.** Euclid draws AL parallel to BD (it is I.31, though he gives no citation), and it cuts the square on BC into two parallelograms, BL and CL. BL is double of the triangle ABD, the square GB double of the triangle FBC (I.41), and I.4 makes those triangles equal. J. L. Heiberg brackets the sentence on doubles because it quotes a Common Notion itself interpolated, as Heath explains (vol. 1, p. 350); in Lean, Common Notion 2 adds the equal triangles to themselves. For CL and HC Euclid says "similarly", and we write the half once, over any letters, and use it at B and at C.

**"I marvel more at the writer of the Elements."** Heath reports Proclus retelling the story that Pythagoras sacrificed an ox for this theorem, and adding: "But for my part, while I admire those who first observed the truth of this theorem, I marvel more at the writer of the Elements" (vol. 1, p. 350). His reason follows on the same page: in Book VI Euclid proves the theorem for any similar figures, similarly situated, on the three sides (VI.31). Not every reader marvelled. Heath thinks a proof by proportion suggested Euclid's, and calls his recasting of it on Book I alone "a veritable *tour de force* which compels admiration", though Arthur Schopenhauer called it "a mouse-trap proof" (p. 354).

**"Let the angle ABC be added to each."** The sum makes the angle DBA only if C lies within it, and the angle FBC only if A lies within that. Euclid reads both off his figure, citing nothing; Lean finds a reason in I.17 and I.13. The angles at B and C are less than right, and the squares stand outward, so `Prop47.within` puts each point where the figure does. It also puts A between BD and CE, and so the foot of AL between B and C: a point Euclid never names, since he calls the pieces BL and CL by their opposite corners. We give the three squares in our statement, so the Lean needs no I.46.

**The modern way.** Put A = (0, 0), B = (b, 0) and C = (0, c), and write a for BC. The foot of AL divides BC into b²/a and c²/a, so the rectangles BL and CL, of height a, have areas b² and c², and together they make the square on BC: b² + c² = a².
