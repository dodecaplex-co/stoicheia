**"I marvel more at the writer of the Elements."** "If we listen," says Proclus, "to those who wish to recount ancient history, we may find some of them referring this theorem to Pythagoras and saying that he sacrificed an ox in honour of his discovery. But for my part, while I admire those who first observed the truth of this theorem, I marvel more at the writer of the Elements" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 350). His reason follows on the same page: in Book VI Euclid proves the theorem for any similar figures, similarly situated, on the three sides (VI.31).

**One parallel does it.** Euclid describes a square on each side, the figure often called the windmill, and draws AL through A parallel to BD. It cuts the square on BC into two parallelograms, BL and CL. BL is double of the triangle ABD, and the square GB double of the triangle FBC, each pair on one base and in the same parallels (I.41); and I.4 makes those triangles equal. So BL is equal to the square on AB, CL likewise to the square on AC, and together they fill the square on BC.

**"Which compels admiration."** Heath thinks a proof by proportions suggested Euclid's: the perpendicular from the right angle cuts the triangle into two similar to the whole, so each smaller square is equal to the rectangle contained by the base and the piece of it beside that square, "whence the result follows by addition" (vol. 1, p. 353). Proportions wait for Book VI, so Euclid recast the proof on Book I alone, and Heath calls the result "a veritable *tour de force* which compels admiration, notwithstanding the ignorant strictures of Schopenhauer" (p. 354). Arthur Schopenhauer had called it "a mouse-trap proof" (p. 354).

**"Let the angle ABC be added to each."** The sum makes the angle DBA only if C lies within it, and the angle FBC only if A lies within that. Turn the square on BC in over the triangle, and the angle ABC would have to be taken from the right angle DBC, not added to it. Where the text describes the squares it says only "described on" the sides; but Euclid's next step takes AC and AG as "not lying on the same side" of BA, and his figure stands all three squares outward. Then there is no other case to leave to the reader: the right angle at A leaves the angle at B less than right, and every right-angled triangle gives his arrangement. We prove it from I.17 and I.13: the angles at B and C are less than right, and our statement stands the squares outward (`Prop47.within`). The same step puts the foot of AL between B and C, a point Euclid never names, since he calls the pieces BL and CL by their opposite corners.

**The modern way.** Put A = (0, 0), B = (b, 0) and C = (0, c), and write a for BC. The foot of AL divides BC into b²/a and c²/a, so the rectangles BL and CL, of height a, have areas b² and c², and together they make the square on BC: b² + c² = a².
