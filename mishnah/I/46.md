**Why "describe"?** Proclus notes that Euclid constructs a triangle but describes a square on a straight line, and Heath puts the difference so: "The *triangle* (or *angle*) is, so to say, pieced together, while the describing of a square on a given straight line is the making of a figure 'from' *one* side, and corresponds to the multiplication of the number representing the side by itself" (vol. 1, p. 348). So Euclid builds ADEB from AB alone, with a right angle, an equal straight line and two parallels, and only then proves it a square: equilateral and right-angled, as Definition I.22 asks.

**Which side of AB?** Euclid's figure settles it; his enunciation is silent. We take a point off AB, with no letter in Heath, to choose the side, and our statement promises D and E on it, because I.47 needs its squares outward and I.48 describes three of them so. I.11 draws AC on that side, and a circle about A lays off AD equal to AB (Postulate 3). Heath draws C beyond D; our proof leaves their order open. One step goes unnamed: "the angle ADE is also right" takes the right angle BAD from two right angles, and that is Common Notion 3.

**Equal squares, equal sides.** Euclid takes for granted in I.48 that squares on equal straight lines are equal, and the converse. Heath reports Proclus proving both here (vol. 1, pp. 348–349), and we prove them after I.46 as `Prop46.square_eq` and `Prop46.side_eq`. The first, Heath says, "is immediately obvious if we divide the squares into two triangles by drawing a diagonal in each" (p. 348): I.4 makes the triangles equal. For the converse Proclus sets the squares side by side, but Lean cannot move a figure. So I.3 cuts off from the greater side a straight line equal to the less, I.46 describes a square on it inside the greater square, and by I.34 the half of the one is a part of the half of the other, and so less.

**The modern way.** In the complex plane, turning AB about A through a right angle gives AD: D = A + i(B − A), and E = B + i(B − A) completes the square ADEB. Multiplying by −i instead describes the square on the other side of AB, and the given point says which.
