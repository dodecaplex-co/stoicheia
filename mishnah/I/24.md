Euclid's one figure has F below EG. Robert Simson added a sentence to make it the only case, "Of the two sides DE, DF let DE be the side which is not greater than the other", and Heath leaves it out: "I doubt whether Euclid could have been induced to insert the words himself, even if it had been represented to him that their omission meant leaving two possible cases out of consideration. His habit and that of the great Greek geometers was, not to set out all possible cases, but to give as a rule one case, generally the most difficult, as here, and to leave the others to the reader to work out for himself. We have already seen one instance in I. 7" (vol. 1, p. 297). Heath said as much under [I.2](02.md).

Proclus supplies the other two, which are, says Heath, "decidedly simpler than the case taken by Euclid as typical, and could well be left to the ingenuity of the learner" (vol. 1, p. 297). If F falls on EG, EF is a part of EG; if within the triangle DEG, "by I. 21, DF, FE are together less than DG, GE. But DF is equal to DG; therefore EF is less than EG" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 297), with equals taken away by a law Euclid never states. Our proof takes all three. Since the angle EDF is the less, DF, produced if need be, crosses EG, and F lies beyond the crossing, on it, or short of it. In Euclid's case the crossing lies between D and F, and that is what makes each of his comparisons the whole greater than the part: the angle EGF is a part of DGF, and DFG a part of EFG.

Why not compare the areas, as in I.4? Proclus, as Heath reports him, answers before anyone asks: unequal angles need not give unequal areas, and "the areas cannot be compared without the help of the theory of parallels" (vol. 1, p. 298). When the angles at A and D together make two right angles, the triangles are equal; when more, "*that triangle which has the greater angle is less*"; when less, greater (Heath, vol. 1, p. 299). Heath proves it under I.38, where an-Nairīzī has Heron put it.

**The modern way.** With $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ along the two given sides from the vertex, and $\theta$ the angle between them, the base is $|\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{c}|$, and $|\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{c}|^2 = |\mathbf{b}|^2 + |\mathbf{c}|^2 - 2 \thinspace |\mathbf{b}| \thinspace |\mathbf{c}| \cos\theta$. As $\theta$ grows from $0$ to $\pi$, $\cos\theta$ falls and the base grows. The area, $\frac{1}{2} \thinspace |\mathbf{b}| \thinspace |\mathbf{c}| \sin\theta$, is the same at $\theta$ and at $\pi - \theta$: the heart of Proclus' answer.
