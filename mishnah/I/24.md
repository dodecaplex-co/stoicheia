**"Not greater than the other."** Euclid's one figure has F below EG. Robert Simson added a sentence to make it the only case, "Of the two sides DE, DF let DE be the side which is not greater than the other", and Heath leaves it out: "His habit and that of the great Greek geometers was, not to set out all possible cases, but to give as a rule one case, generally the most difficult, as here, and to leave the others to the reader to work out for himself" (vol. 1, p. 297).

**"Decidedly simpler."** Proclus supplies the other two. If F falls on EG, EF is a part of EG. If it falls within the triangle DEG, "by I. 21, DF, FE are together less than DG, GE. But DF is equal to DG; therefore EF is less than EG" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 297), with equals taken away by a law Euclid never states. They are, says Heath, "decidedly simpler than the case taken by Euclid as typical, and could well be left to the ingenuity of the learner" (vol. 1, p. 297).

**Beyond EG, on it, or within?** We prove all three. Since the angle EDF is the less, DF, produced if need be, crosses EG at X (`crossbar`), and `Prop24.cases` puts F on EG, within the triangle DEG, or beyond it. For Proclus' second case the Lean cites I.21. In Euclid's case X lies between D and F, making each of his steps the whole greater than the part: the angle EGF is XGF, a part of DGF, and DFG is XFG, a part of EFG. DG is laid off on F's side of DE, as our I.23 allows.

**Why not the areas?** Proclus answers a question before it is asked: why not compare the areas, as in I.4? Unequal angles need not give unequal areas, and "the areas cannot be compared without the help of the theory of parallels" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 298). When the angles at A and D together make two right angles, the triangles are equal; when more, "*that triangle which has the greater angle is less*"; when less, greater (Heath, vol. 1, p. 299). Heath proves it under I.38, where an-Nairīzī has Heron put it.

**The modern way.** With vectors b and c along the equal sides and θ the angle between them, the base is |b − c|, and |b − c|² = |b|² + |c|² − 2|b||c| cos θ. As θ grows from nothing to two right angles, cos θ falls and the base grows. The area, ½|b||c| sin θ, is the same at θ and at two right angles less θ: the heart of Proclus' answer.
