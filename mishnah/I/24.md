**One case, and the hardest.** Euclid draws F below EG and argues from that figure alone. To spare the reader the other cases, Robert Simson added a sentence of his own, "Of the two sides DE, DF let DE be the side which is not greater than the other", and Heath leaves it out: "I doubt whether Euclid could have been induced to insert the words himself, even if it had been represented to him that their omission meant leaving two possible cases out of consideration. His habit and that of the great Greek geometers was, not to set out all possible cases, but to give as a rule one case, generally the most difficult, as here, and to leave the others to the reader to work out for himself. We have already seen one instance in I. 7" (vol. 1, p. 297).

**Where F falls.** Lean can't leave anything to the reader, so it has to find out where F can be. First, F lies within the angle EDG, because the angle EDF is the less: `Prop24.within`. Were F on DG, the two angles would be one angle (`angle_onRay_right`); were it beyond DG, then G would lie within the angle EDF (System E's triple incidence rule 2), and the angle EDG would be a part of it (`Prop16.part`). Then Book Zero's `crossbar` says that DF, drawn on, crosses EG at some point X. `Prop24.cases` reads off the three possibilities: F is X itself, on EG; or F falls short of X, within the triangle DEG; or F lies beyond X, below EG, which is Euclid's figure.

**Proclus' two cases.** "Proclus of course gives the other two cases which arise if we do not first provide that DE is not greater than DF. (1) In the first case G may fall on EF produced, and it is then obvious that EG is greater than EF. (2) In the second case EG may fall below EF. If so, by I. 21, DF, FE are together less than DG, GE. But DF is equal to DG; therefore EF is less than EG, i.e. than BC." Heath adds that these "are therefore decidedly simpler than the case taken by Euclid as typical, and could well be left to the ingenuity of the learner" (vol. 1, p. 297). The Lean takes them just as Proclus does. The first is `len_lt_of_between`, the whole greater than the part. The second cites I.21, which is why I.21 is on the certificate although Euclid never mentions it. Taking the equal DF and DG away from the two sums is `Magnitude.lt_of_add_lt_add_left` in Book Zero, one of the laws of magnitudes that System E spells out and Euclid uses without a word.

**Euclid's case: two parts and two wholes.** Euclid says "therefore the angle DFG is greater than the angle EGF", and then "much greater". Each step is the whole greater than the part, and the point X is what makes each one a part. The angle EGF is the angle XGF, since X lies on the ray from G through E (`angle_onRay_left`), and it is a part of the angle DGF because X lies between D and F. In the same way the angle DFG is the angle XFG, a part of the angle EFG because X lies between E and G. Both steps are `Prop24.part`: a straight line from a corner to a point of the opposite side cuts the angle in two (`angle_add_of_between`), and neither piece is nothing, since the point is off the arms (Diagram-angle 1). Then Common Notion 5. Only when F is below EG does X lie between D and F, and that is why the other two cases need arguments of their own.

**What else Lean made us say.** "Let DG be made equal to either of the two straight lines AC, DF" comes with no citation, and none is needed: Book Zero's `OnRay.exists_len_eq` describes the circle with centre D and distance DF (Post. 3) and takes G where the arm of the new angle cuts it (System E, Intersections 5); the angle EDG is still the angle I.23 built, its arms being the same rays (`angle_eq_of_onRay`). Euclid's figure also puts G on the same side of DE as F. Our I.23 lets us ask for that side, and `Prop24.side` checks that a point laid along the new arm stays there. To cite I.5 the Lean produces DF and DG, because our I.5 is stated as Euclid states it, with the angles under the base, and so Postulate 2 is on the certificate. Every tacit rule used here is from the System E of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma, and LeanEuclid's careful Lean version of it, by Logan Murphy, Kaiyu Yang, Jialiang Sun, Zhaoyu Li, Anima Anandkumar and Xujie Si, was our guide.

**Simson's road.** Heath warns that Simson's extra sentence is not free: "If however after all we prefer to insert Simson's words and avoid the latter two cases, the proof is not complete unless we show that, with his assumption, F must, in the figure of the proposition, fall below EG" (vol. 1, pp. 297–298). Augustus De Morgan "would make the following proposition precede: Every straight line drawn from the vertex of a triangle to the base is less than the greater of the two sides, or than either if they are equal". Heath prefers "the method employed by Pfleiderer, Lardner, and Todhunter": let DF meet EG in H; the angle DHG is greater than DEG [I. 16], which is not less than DGE [I. 18]; "Hence DH is less than DG, [I. 19] and therefore DH is less than DF" (vol. 1, p. 298). So Simson's road needs I.16, I.18 and I.19, where Proclus' two cases need only I.21 and the whole greater than the part.

**Another proof.** "Lastly, the modern alternative proof is worth giving." Bisect the angle FDG by DH, meeting EG in H, and join HF. By I.4, HF is equal to HG, so EG is EH and HF together, and these are greater than EF by I.20 (vol. 1, p. 298). Apart from F falling on EG itself, where EG is plainly the greater, it needs no cases at all.

**And the areas?** Why doesn't Euclid compare the areas, as he did in I.4? Proclus, Heath reports, "observes that inequality of the areas does not follow from the inequality of the angles contained by the equal sides", and that the areas "cannot be compared without the help of the theory of parallels". Proclus gives the answer all the same: if the angles at A and D are together equal to two right angles, the triangles are equal; "if greater than two right angles, that triangle which has the greater angle is less, and (3) if they are less, greater" (vol. 1, pp. 298–299). Heath traces it to Heron, through an-Nairizi, and saves it for I.38.

**The modern way.** By the law of cosines, BC² = AB² + AC² − 2 · AB · AC · cos A, and EF² is the same with cos D in place of cos A; the cosine falls as the angle grows from 0 to two right angles, so the greater angle gives the greater base. With vectors b and c along the equal sides, |b − c|² = |b|² + |c|² − 2 b·c, and only the dot product changes.
