Euclid's one figure has F below EG. Robert Simson added a sentence to make it the only case, "Of the two sides DE, DF let DE be the side which is not greater than the other", and Heath leaves it out, for Euclid's habit was "to give as a rule one case, generally the most difficult, as here, and to leave the others to the reader to work out for himself" (vol. 1, p. 297), as Heath said under [I.2](02.md).

Proclus supplies the other two, which are, says Heath, "decidedly simpler than the case taken by Euclid as typical, and could well be left to the ingenuity of the learner" (vol. 1, p. 297). If F falls on EG, EF is a part of EG; if within the triangle DEG, "by I. 21, DF, FE are together less than DG, GE. But DF is equal to DG; therefore EF is less than EG" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 297), with equals taken away from unequals by a tacit rule of magnitude, which Euclid never states.

Our proof goes the other way. It inserts Simson's words, and then, says Heath, "the proof is not complete unless we show that, with his assumption, F must, in the figure of the proposition, fall *below* EG" (vol. 1, p. 298). He shows it in a few lines: "But it is easy to prove directly that F falls below EG, if DE is not greater than DG, by the method employed by Pfleiderer, Lardner, and Todhunter" (vol. 1, p. 298). Let DF, produced if need be, meet EG at H. The angle DHG, outside the triangle DEH, is greater than DEG (I.16), and DEG is not less than DGE, since DE is not the greater side (I.18, or I.5 when the two are equal); so in the triangle DHG the angle at H is greater than the angle at G, and DH is less than DG (I.19), which is DF. So H lies between D and F, and F beyond EG (`Prop24.below`). The words cost nothing in scope, for the enunciation reads the same with B and C, and E and F, exchanged: whichever of DE and DF is not the greater may be called DE. Then Euclid's own comparisons, each the whole greater than the part, carry every case: the angle EGF is a part of DGF, and DFG a part of EFG.

Why not compare the areas, as in I.4? Proclus, as Heath reports him, answers before anyone asks: unequal angles need not give unequal areas, and "the areas cannot be compared without the help of the theory of parallels" (vol. 1, p. 298). When the angles at A and D together make two right angles, the triangles are equal; when more, "*that triangle which has the greater angle is less*"; when less, greater (Heath, vol. 1, p. 299). Heath gives Heron's proof under [I.38](38.md), where an-Nairīzī places the theorem and names Heron as its author.

**The modern way.** With $\mathbf{b}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ along the two given sides from the vertex, and $\theta$ the angle between them, the base is $|\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{c}|$, and $|\mathbf{b} - \mathbf{c}|^2 = |\mathbf{b}|^2 + |\mathbf{c}|^2 - 2 \thinspace |\mathbf{b}| \thinspace |\mathbf{c}| \cos\theta$. As $\theta$ grows from $0$ to $\pi$, $\cos\theta$ falls and the base grows. The area, $\frac{1}{2} \thinspace |\mathbf{b}| \thinspace |\mathbf{c}| \sin\theta$, is the same at $\theta$ and at $\pi - \theta$: the heart of Proclus' answer.
