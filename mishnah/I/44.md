**Applying an area.** Heath judges that this proposition "will always remain one of the most impressive in all geometry" (vol. 1, p. 342), for its result, a parallelogram of given area and angle on a given side, and for the simplicity of its means: BF becomes a complement of the larger parallelogram HLKF, and I.43 makes the other complement, LB, equal to it. Proclus gives an example in numbers: an area of twelve feet, applied in a right angle to a straight line of four feet, has a breadth of three (Heath, vol. 1, p. 343).

**"Let it be placed."** I.42 builds its parallelogram on half of the triangle's base, and Euclid simply moves it to B. Lean cannot move a figure, so `Prop44.place` builds an equal one there: I.2 copies the sides, I.23 the angle, I.31 draws the parallels, `Prop30.meet` finds where they meet, and I.4 with I.34 makes the areas equal. The point P of our statement names the side of AB on which the parallelogram stands; G goes on the other side, so that M falls on P's. P has no letter in the figure.

**Where the straight lines meet.** `Prop30.meet` gives H, L and M: a straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other, by I.30, as Heath reports Proclus drawing it under I.31. Euclid's "parallel to either" is I.30 again (`Prop30.parallel`): AH, drawn parallel to BG, is parallel to EF, and KL, drawn parallel to FH, to AB. Parallels keep order, so `Between.of_sameSide` carries the order of A, B, E over to H, G, F, and on to the other orders that I.43 and I.15 ask for.

**The fifth postulate.** I.29 makes the angles AHF, HFE together two right angles. HB splits the angle AHF (`angle_add_of_within`), so by Common Notion 5 the angles BHG, GFE are less than two right angles, and `Postulate5` gives K, where HB and FE meet, on B's side of FG. The proof also uses Postulate 1 to join HB, and, in `Prop44.place`, Postulates 2 and 3 and Definition 15; superposition comes in through I.4 and I.23.

**The modern way.** A parallelogram with sides a and b in the angle θ has area ab sin θ, so an area q applied to a has breadth q/(a sin θ). In oblique axes along BE and BM, with B at the origin, H = (−AB, −BG) and K = (BE, BM) lie on one straight line through B, so BM = BE·BG/AB, and AB·BM = BE·BG.
