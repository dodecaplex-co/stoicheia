**"One of the most impressive in all geometry."** To apply a parallelogram to a straight line is to build it on that whole straight line. Euclid sets the parallelogram of I.42 beside AB as a complement in the larger parallelogram HLKF, and I.43 makes the other complement, standing on AB, equal to it. Heath judges that this proposition "will always remain one of the most impressive in all geometry" (vol. 1, p. 342), for the simplicity of its means and for their result: a parallelogram of any shape remade on a side "of any given length, e.g. a *unit* length" (vol. 1, p. 343).

**"The Muse of the Pythagoreans."** Proclus, citing Eudemus, calls the application of areas, with its exceeding and its falling-short, "discoveries of the Muse of the Pythagoreans" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 343). An area that overruns the straight line exceeds; one that leaves some of it over falls short. Apollonius took the names for the curves we still call conic sections, "designating one of these a *parabola* (application), another a *hyperbola* (exceeding) and another an *ellipse* (falling-short)" (vol. 1, p. 343). Proclus' example in numbers: an area of twelve feet, applied in a right angle to a straight line of four feet, has a breadth of three.

**"Let it be placed."** Euclid simply moves I.42's parallelogram to B. Lean cannot move a figure, so `Prop44.place` builds an equal one there: I.2 copies its sides, I.23 its angle, I.31 draws the parallels, and I.4 with I.34 makes the areas equal. Our statement names a point P, with no letter in the figure, for the side of AB the parallelogram stands on; G goes on the other side, so that M falls on P's. Euclid's "parallel to either BG or EF" is I.30, and the parallels carry the order of A, B, E to the straight lines crossing them.

**"HB, FE, when produced, will meet."** Here Euclid cites Postulate 5 for the second and last time in Book I. I.29 makes the angles AHF, HFE together two right angles; HB splits the angle AHF, so BHG and GFE are less, by Common Notion 5, and `Postulate5` gives K on B's side of FG. Charles Dodgson, defending Euclid's postulate, gave I.44 as an example of its use, as Heath reports under I.29.

**The modern way.** A parallelogram with sides a and b in the angle θ has area ab sin θ, so an area q applied to a has breadth q/(a sin θ). In oblique axes along BE and BM, with B at the origin, H = (−AB, −BG) and K = (BE, BM) lie on one straight line through B, so BM = BE·BG/AB, and AB·BM = BE·BG.
