To apply a parallelogram to a straight line AB is to build it with AB as one complete side. This "apply" is not the "applied" of I.4 and I.8, where one triangle is laid on another. Euclid sets the parallelogram of I.42 beside AB as one complement in a larger parallelogram, HLKF, and I.43 makes the other complement, the one standing on AB, equal to it. Heath judges that this proposition "will always remain one of the most impressive in all geometry" (vol. 1, p. 342), for the simplicity of its means and for what they achieve: a parallelogram of any shape remade on a side "of any given length, e.g. a *unit* length" (vol. 1, p. 343).

**"Let it be placed."** Euclid simply moves I.42's parallelogram to B. Our superposition rule lays a triangle down only in thought and hands back no point, so it cannot put a parallelogram at B; we build an equal one where Euclid puts his, with constructions Book I already has (`Prop44.place`). A given point says on which side of AB the parallelogram is to stand, and the parallels carry the order of A, B, E to the straight lines crossing them.

Then, for the second and last time in Book I, Euclid calls on Postulate 5: "HB, FE, when produced, will meet." I.29 makes the angles AHF, HFE together two right angles; HB splits the angle AHF, so BHG and GFE are less, by Common Notion 5, and the postulate gives K on B's side of FG. Charles Dodgson, defending Euclid's postulate, gave I.44 as an example of its use, as Heath reports under I.29.

Proclus, citing Eudemus, calls the application of areas, with its exceeding and its falling-short, "discoveries of the Muse of the Pythagoreans" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 343). An area that overruns the straight line exceeds; one that leaves some of it over falls short. Apollonius took the names for the curves we still call conic sections, "designating one of these a *parabola* (application), another a *hyperbola* (exceeding) and another an *ellipse* (falling-short)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 343). Proclus' own example is in numbers: an area of twelve feet, applied in a right angle to a straight line of four feet, has a breadth of three.

**The modern way.** A parallelogram with sides $a$ and $b$ in the angle $\theta$ has area $ab\sin\theta$, so an area $q$ applied in that angle to a straight line of length $a$ has breadth $q/(a\sin\theta)$. In Euclid's figure, take oblique axes along BE and BM with B at the origin: $\mathrm{H} = (-\mathrm{AB}, -\mathrm{BG})$ and $\mathrm{K} = (\mathrm{BE}, \mathrm{BM})$ lie on one straight line through B, so $\mathrm{BM} = \mathrm{BE} \cdot \mathrm{BG}/\mathrm{AB}$, and $\mathrm{AB} \cdot \mathrm{BM} = \mathrm{BE} \cdot \mathrm{BG}$.
