**"Let the straight line GK fall upon them."** Heath's figure sets EF between AB and CD, so GK crosses EF at H. I.29 makes the alternate angles AGK, GHF equal, and the exterior angle GHF equal to the interior and opposite angle GKD; Common Notion 1 joins the two. "Therefore AB is parallel to CD" cites nothing: it is I.27, and the Lean cites it. Heath notes that the usual "Therefore etc." is, "curiously enough, wanting at the end of this proposition" (vol. 1, p. 314), and our Lean goes without it too. The given points may stand elsewhere, so `Prop30.point_on_side` finds an end of each straight line on the side of GK his figure wants, producing it if it must.

**What if EF lies outside?** Euclid's enunciation allows it, and then a straight line from AB to CD may miss EF. So we first prove that two straight lines through a point, each parallel to a third, are one straight line (`Prop30.unique`). Join the point to a point H of the third. By I.29, the angle each makes with the joining line, together with the angle at H, makes two right angles. Take away the shared angle at H (Common Notion 3), and the two make equal angles on one side, so they run along one ray. Were AB and CD to meet, they would be one; our statement keeps C off AB, for a straight line is not parallel to itself.

**One statement, turned round.** The axiom that bears John Playfair's name says that two straight lines which cut one another are never both parallel to a third; I.30 says that two straight lines parallel to a third never cut one another. Augustus De Morgan saw that these are one: "The proposition is, as De Morgan points out, the *logical* equivalent of 'Playfair's' axiom" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 314). So `Prop30.unique` is that axiom, proved here from I.29. De Morgan adds that another proposition is "much wanted", and Heath supplies it: when the sides of one angle are parallel, or perpendicular, to the sides of another, the two angles are equal or together make two right angles (vol. 1, p. 315).

**The modern way.** Give each straight line a point and a direction. Straight lines that never meet have directions that are multiples of one another, so AB and CD, each parallel to EF, have directions that are multiples of each other's; with C off AB, they are two straight lines with one direction, and never meet.
