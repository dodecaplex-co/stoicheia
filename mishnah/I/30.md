Heath's figure sets EF between AB and CD, so GK crosses EF at H. I.29 makes the alternate angles AGK, GHF equal, and the exterior angle GHF equal to the interior and opposite angle GKD; Common Notion 1 joins the two, and the alternate angles AGK, GKD make AB parallel to CD. That last step is I.27, though Heath gives no reference for it. Heath notes that the usual "Therefore etc." is, "curiously enough, wanting at the end of this proposition" (vol. 1, p. 314), and our Lean goes without it too.

**What if EF lies outside?** Euclid's enunciation allows it, and then a straight line from AB to CD may miss EF. His argument wants a straight line falling on all three, and a straight line GH from AB to EF is one, for it meets CD. That rests on uniqueness: two straight lines through one point, each parallel to a third, are one straight line (`Prop30.unique`). Join their common point to a point of the third, and by I.29 and Common Notion 3 the two make equal angles with that joining line, on one side of it, and so run along one ray. Were GH to miss CD, GH and EF would be two parallels to CD through H, and so one straight line, though G is off EF. The rule that a straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other would give the meeting at once, but our later proofs draw that rule from I.30 itself (`Prop30.meet`), and to use it here would be to argue in a circle. So uniqueness, proved from I.29, supplies no more than what Euclid's "let the straight line GK fall upon them" (vol. 1, p. 314) takes for granted, and his own chase goes through with the straight lines in another order. AB and CD lie on one side of EF, so H is not between G and K, and CD or AB is in the middle. With CD in the middle, I.29 makes the alternate angles at G and H equal, and the exterior angle at K equal to the interior and opposite angle at H; Common Notion 1 joins them, and I.27 makes AB parallel to CD. With AB in the middle, it is the same argument with AB and CD exchanged.

John Playfair's axiom says that two straight lines which cut one another are never both parallel to a third; I.30 says that two straight lines parallel to a third never cut one another. Heath credits Augustus De Morgan with seeing that these are one: "The proposition is, as De Morgan points out, the *logical* equivalent of 'Playfair's' axiom" (vol. 1, p. 314). So `Prop30.unique` is that axiom, proved here from I.29. De Morgan adds that another proposition is "much wanted", and Heath supplies it: when the sides of one angle are parallel, or perpendicular, to the sides of another, the two angles are equal or together make two right angles (vol. 1, p. 315).
