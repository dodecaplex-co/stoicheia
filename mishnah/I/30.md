**"Let the straight line GK fall upon them."** Heath's figure sets EF between AB and CD, so GK crosses EF at H. I.29 makes the alternate angles AGK, GHF equal, and the exterior angle GHF equal to the interior and opposite angle GKD; Common Notion 1 joins the two. Euclid's last step, "Therefore AB is parallel to CD", leans on I.27, though Heath gives no bracket for it. Heath notes that the usual "Therefore etc." is, "curiously enough, wanting at the end of this proposition" (vol. 1, p. 314), and our Lean goes without it too.

**What if EF lies outside?** Euclid's enunciation allows it, and then a straight line from AB to CD may miss EF. So we first prove that two straight lines through a point, each parallel to a third, are one straight line (`Prop30.unique`): joined to the third, they make equal angles on one side of the straight line joining them, by I.29 and Common Notion 3, and so run along one ray. Were AB and CD to meet, they would be one.

**Playfair's axiom in disguise.** John Playfair's axiom says that two straight lines which cut one another are never both parallel to a third; I.30 says that two straight lines parallel to a third never cut one another. Heath credits Augustus De Morgan with seeing that these are one: "The proposition is, as De Morgan points out, the *logical* equivalent of 'Playfair's' axiom" (vol. 1, p. 314). So `Prop30.unique` is that axiom, proved here from I.29. De Morgan adds that another proposition is "much wanted", and Heath supplies it: when the sides of one angle are parallel, or perpendicular, to the sides of another, the two angles are equal or together make two right angles (vol. 1, p. 315).

**The modern way.** Give each straight line a point and a direction. As in I.29, AB and CD, each parallel to EF, have directions that are multiples of EF's, and so of each other's; with C off AB, they are two straight lines with one direction, and never meet.
