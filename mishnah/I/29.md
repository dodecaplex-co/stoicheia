**"Less than two right angles."** Here Euclid uses Postulate 5 for the first time. Heath says of it: "When we consider the countless successive attempts made through more than twenty centuries to prove the Postulate, many of them by geometers of ability, we cannot but admire the genius of the man who concluded that such a hypothesis, which he found necessary to the validity of his whole system of geometry, was really indemonstrable" (vol. 1, p. 202). `Postulate5` is on the list of things I.29 rests on, and our checks make sure that nothing before I.29 rests on it.

**What the postulate asks.** Euclid cites it in shorter words than its own, and Heath notes what they leave out: "A good deal is left to be understood, namely that the straight lines begin from points at which they meet a transversal" (vol. 1, p. 312). `Postulate5` asks for all of it: the straight lines through B and G, through G and H, and through H and D, with B and D on one side of GH and the angles BGH, GHD less than two right angles. The statement puts B and D on one side of EF, and `SameSideOf.inner` says that EF is the straight line GH. What the postulate gives, a point on both AB and CD, is just what Definition 23, our `Parallel`, rules out.

**The other case.** Euclid lets the angle AGH be the greater and says nothing of the angle GHD being greater. That case is his argument from H's end, on the side of A and C, with I.13 at H. So we give the argument once, for any two parallels and any straight line falling on them, and use it twice, as I.16 does for its "similarly". "Let the angle BGH be added to each" cites nothing: as in I.17, adding the same thing keeps "less than" (`add_lt_add_right`).

**Playfair's way.** Heath also proves I.29 from "Playfair's" axiom, that two straight lines which cut one another cannot both be parallel to a third. He reports Charles Dodgson's case for keeping Euclid's postulate all the same: it "gives a direct criterion for judging that two straight lines meet, a criterion which is constantly required, e.g. in I. 44" (vol. 1, p. 314).

**The modern way.** In coordinates, straight lines that never meet have one direction: if u points from A to B, then D − C is a multiple of u, and a positive one, since B and D lie on one side of EF. With w pointing from G to H, the angle AGH lies between −u and w, the angle GHD between −w and u, and both have cosine −u·w/(|u||w|).
