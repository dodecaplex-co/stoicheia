**Two directions, two sides.** Euclid's "in the direction of B, D or towards A, C" is literally, Heath notes, "towards the *parts* B, D or towards A, C" (vol. 1, p. 308), and Lean reads the parts as the two sides of EF. A point G where AB and CD meet is off EF, for AB meets EF at E alone, and were G at E, CD would be EF itself. So G lies on D's side of EF or on A's, since a straight line has just two sides (System E, Same side 5, `same_side_or`). Those are the two directions, and Definition 23 is `AreParallel.of_not_meet`.

**Where G falls.** The angle AEF is exterior to the triangle GEF because E lies between A and G, and EFG is the given angle EFD because G lies on the ray from F through D. Euclid takes both from his figure; Lean finds them from G's side of EF. B is on that side too, so G lies on the ray from E through B (`OnRay.of_sameSideOf`), beyond E from A (`OnRay.between`); in the same way it lies on the ray from F through D, so EFG is EFD (`angle_onRay_right`). Then I.16 does the rest. Euclid's "similarly" is the same argument with the figure read from the other end, so the Lean gives it once and uses it twice, as in I.16.

**A figure that cannot be.** Heath draws AB and CD as though parallel and bends BG and DG to meet at G, since no straight lines can do what the supposition asks. Our figure draws what the proof supposes, A, E, B, G on one straight line and C, F, D, G on another, so the angles AEF and EFD, supposed equal, plainly are not. "De Morgan observes that I. 27 is a *logical* equivalent of I. 16" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 309): straight lines that met would make a triangle with EF, and I.16 would forbid the equal angles.

**Before Postulate 5.** Heath gathers from Aristotle that I.27 and I.28 are older than Euclid, whose own change came in I.29, with his Postulate 5. The certificate agrees: I.27 rests on Postulates 1 to 4, through I.16, and not on Postulate 5. The proof uses Postulate 1 to draw AB, CD and EF (I.16 brings it in as well); "when produced" costs nothing more, for a straight line in Lean is already whole.

**The modern way.** Let u and v be the unit vectors from E towards B and from F towards D, and w = E − F. Then AEF is the angle between u and w, and EFD the angle between w and v; since B and D lie on one side of EF, equal angles make u = v. A common point E + su = F + tu would make w a multiple of u, putting F on AB, and so A on EF.
