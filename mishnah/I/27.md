**"The second section of the first Book."** "With this proposition begins the second section of the first Book," Heath writes (vol. 1, p. 308): the section on parallels, which the areas to come will need. EF falls on AB and CD, making the alternate angles AEF, EFD equal, on opposite sides of EF between AB and CD. If AB and CD met at G, they would close a triangle GEF whose exterior angle AEF is equal to the interior and opposite angle EFG: I.16 forbids it. So they never meet, and Definition I.23 calls them parallel.

**Older than Euclid.** From two passages of Aristotle Heath concludes: "It seems clear then from Aristotle that I. 27, 28 at least are pre-Euclidean, and that it was only in I. 29 that Euclid made a change by using his Postulate" (vol. 1, p. 309). Augustus De Morgan saw that I.27 "is a *logical* equivalent of I. 16" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 309), the same fact turned round.

**"In the direction of B, D."** Literally, Heath notes, it is "towards the *parts* B, D or towards A, C" (vol. 1, p. 308), and we read the parts as the two sides of EF. A point where AB and CD meet is off EF, for AB meets EF at E alone, and were it E, CD would be EF itself; so it lies on one side of EF or the other. On D's side, G lies on the rays from E through B and from F through D, so AEF is exterior to the triangle GEF, and EFG is the given angle EFD. Euclid's figure shows both, and we prove both.

**A figure that cannot be.** Heath draws AB and CD as though parallel, bending BG and DG to meet at G: no straight lines can do what the supposition asks. Our figure draws what the proof supposes, A, E, B, G on one straight line and C, F, D, G on another, so the angles AEF and EFD, supposed equal, are not.

**The modern way.** Let u and v be the unit vectors from E towards B and from F towards D, and w = E − F. Then AEF is the angle between u and w, and EFD the angle between w and v; since B and D lie on one side of EF, equal angles make u = v. A common point E + su = F + tu would make w a multiple of u, putting F on AB, and so A on EF.
