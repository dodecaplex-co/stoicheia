**Evident even to an ass.** "It was the habit of the Epicureans, says Proclus (p. 323), to ridicule this theorem as being evident even to an ass and requiring no proof": put fodder at one corner of a triangle and the ass at another, and he goes along the one side between them, not the other two. Savile thought the Epicureans fit to eat hay with the ass. "Proclus replies truly that a mere perception of the truth of the theorem is a different thing from a scientific proof of it and a knowledge of the reason why it is true. Moreover, as Simson says, the number of axioms should not be increased without necessity" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 287). Lean takes Simson's side: no tacit rule states the theorem, so I.20 is proved, as Euclid proves it, from I.5 and I.19.

**Room to cut off AD.** Euclid's "let DA be made equal to CA" has no bracket, but it is I.3's work, as in I.11 and I.16, and I.3 cuts off only from a greater straight line. Book Zero's `produce_longer` makes room: a circle about A through C (Post. 3) is cut by BA produced beyond the centre (System E, Intersections 5), and BA produced a little further (Post. 2) is greater than AC (Def. 15). I.3 cuts AD off it, and `between_cut` puts A between B and D.

**What Lean made us say.** Euclid's "therefore the angle BCD is greater than the angle ADC" carries the bracket [C.N. 5]: greater, that is, than ACD, which I.5 has just made equal to it. So CA must fall within the angle BCD, and it does, because A lies between B and D (`angle_add_of_between`, with `Triangle.angle_pos` for the part BCA). Euclid then writes BDC for ADC, one angle since A lies on the ray from D through B (`angle_onRay_left`); DCB is a triangle, C being off BD (`Triangle.of_line`); and DB is BA and AD together (System E, Diagram-segment 1).

**"Similarly."** Euclid proves that BA, AC are greater than BC and leaves the other two pairs to the reader. The Lean gives his argument once, for every triangle, and uses it three times, naming the triangle from each corner in turn (`Triangle.bac`, `Triangle.cba`).

**The modern way.** For vectors u and v, |u + v|² = |u|² + 2u·v + |v|², and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality u·v ≤ |u||v| makes this at most (|u| + |v|)², with equality only when u and v point the same way. In the complex plane it reads |z + w| ≤ |z| + |w|, and what Euclid proves, the theory of metric spaces takes as part of the definition of distance: the triangle inequality.
