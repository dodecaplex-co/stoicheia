**Evident even to an ass.** Heath tells the story: "It was the habit of the Epicureans, says Proclus (p. 323), to ridicule this theorem as being evident even to an ass and requiring no proof, and their allegation that the theorem was 'known' (γνώριμον) even to an ass was based on the fact that, if fodder is placed at one angular point and the ass at another, he does not, in order to get to his food, traverse the two sides of the triangle but only the one side separating them." It is "an argument which makes Savile exclaim that its authors were 'digni ipsi, qui cum Asino foenum essent'": fit to eat hay with the ass themselves. "Proclus replies truly that a mere perception of the truth of the theorem is a different thing from a scientific proof of it and a knowledge of the reason why it is true. Moreover, as Simson says, the number of axioms should not be increased without necessity" (vol. 1, p. 287). Lean takes Simson's side. Neither our tacit rules nor System E's, from which they come, say that two sides of a triangle are greater than the third, so I.20 has to be proved, and it is proved here from I.5 and I.19, as Euclid does it.

**Room to cut off AD.** "Let BA be drawn through to the point D, let DA be made equal to CA." Heath gives no bracket, but making one straight line equal to another on a straight line produced is the work of I.3, as in I.11 and I.16, and I.3 cuts off only from a *greater* straight line. Book Zero's [`produce_longer`](../../Elements/Figure/Lines.lean) makes room. Describe the circle with centre A and distance AC (Post. 3). BA, produced beyond the centre, cuts it at some point (System E, Intersections 5), and produced a little further still (Post. 2) it is greater than AC [Def. 15]. Then I.3 cuts AD off it, and one fact of order from Book Zero, `between_cut`, puts A between B and D. It is the same step that I.11 and I.16 take, and I.22 takes it three times. As in I.11, the circle has already marked a point at the right distance, so Lean could do without I.3, but "made equal" is I.3's step, and we take it.

**What Lean made us say.** "Therefore the angle BCD is greater than the angle ADC [C.N. 5]": greater, that is, than the angle ACD, which I.5 has just made equal to ADC. The whole is greater than the part only if CA falls within the angle BCD, and it does because A lies between B and D. Book Zero says so: the angle BCD is made up of BCA and ACD (`angle_add_of_between`, from Pasch 2 and Diagram-angle 2), and BCA, an angle of the triangle ABC, is something (`Triangle.angle_pos`, from Diagram-angle 1). In the next breath Euclid speaks of the angle BDC where he had ADC, and they are one angle because A lies on the ray from D through B (`angle_onRay_left`). I.19 is then applied to the triangle DCB, which is a triangle because C is off the straight line BD (`Triangle.of_line`). And "But DA is equal to AC; therefore BA, AC are greater than BC" rests on DB being BA and AD together, since A lies between B and D (System E, Diagram-segment 1). Each of these rules is Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma's, and LeanEuclid's careful Lean version of System E, by Logan Murphy, Kaiyu Yang, Jialiang Sun, Zhaoyu Li, Anima Anandkumar and Xujie Si, was open beside us the whole way.

**I.5, with its sides produced.** Our I.5 states both of its parts, the angles at the base and the angles under it, so to cite it Lean must produce AD and AC beyond D and C (Book Zero's `produce`), though only the angles at the base are wanted here. I.7 does the same.

**"Similarly."** Euclid proves that BA, AC are greater than BC, and leaves the other two pairs to the reader. As in I.16, the Lean gives his argument once, for every triangle, and uses it three times: in the triangle ABC as Euclid draws it, in BAC with AB drawn through beyond B, and in CBA with BC drawn through beyond C. Naming a triangle from another corner (`Triangle.bac`, `Triangle.cba`) and reading a straight line from either end (`len_symm`) are all the "similarly" needs.

**Heron and Porphyry.** They proved the theorem, Heath reports from Proclus, "without producing one of the sides" (vol. 1, p. 287). In the first proof the angle BAC is bisected by AD meeting BC in D; by I.16 the exterior angle ADC is greater than the angle BAD, "that is, greater than the angle DAC", so by I.19 "the side AC is greater than the side CD"; similarly AB is greater than BD, and the two are added. In Lean, AD meeting BC would be I.9 together with Book Zero's `crossbar`. The second, also direct, has to sort the triangles into cases, and the third is by *reductio ad absurdum* (vol. 1, pp. 287–288). Euclid's own needs only one figure.

**The modern way.** For vectors u and v, |u + v|² = |u|² + 2u·v + |v|², and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality u·v ≤ |u||v| makes this at most (|u| + |v|)², with equality only when u and v point the same way, that is, when the triangle has collapsed onto a straight line. In the complex plane it reads |z + w| ≤ |z| + |w|; and what Euclid proves, the modern theory of metric spaces takes as part of the definition of distance, under the name of the triangle inequality.
