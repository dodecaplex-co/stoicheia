**Evident even to an ass.** "It was the habit of the Epicureans, says Proclus (p. 322), to ridicule this theorem as being evident even to an ass and requiring no proof" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 287). Put fodder at one corner of a triangle and the ass at another, and he walks the one side between them, not the other two. Henry Savile exclaimed that they were "digni ipsi, qui cum Asino foenum essent" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 287), fit to eat hay with the ass themselves.

**Seeing is not proving.** "Proclus replies truly that a mere perception of the truth of the theorem is a different thing from a scientific proof of it and a knowledge of the reason *why* it is true. Moreover, as Simson says, the number of axioms should not be increased without necessity" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 287). We take Robert Simson's side: no tacit rule of ours states the theorem, so I.20 is proved, as Euclid proves it, from I.5 and I.19.

**Without producing a side.** Heron and Porphyry, Heath reports from Proclus, "proved this theorem in different ways … without producing one of the sides" (vol. 1, p. 287). The first bisects the angle BAC by AD, meeting BC at D. The angle ADC, outside the triangle ABD, is greater than BAD (I.16), that is, than DAC, so AC is greater than CD (I.19); likewise AB is greater than BD, and adding gives the theorem.

**Why is BCD the greater?** Heath's bracket gives Common Notion 5, the whole greater than the part: BCD is greater than ACD, which I.5 has just made equal to ADC. So CA must fall within the angle BCD, and we say why: A lies between B and D, where Euclid put it when he drew BA through to D.

**The modern way.** For vectors u and v, |u + v|² = |u|² + 2u·v + |v|², and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality u·v ≤ |u||v| makes this at most (|u| + |v|)², with equality only when u and v point the same way. In the complex plane it reads |z + w| ≤ |z| + |w|, and what Euclid proves, the theory of metric spaces takes as part of the definition of distance: the triangle inequality.
