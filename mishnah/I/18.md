**Which comes first.** I.18 and I.19 say almost the same words in a different order, and Heath gives a rule for telling them apart: "the *datum* comes first and the *quaesitum* second, the *datum* being in this proposition the greater *side* and in the next the greater *angle*" (vol. 1, p. 283). He passes on Isaac Todhunter's memory aid as well: "In order to assist the student in remembering which of these two propositions [I. 18, 19] is demonstrated directly and which indirectly, it may be observed that the order is similar to that in I. 5 and I. 6" (vol. 1, p. 283). I.18, like I.5, goes straight at its conclusion; I.19, like I.6, gets there by *reductio*.

**A citation Euclid leaves out.** Euclid brackets I.3 and I.16, but "the angle ADB is equal to the angle ABD, since the side AB is equal to AD" is I.5, given without a reference. Lean has to cite it, for the isosceles triangle ABD. Our I.5 also speaks of the angles under the base, so `Prop5.base` produces AB and AD for it (Post. 2), and only the first half is used. The exterior angle itself needs no producing: I.3 has put D between A and C, so the side CD of the triangle BCD is already produced, to A.

**"Much greater."** The angle DCB that I.16 gives is the triangle's angle ACB, because D lies on the ray from C through A (`OnRay.of_between`, `angle_onRay_left`). And "much greater" needs BD within the angle ABC, so that ABD is a part of it: since D lies between A and C, the angle ABC is made up of ABD and DBC (`angle_add_of_between`), and DBC is something, B being off AC (`Triangle.angle_pos`). Then come Common Notion 5, the whole greater than the part, and `lt_trans`: what is greater than the greater is greater still.

**The modern way.** Name the sides a = BC, b = CA, c = AB. The law of cosines gives cos C − cos B = (b − c)(b + c − a)(a + b + c)/(2abc), and b + c is greater than a (that is I.20), so when b is greater than c, cos B is less than cos C and the angle B is the greater.
