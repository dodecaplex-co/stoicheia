**Which comes first.** I.18 and I.19 say almost the same words in a different order, and Heath gives a rule for telling them apart: "the datum comes first and the quaesitum second, the datum being in this proposition the greater side and in the next the greater angle" (vol. 1, p. 283). He passes on Todhunter's memory aid as well: "In order to assist the student in remembering which of these two propositions [I. 18, 19] is demonstrated directly and which indirectly, it may be observed that the order is similar to that in I. 5 and I. 6" (vol. 1, p. 283). I.18, like I.5, goes straight at its conclusion; I.19, like I.6, will get there by *reductio*.

**A citation Euclid leaves out.** Euclid brackets I.3 and I.16, but "the angle ADB is equal to the angle ABD, since the side AB is equal to AD" is I.5, given without a reference. In Lean the step has to be cited, so `Prop18.lean` imports I.5 and applies it to the isosceles triangle ABD. I.5 as stated also speaks of the angles under the base, so it asks for AB and AD produced; `Prop5.base` produces them, and only the first half of I.5 is used. Nor does the exterior angle need Postulate 2 here: the side CD of the triangle BCD is already produced, to A, because I.3 put D between A and C. `Triangle.of_line` makes BCD a triangle, since B is off the straight line AC on which C and D lie.

**What Lean made us say.** The figure settles three things Euclid never states. The angle DCB, which I.16 compares with the exterior angle, is the angle ACB of the triangle, because D lies on the ray from C through A (`OnRay.of_between` and `angle_onRay_left`, from System E's Diagram-angle 4). And "the angle ABC is much greater" rests on BD falling within the angle ABC, so that the angle ABD is only a part of it. Two Book Zero lemmas prove this: since D lies between A and C, the angle ABC is made up of ABD and DBC (`angle_add_of_between`, from Pasch 2 and Diagram-angle 2), and DBC is something, because DBC is a triangle, B being off the straight line AC on which D and C lie (`Triangle.angle_pos`, from Diagram-angle 1). Then Common Notion 5, the whole greater than the part, and `lt_trans`, the law of magnitudes that lets "greater than the greater" pass to "much greater". These rules are Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma's System E, read alongside LeanEuclid's Lean version by Logan Murphy, Kaiyu Yang, Jialiang Sun, Zhaoyu Li, Anima Anandkumar and Xujie Si.

**Porphyry's proof.** Heath reports that "Porphyry gave an alternative proof of I. 18 to meet a childish objection which is supposed to require the part of AC equal to AB to be cut off from CA and not from AC" (vol. 1, p. 24). The proof itself, preserved by Proclus, "is interesting" (vol. 1, p. 283): make CD, not AD, equal to AB, produce AB to E so that BE is equal to AD, and join EC. Then AE is equal to AC, so the angle AEC is equal to the angle ACE; "Now the angle ABC is greater than the angle AEC [I. 16], and therefore greater than the angle ACE. Hence, *a fortiori*, the angle ABC is greater than the angle ACB" (vol. 1, p. 284). In Lean the objection never arises: `Prop3` cuts off from whichever end it is handed, and Euclid hands it A.

**Without the parallel postulate.** Everything here goes back to I.16, which rests nowhere on Postulate 5, and so I.18 holds in the geometry of Bolyai and Lobachevsky as well; CI checks that Postulate 5 is not on its certificate.

**The modern way.** Name the sides a = BC, b = CA, c = AB. The law of cosines gives cos C − cos B = (b − c)(b + c − a)(a + b + c)/(2abc), and b + c is greater than a (that is I.20), so when b is greater than c, cos B is less than cos C and the angle B is the greater.
