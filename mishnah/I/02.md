**A compass that closes.** Why can't we simply draw a circle about A with radius BC? Proclus calls that a *petitio principii*, a begging of the question (Heath, vol. 1, p. 246): Postulate 3 lets us draw a circle with a given centre through a given point, and nothing more. Augustus De Morgan, quoted by Heath, asks us to "suppose the compasses to close of themselves the moment they cease to touch the paper. These two propositions [I. 2, 3] extend the power of construction to what it would have been if *all* the usual power of the compasses had been assumed" (vol. 1, p. 246). I.2 does its share with an equilateral triangle and two circles.

**One case, or many?** Proclus sorts this proposition into cases by where A lies. Heath answers that "Euclid's method is to give one case only, for choice the most difficult, leaving the reader to supply the rest for himself" (vol. 1, p. 246). Lean can't be left to supply anything, yet our proof covers every position at once: the rules that find G and L say only that DB and DA, produced, cut their circles, wherever they lie. One case it must set apart: A at B itself, with no straight line AB to build on. There, as Heath says of Proclus' point at an end, "we are given what we are required to find" (vol. 1, p. 245).

**Where does L come from?** Euclid produces DA and DB (Postulate 2) before drawing his circles. Lean needs no Postulate 2 here: G and L come straight from the rule that a straight line through the inside of a circle, produced, meets it (System E, Intersections 5). That rule wants A inside the circle GKL, which holds because DA is equal to DB, and DB is only part of DG: the whole is greater than the part, Common Notion 5, used here without a word from Euclid.

**The modern way.** In vectors, the point A + (C − B) is as far from A as C is from B, but sliding BC over to A, parallel to itself, keeps its length only by I.34, which rests on Postulate 5. Euclid's L needs no parallels: it lies on DA produced, at A + |BC| u, where u is the unit vector from D towards A.
