**A compass that closes.** Postulate 3 lets us draw a circle with a given centre through a given point, and nothing more: no carrying a distance from one place to another. Augustus De Morgan, quoted by Heath, asks us to "suppose the compasses to close of themselves the moment they cease to touch the paper. These two propositions [I. 2, 3] extend the power of construction to what it would have been if all the usual power of the compasses had been assumed" (vol. 1, p. 246). I.2 does its share with an equilateral triangle and two circles.

**One case, or many.** Proclus, ever the careful commentator, sorted this proposition into cases by where the point A lies relative to BC. Heath answers that "Euclid's method is to give one case only, for choice the most difficult, leaving the reader to supply the rest for himself" (vol. 1, p. 246). Lean can't be left to supply anything, yet the proof above covers every position at once: the rules that find G and L say only that DB and DA, produced, cut their circles, wherever those circles happen to be. The one case set aside is the trivial one, where A is B itself.

**What Lean needed.** Euclid produces DA and DB (Postulate 2) before drawing his circles. In Lean the points G and L come straight from the rule that a straight line through the inside of a circle, produced, meets it (System E, Intersections 5), so Postulate 2 is not on the list. And A lies inside the circle GKL because DA, being equal to DB, is only part of DG: the whole is greater than the part, Common Notion 5, used here without a word from Euclid.

**The modern way.** In vectors, the point A + (C − B) is as far from A as C is from B: slide BC over to A, parallel to itself. Euclid's L is reached another way: it lies on DA produced, at A + |BC| u, where u is the unit vector from D towards A, and his construction needs no parallels, which wait until I.27.
