**Two cuts, two contradictions.** Euclid takes in turn the two places the equal side can stand. When it adjoins the equal angles, he supposes AB greater than DE and cuts off BG equal to DE: I.4 then makes the angle BCG equal to BCA, "the less to the greater". When it subtends one of them, he supposes BC greater than EF and cuts off BH equal to EF: I.4 now makes the exterior angle BHA equal to the interior and opposite angle BCA, which I.16 forbids. Either way the sides are equal, and I.4 finishes. Our figure, like Heath's, shows G and H together, though each has its own supposition.

**"The distance of ships in the sea."** Proclus tells a story: "Eudemus in his geometrical history refers this theorem to Thales. For he says that, in the method by which they say that Thales proved the distance of ships in the sea, it was necessary to make use of this theorem" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 304). Heath guesses that Thales stood on a tower with an upright stick and a cross-piece on a nail. He pointed the cross-piece at the ship, then turned the stick, still upright, until it pointed at a mark on the shore; the two triangles share the tower and the angles at its ends, so the mark was as far off as the ship (vol. 1, p. 305).

**Which is the greater?** Euclid lets AB be the greater, but it may as well be DE, so we write each argument once, for any two triangles, and use it twice, exchanging the triangles. Euclid gives no citation for "let BG be made equal to DE": it is I.3, and the Lean cites `Prop3` for both cuts. I.3 also puts G between B and A and H between B and C, which is what the contradictions need: BCG is then a part of BCA (`angle_lt_of_between`), and BHA an exterior angle of the triangle AHC.

**An author unknown.** The other way to prove it, by applying one triangle to the other, "was very early discovered", Heath says, at least for the side adjoining the equal angles; an-Nairīzī gives it, "observing that the proof is one which he had found, but of which he did not know the author" (vol. 1, p. 303). Euclid's own proof moves nothing, and leaves superposition inside I.4.

**The modern way.** The sine rule, a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C, settles it at once: the two angles give the third, two right angles less their sum, and the one side then gives the other two. But the third angle comes from I.32, which needs Postulate 5; Euclid's proof does without it.
