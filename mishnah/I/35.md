**"Equality in a new sense."** So Heath heads his note. Until now equal figures were congruent, but here figures "are inferred to be *equal* which are equal in *area* or in *content* but need not be of the same *form*" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 327). I.34, I.29 and I.4 make the triangles EAB, FDC equal; Euclid takes DGE from each and adds GBC to what remains. Beneath Common Notions 2 and 3 Heath finds "the further *tacit* assumption that it is indifferent to *what part* or from *what part* of the same or equal areas the same or equal areas are added or subtracted" (vol. 1, p. 328).

**"He allows this to appear from the figure simply."** Euclid names the triangle DGE without saying that, "in the case which he takes (where AD, EF have no point in common), BE, CD must meet at a point G between the two parallels" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 327). Our `Prop35.figure` proves it. ED is parallel to BC, so `AreParallel.cross_or` makes EB cross DC or EC cross BD, and E and B, on opposite sides of DC, settle which. The same helper puts E between D and F, as "the whole DF" needs, and Lean writes each taking away as an adding.

**"The most difficult of the three possible cases."** Proclus, "observing that Euclid has given only the most difficult of the three possible cases, adds the other two with separate proofs" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 328). When E falls between A and D, he adds the equal triangles to the trapezium EBCD, and so does our `Prop35.between`; `Prop35.same` takes E at D. Exchanging the parallelograms covers each the other way round. Only the cases Euclid leaves out need Common Notion 5, the whole greater than the part, and in them Lean names the points where diameters cross, which Heath leaves unlettered.

**An ancient "Budget of Paradoxes."** Proclus counts I.35 among the paradoxes of mathematics, "since the uninstructed might well regard it as impossible that the area of the parallelograms should remain the same while the length of the sides … may increase indefinitely" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 329). Following Geminus, he puts the cause in "the height of the parallels, which remains the same" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 331).

**The modern way.** The parallelogram on u and v has area |u × v|, where u × v = u₁v₂ − u₂v₁, and u × u = 0. With E on AF, parallel to BC, E − B = (A − B) + t(C − B) for some t, so |(C − B) × (E − B)| = |(C − B) × (A − B)|: one base, one height, one area.
