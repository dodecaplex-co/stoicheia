**"Equality in a new sense."** So Heath heads his note. Until now equal figures were congruent, but here figures "are inferred to be *equal* which are equal in *area* or in *content* but need not be of the same *form*" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 327). I.34, I.29 and I.4 make the triangles EAB, FDC equal; Euclid takes DGE from each and adds GBC to what remains. Beneath Common Notions 2 and 3 Heath finds "the further *tacit* assumption that it is indifferent to *what part* or from *what part* of the same or equal areas the same or equal areas are added or subtracted" (vol. 1, p. 328).

**"He allows this to appear from the figure simply."** Euclid names the triangle DGE without saying that, "in the case which he takes (where AD, EF have no point in common), BE, CD must meet at a point G between the two parallels" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 327). We prove it, from the diameters of the figure EBCD: ED and BC are parallel, so the diameters cross, and G is where they do (`Prop35.figure`). The same step puts E between D and F, as "the whole DF" requires.

Proclus, "observing that Euclid has given only the most difficult of the three possible cases, adds the other two with separate proofs" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 328). When E falls between A and D, he adds the equal triangles to the trapezium EBCD, and so do we, and likewise when E falls at D; exchanging the parallelograms covers each case the other way round. Proclus also counts I.35 among the paradoxes of mathematics, "since the uninstructed might well regard it as impossible that the area of the parallelograms should remain the same while the length of the sides … may increase indefinitely" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 329). Following Geminus, he puts the cause in "the height of the parallels, which remains the same" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 331).

**The modern way.** For vectors $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ in the plane write $\vec{u} \times \vec{v} = u_{1}v_{2} - u_{2}v_{1}$, the determinant of their coordinates. The parallelogram on $\vec{u}$ and $\vec{v}$ has area $\lvert\vec{u} \times \vec{v}\rvert$, and $\vec{u} \times \vec{u} = 0$. With E on AF, parallel to BC, $\mathrm{E - B} = \mathrm{(A - B)} + t\mathrm{(C - B)}$ for some real $t$, so $\lvert\mathrm{(C - B)} \times \mathrm{(E - B)}\rvert = \lvert\mathrm{(C - B)} \times \mathrm{(A - B)}\rvert$: one base, one height, one area.
