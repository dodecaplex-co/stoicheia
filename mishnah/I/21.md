**"But will contain a greater angle."** Two straight lines from B and C to a point D within the triangle are together less than BA, AC, yet contain a greater angle than BAC. Euclid gets both halves from one stroke, BD drawn through to E: then I.20 twice for the lengths, and I.16 twice for the angles. Heath adds a word: "The word 'meeting' is not in the Greek … but it is necessary in English to indicate that they *meet*" (vol. 1, p. 289).

**Why from the ends?** Heath: "The editors generally call attention to the fact that the lines drawn within the triangle in this proposition must be drawn, as the enunciation says, from the *ends* of the side; otherwise it is not necessary that their sum should be less than that of the remaining sides of the triangle" (vol. 1, p. 290). Our proof leans on them: BD, from the end B, is drawn through to AC.

**"The so-called paradoxes."** Pappus goes further, with propositions "selected apparently from 'the so-called paradoxes' of one Erycinus" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 290). "In any triangle, except an equilateral triangle or an isosceles triangle with base less than one of the other sides, it is possible to construct on the base and within the triangle two straight lines the sum of which is equal to the sum of the other two sides of the triangle"; and "the lines may be so constructed that their sum will bear to the sum of the two sides of the triangle any ratio less than 2 : 1" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 290). Such lines need not start from the ends of the base, a freedom I.21 withholds.

**Three sides, three jobs.** In our statement "within the triangle" is three facts of side (`InsideTriangle`), and Lean needed all three. D on A's side of BC and on C's side of AB puts it within the angle ABC, so BD, drawn on, meets AC (`crossbar`, as in I.10). D on B's side of CA keeps it short of E, since B and D lie on one side of AC (`Prop21.through`). And A's side of BC keeps C off BE, so CED is a triangle for I.20 and I.16.

**The modern way.** The points P with PB + PC equal to AB + AC make an ellipse with foci B and C through A, and the triangle lies within it, where the sum is less. The points on A's side of BC that see BC under the angle BAC make an arc of the circle through A, B and C (III.21), and from within that circle, as D is, BC is seen under a greater angle.
