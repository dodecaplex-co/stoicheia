Two straight lines from B and C to a point D within the triangle are together less than BA, AC, yet contain a greater angle than BAC. Euclid gets both halves from one stroke, BD drawn through to E: then I.20 twice for the lengths, and I.16 twice for the angles. Of the enunciation's "meeting", Heath notes: "The word 'meeting' is not in the Greek … but it is necessary in English to indicate that they *meet*" (vol. 1, p. 289).

"The editors generally call attention to the fact that the lines drawn within the triangle in this proposition must be drawn, as the enunciation says, from the *ends* of the side; otherwise it is not necessary that their sum should be less than that of the remaining sides of the triangle" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 290). Proclus shows it simply: in a right-angled triangle ABC, take a point D on BC, cut off DE from DA equal to AB, and bisect AE at F; then CF, FD together are greater than CA, AB (Heath, vol. 1, p. 290). Pappus goes further, with propositions "selected apparently from 'the so-called paradoxes' of one Erycinus" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 290): in any triangle but an equilateral one, or an isosceles one with base less than one of the other sides, two straight lines on the base and within the triangle, not drawn from its ends, can together equal the other two sides, and "the lines may be so constructed that their sum will bear to the sum of the two sides of the triangle any ratio less than 2 : 1" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 290).

Our statement reads "within the triangle" as three facts, D on the inner side of each side, and our proof needs all three: two bring BD, drawn on, to meet AC, as in I.10, and the third keeps D short of E. Euclid's figure shows the three together.

**The modern way.** The points $P$ with $|P - \mathrm{B}| + |P - \mathrm{C}| = \mathrm{AB} + \mathrm{AC}$ make an ellipse with foci B and C through A, and the triangle lies within it, where the sum is less. The points on A's side of BC that see BC under the angle BAC lie on one part of the circumference of the circle through A, B and C (III.21), and from within that circle, as D is, BC is seen under a greater angle.
