**The datum comes first.** I.18 and I.19 are easy to mix up: their enunciations differ, Heath notes, only "in the order of the words". His rule for telling them apart is "that the *datum* comes first and the *quaesitum* second, the datum being in this proposition [I.18] the greater side and in the next the greater angle", and so "in order to keep the proper order in English we must use the passive of the verb in I. 19": the greater angle *is subtended by* the greater side. Aristotle, he adds, quotes the result of I.19 "using the exact wording" in the *Meteorologica*. Todhunter offered a second aid to memory: "In order to assist the student in remembering which of these two propositions [I. 18, 19] is demonstrated directly and which indirectly, it may be observed that the order is similar to that in I. 5 and I. 6" (all vol. 1, p. 283).

**Proof by division.** There is nothing to draw. "Here, says Proclus (p. 318, 16–23), Euclid proves the impossibility 'by means of division' (ἐκ διαιρέσεως). This means simply the separation of different hypotheses, each of which is inconsistent with the truth of the theorem to be proved, and which therefore must be successively shown to be impossible. If a straight line is not greater than a straight line, it must be either equal to it or less; thus in a reductio ad absurdum intended to prove such a theorem as I. 19 it is necessary to dispose successively of two hypotheses inconsistent with the truth of the theorem" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 285). Euclid takes the division itself for granted. In Lean it is the tacit law of magnitudes `lt_trichotomy` (System E §3.5, from Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma), and the last step, from "neither equal nor less" to "greater", is `Classical.byContradiction`, just as at the end of I.6.

**A purely logical deduction.** Heath: "This proposition, like I. 6, can be proved by merely logical deduction from I. 5 and I. 18 taken together, as pointed out by De Morgan." De Morgan's general form, from his *Formal Logic* (1847), p. 25, takes three assertions X, Y, Z "of which it is the property that one or the other must be true, and one only", and three more, P, Q, R, of the same kind; then if X brings P, Y brings Q and Z brings R, "it follows that, when P is true, X is true, when Q is true, Y is true, when R is true, Z is true" (vol. 1, p. 284). Here the first three are "AC equal to, greater than, less than AB", the second three the same for the angles ABC and ACB, and I.5 and I.18 supply the connexions. The Lean proof is De Morgan's argument written out. It cites I.5 and I.18 by their statements alone and needs neither of their proofs.

**What Lean made us say.** Very little, and no helper theorems. Euclid's hypothesis calls the less angle BCA, and his argument calls it ACB. Lean turns an angle round only when told to, with `angle_symm` (System E, Metric 4). Once that is said, each "but it is not" is a law of magnitudes: an angle less than another is not equal to it (`Magnitude.ne_of_lt` in Book Zero), and cannot also be greater than it (`lt_trans`, `lt_irrefl`). And our I.5 says both of Euclid's things at once, the angles under the base as well as those at it, so it wants the equal sides produced to points D and E. Only the angles at the base are needed here, so Book Zero's `produce` (Post. 2) supplies D and E anywhere at all, as in I.7.

**I.18 the other way round.** "Then the angle ABC would also have been less than the angle ACB" is I.18 for the triangle ACB: the same three corners named in another order (`Triangle.acb`), with AB now the greater side. I.18 hands back the angle CBA, which is ABC read from the other arm, so `angle_symm` turns it round once more. This is De Morgan's third case, and it is nothing but I.18 itself with B and C exchanged.

**Heron's direct proof.** Proclus gives a direct proof "which an-Nairīzī also has and attributes to Heron. It requires a lemma and is consequently open to the slight objection of separating a theorem from its converse. But the lemma and proof are worth giving" (vol. 1, p. 285). The lemma: if the bisector of an angle of a triangle cuts the base into unequal parts, the side that meets the greater part is the greater. The proof bisects BC at D, produces AD to E so that DE is equal to AD, and joins BE. Two sides and the vertical angles at D are equal, so BE is equal to AC and the angle DBE to the angle at C, which is less than the angle ABC, that is, ABD. So the bisector of the angle ABE meets AE between A and D, and the lemma makes BE, that is AC, greater than BA (vol. 1, p. 286). Euclid's own route keeps I.19 beside its converse, I.18, as I.6 stands beside I.5.

**The modern way.** Put B at (0, 0), C at (a, 0) and A at (x, y) with y > 0. Then cot B = x/y and cot C = (a − x)/y, so the angle at B is the greater exactly when x < a/2; and AC² − AB² = a(a − 2x), so AC is the greater exactly then too: I.18 and I.19 in one line.
