In Heath's words, "This theorem is of course the *partial* converse of I. 37" (vol. 1, p. 337): I.37's base and its conclusion, equal areas, are the hypotheses here, and its parallels the conclusion. One hypothesis is new. "The additional qualification in this proposition that the triangles must be *on the same side* of the base is necessary because it is not, as in I. 37, involved in the other hypotheses" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 337): equal triangles on opposite sides of BC need not make AD parallel to BC.

Proclus remarks, Heath reports, "that Euclid only converts I. 37 and I. 38 relative to triangles, and omits the converses of I. 35, 36 about parallelograms as unnecessary because it is easy to see that the method would be the same, and therefore the reader may properly be left to prove them for himself" (vol. 1, p. 337). Proclus counts I.40 among Euclid's; its page tells why Heath prints it in brackets. One sentence of I.39 is in brackets too, "I say that they are also in the same parallels." J. L. Heiberg proved from a papyrus fragment, Heath reports, that it was added by someone "who did not observe that the words 'And let AD be joined' are part of the *setting-out*" (vol. 1, p. 337). Taking those words for the construction, the interpolator thought the thing to be proved should be stated first, and altered "And" into "For".

**"The greater to the less."** Suppose, with Euclid, that AD is not parallel to BC, and draw the parallel through A to meet BD at E. Then E cannot be D, since that would make AD parallel to BC. The parallel puts E on A's side of BC, and so on D's: on the ray from B through D. I.37 and the given equality make DBC and EBC equal. If E is between B and D, as in Heath's figure, EBC is a part of DBC and the less; if beyond D, a case Proclus finds equally easy (Heath, vol. 1, p. 337), DBC is the less. Either order makes one triangle strictly less than its equal, a contradiction. So the supposition fails: AD is parallel to BC.

**The modern way.** Put $\mathrm{B} = (0, 0)$ and $\mathrm{C} = (a, 0)$, with $a \gt 0$: a triangle on BC with its vertex at $(x, y)$ has area $\tfrac{1}{2}a|y|$. Equal areas give equal $|y|$, and the same side of BC gives $y$ one sign, so A and D are at one height and AD is parallel to BC.
