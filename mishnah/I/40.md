**Added for symmetry.** Heath prints the whole proposition in square brackets, from its heading "[Proposition 40." to the closing "Q. E. D.]", and explains why: "Heiberg has proved by means of the papyrus-fragment mentioned in the last note that this proposition is an interpolation by some one who thought that there should be a proposition following I. 39 and related to it in the same way as I. 38 is related to I. 37, and I. 36 to I. 35" (vol. 1, p. 338).

**A papyrus without it.** The fragment J. L. Heiberg used, of the second or third century, holds I.39 and I.41 one after the other, "showing that I. 40 was wanting, whereas it is found in all the MSS. and is recognised by Proclus"; it was added "although Euclid nowhere uses I. 40, and therefore was not likely to include it" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 51). Heath takes the same hand to have slipped the bracketed sentence into I.39, and I.40 repeats that sentence and its "For" with no brackets of their own. Heath prints it, and so do we: the argument is I.39's again, with the equal bases BC, CE for the one base and I.38 for I.37.

**Two bases that touch.** Our main theorem takes the figure as it stands, with BC and CE meeting at C, though the enunciation, like I.38's, allows any two equal bases on one straight line. Suppose AD were not parallel to BE. F is where the parallel through A meets CD, and our I.38 makes ABC equal to FCE while letting the bases share C and F be A, so neither needs a case of its own. The supposition makes F distinct from D: if they were one point, the parallel would be AD. Between C and D, F makes FCE less than DCE; beyond D, it makes DCE less than FCE. The same-side condition excludes the opposite ray, so either possible order contradicts the equal areas. The companion `Prop40.general` follows this reductio for equal bases apart, touching, overlapping or one, and allows the two vertices to coincide.

**The modern way.** Let b = C − B, which is also E − C, and take the cross product of I.37. A and D lie on one side of BE, so the signed areas ½ b × (A − B) and ½ b × (D − C) have one sign; their difference is ½ b × (D − A), since b × b = 0, so equal areas make D − A a multiple of b, and AD is parallel to BE.
