**I.13 turned round.** I.13 says that a straight line standing on a straight line makes angles equal to two right angles; I.14 says that two angles equal to two right angles, side by side, put their outer arms in one straight line. Euclid proves it by *reductio ad absurdum*, as in I.6. He supposes that BD is not in a straight line with CB, produces CB to E, and finds the angle ABE equal to the angle ABD, "the less to the greater: which is impossible." In Lean the supposition becomes "D is not on the ray from B through E", the argument runs as a sub-proof under it, and `Classical.byContradiction` turns its failure into "D does lie on that ray", from which `Prop14.straight` gives the conclusion. Producing CB is `produce` in Book Zero, so Postulates 1 and 2 are on the certificate, though Euclid gives no bracket there. Later, I.45 and I.47 will lean on I.14 to show that two straight lines they have built are one.

**"Not lying on the same side."** The condition matters, and Heath, following Proclus, says why: "two straight lines on the *same* side of another straight line and meeting it in one and the same point may make with one and the same portion of the straight line terminated at the point two angles which are together equal to two right angles, in which case however the two straight lines would not be in a straight line with one another." Proclus quotes a construction from Porphyry, with angles of half a right angle and one and a half right angles, though Heath adds that "there is no particular value in the construction" (vol. 1, p. 277). In Lean the condition is the hypothesis `OppositeSidesOf C D A B`, and it is used exactly once: to put D on the same side of AB as E, where the last step needs it.

**Post. 4 and C.N. 1.** Heath: "Todhunter points out that for the inference in this line Post. 4, that all right angles are equal, is necessary as well as the Common Notion that things which are equal to the same thing (or rather, here, to *equal things*) are equal. A similar remark applies to steps in the proofs of I. 15 and I. 28" (vol. 1, p. 277). In Lean, Postulate 4 has done its work before I.14 begins: every right angle is written with the one name `∟`, the size Postulate 4 says they all share, and I.13 hands its two right angles back as `∟ + ∟`. So the step itself is `CommonNotion1`, and the subtraction that follows is `CommonNotion3`.

**The less to the greater.** In Euclid's figure BE falls within the angle ABD, so the angle ABE is a part of the angle ABD and cannot be equal to it. Lean has to allow for the other figure too, in which BD falls within ABE. Both are settled by `onRay_of_angle_eq` in Book Zero, the fact that I.4 already used to lay AC along DF: on one side of a straight line, only one ray from a point of it makes a given angle with it. Its proof is Euclid's argument here in general form. System E's Triple incidence 2 says which ray lies within the other's angle, Diagram-angle 2 makes that angle a whole of two parts, and Common Notion 5 makes the whole greater than the part. Heath's figure, as a figure for a *reductio* often must, does not show what is supposed. It draws CBD straight, as it really is, and BE bent away from it. The Lean argues from the figure as supposed: E on CB produced, and D off the ray BE.

**What Lean made us say.** Three facts Euclid reads off his figure. Two are in Book Zero. `OppositeSidesOf.of_between`: since B lies between C and E, the straight line AB separates them, so E is on the other side of AB from C (System E, Pasch 3). `OppositeSidesOf.sameSideOf`: a straight line has only two sides, so E and D, each on the other side from C, are on the same side as each other (Same side 5). The third is a helper theorem in the file, `Prop14.straight`: a point on the ray from B through E is in a straight line with CB, which is the last step from "D lies along BE" to "B lies between C and D". One more small fact sits inside the proof, the word "stands": I.13 needs A off the straight line CBE, which holds because A, B, C are not on one straight line. Every rule cited is one of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma's in System E, read, as always, with LeanEuclid's careful Lean version open alongside.

**"Similarly we can prove."** Euclid refutes one rival straight line BE and then says the same goes for "any other straight line except BD". Heath notes that "the Greek expresses this by the future of the verb, δείξομεν, 'we shall prove,' which however would perhaps be misleading in English" (vol. 1, p. 277). Lean needs no "similarly": E was any point at all on CB produced, and the argument never asked which. Heath's notes on I.14 open with a translator's confession, too: "There is no greater difficulty in translating the works of the Greek geometers than that of accurately giving the force of prepositions." The one word πρός, in this one enunciation, has to be rendered "with", "at" and "on" (vol. 1, p. 277).

**The modern way.** Put B at 0 and A at 1 in the complex plane, and C = r e^{iθ} above the real axis. Then D, below it and making the angle π − θ with BA, is s e^{−i(π − θ)} = −s e^{iθ}, a negative real multiple of C, so B lies between C and D. Had D been above the axis, it would be s e^{i(π − θ)} = −s e^{−iθ}, the mirror image of that point, and in general not in line with C at all: that is Proclus' warning.
