**I.13 turned round.** Euclid now proves the converse of I.13, and it is how he will know that straight lines run straight on: in I.47 it lines up CA and AG, a side of the triangle and a side of a square, and in I.45 the sides of parallelograms set side by side. He argues by *reductio ad absurdum*: suppose BD is not in a straight line with CB, produce CB to E, and the angle ABE comes out equal to the angle ABD, "the less to the greater: which is impossible." As in I.6, we follow him under the supposition as far as the impossible, and then turn it round.

**"Not lying on the same side."** Proclus gives the reason: "two straight lines on the *same* side of another straight line … may make … two angles which are together equal to two right angles, in which case however the two straight lines would not be in a straight line with one another" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 277). We need the condition once, at "the less to the greater": E and D, both across AB from C, lie on one side of it, where just one ray from B makes a given angle with BA (`onRay_of_angle_eq`), or one of two equal angles would be part of the other. So D lies along BE, whether BE falls within the angle ABD, as Euclid draws it, or BD within ABE.

**Whose two right angles?** The two right angles of I.13 are not, on their face, the two of the hypothesis. Isaac Todhunter, Heath reports, "points out that for the inference in this line Post. 4, that all right angles are equal, is necessary as well as the Common Notion that things which are equal to the same thing (or rather, here, to *equal things*) are equal. A similar remark applies to steps in the proofs of I. 15 and I. 28" (vol. 1, p. 277). In our Lean, as I.13 showed, every right angle is already the one `∟`, so the step is Common Notion 1 alone, and the subtraction after it Common Notion 3.

**The modern way.** Put B at 0 and A at 1 in the complex plane and C at r(cos θ + i sin θ), above the real axis: D, below it and making the angle π − θ with BA, is s(cos(θ − π) + i sin(θ − π)) = −s(cos θ + i sin θ), so B lies between C and D. Were D above the axis, it would be s(−cos θ + i sin θ), in general not in a straight line with CB: that is Proclus' warning.
