**"Within the angle ACD."** I.17, I.18 and I.21 lean on I.16, and I.27 draws its parallels with it. Its proof rests on one step that only the figure supports: "the angle ECD is greater than the angle ECF", by Common Notion 5, the whole greater than the part. For that, Heath says, "it is necessary that CF should always fall within the angle ACD" (vol. 1, p. 280).

**"Returns on itself."** It need not fall there. "As is well known, this proposition is not universally true under the Riemann hypothesis of a space endless in extent but not infinite in size. On this hypothesis a straight line is a 'closed series' and returns on itself" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 280). In that sphere-like geometry, met under I.12, a long enough BE brings F round onto CD, making the exterior angle equal to the interior one, and a longer BE carries F past, making it less.

**What keeps F inside?** So we prove where F falls (`Prop16.within`). F is on D's side of AC, because AC separates B from F (E lies between them) and B from D (C lies between them), and a straight line has only two sides. F is on E's side of BC, because BF sets out from B, on BC, and passes through E, off it, on its way to F. These rules of order and sides (System E, Pasch 2, Pasch 3 and Same side 5) are what a space whose straight lines return on themselves does without. As in I.11, `produce_longer` gives us room on BE produced for I.3 to cut EF off.

**Three equal straight lines?** I.16 answers the critic of I.12: "The present proposition enables Proclus to prove what he did not succeed in establishing conclusively in his note on I. 12, namely that *from one point there cannot be drawn to the same straight line three straight lines equal in length*" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 280). Were there three, I.5 in the two isosceles triangles they make would give a triangle an exterior angle equal to an interior and opposite one.

**The modern way.** Put C at 0, B at −1 and A at a, above them, so that D lies along the positive real axis: the exterior angle ACD is arg a, the angles at B and A are arg(a + 1) and arg(a/(a + 1)), all between 0 and π, and since a = (a + 1) · a/(a + 1), the first is the sum of the other two. That is I.32, which needs the parallel postulate and is not Euclid's route here; I.16 is the part of it that holds without.
