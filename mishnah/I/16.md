**Where F falls.** "But the angle ECD is greater than the angle ECF," says Euclid, citing Common Notion 5, the whole greater than the part. That is only so if CF falls within the angle ECD, and nothing but the figure says it does. Heath puts it sharply: "As is well known, this proposition is not universally true under the Riemann hypothesis of a space endless in extent but not infinite in size. On this hypothesis a straight line is a 'closed series' and returns on itself." And so, "in order that the proof of the present proposition may be universally valid, it is necessary that CF should always fall within the angle ACD so that the angle ACF may be less than the angle ACD. But this will not always be so on the Riemann hypothesis" (vol. 1, p. 280). (Heath's "Riemann hypothesis" is Riemann's geometry of a closed, sphere-like space, what we now call elliptic geometry; it has nothing to do with the famous conjecture about prime numbers.) In such a space, if BE is long enough, F comes right round onto CD, or even past it.

**What Lean made us say.** So the place where F falls has to be proved, and `Prop16.within` proves it. F is on D's side of AC, because AC separates B from F (E lies between them) and B from D (C lies between them), and a straight line has only two sides. F is on E's side of BC, because BF sets out from B, on BC, and passes through E, off it, on its way to F. These are System E's Pasch rules 2 and 3 and its fifth rule for sides, rules about the order of points and the sides of a straight line that a space whose straight lines return on themselves does without. Then `Prop16.part` reads the angles off: with F within the angle ECD, that angle is made up of ECF and FCD (Diagram-angle 2), and FCD is something, since F is off the straight line CD (Diagram-angle 1). Only then does Common Notion 5 apply. Two Book Zero lemmas finish the step: the angle ECD is the angle ACD, and BAE is BAC, because E lies on the rays from C through A and from A through C (`angle_onRay_left`, `angle_onRay_right`). Every one of these rules is Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma's, and LeanEuclid's careful Lean version of System E, by Logan Murphy, Kaiyu Yang, Jialiang Sun, Zhaoyu Li, Anima Anandkumar and Xujie Si, was open beside us the whole way.

**Far enough to cut off.** "Let EF be made equal to BE [I. 3]." But I.3 cuts off from the greater of two straight lines, and Postulate 2 produces BE without saying how far. `Prop16.beyond` makes sure it is far enough: produce EB beyond B to a point P (Book Zero's `produce`), describe the circle with centre E and distance EP (Post. 3), and take X where BE produced cuts it (System E, Intersections 5). Then EX is equal to EP [Def. 15], which is greater than EB, and I.3 can cut EF off from EX. One fact of order, `between_inner`, then says that E lies between B and F. That is why Postulate 3 and Definition 15 are on the certificate, though Euclid cites neither here.

**"Similarly."** Euclid proves the angle BAC less than the exterior angle, and for the angle ABC says only that, "if BC be bisected", it "can be proved" less "as well". As in I.6, the Lean gives his argument once, for every triangle with a side produced, and uses it twice: first in his own figure, then in the triangle BAC, with AC produced to G. That is what G is for. Euclid draws AC through to G (the word, Heath notes, is "a variation on the more usual" one for "let it be produced", vol. 1, p. 280) only so that the second use has an exterior angle, BCG, and I.15 makes BCG equal to ACD, since AG and BD cut one another at C.

**Without the parallel postulate.** Like everything before I.29, I.16 rests nowhere on Postulate 5, and CI checks that. Heath reports that Proclus hints elsewhere "at the possibility that, while I. 16 may remain universally true", two straight lines might be turned through an angle "and yet may not meet (the Bolyai-Lobachewsky hypothesis)" (vol. 1, p. 281). It is a theorem of the geometry of János Bolyai and Nikolai Lobachevsky as much as of Euclid's. Proclus also put it to work: it lets him prove "that from one point there cannot be drawn to the same straight line three straight lines equal in length" (vol. 1, p. 280), for if AB, AC, AD were equal, with B, C, D on one straight line, I.5 would make the exterior angle ACB equal to the interior angle ADC, "which is impossible" (vol. 1, p. 281).

**The modern way.** Put C at 0 and B at −1, so that D lies along the positive real axis, and let A be the complex number a, above it. The exterior angle ACD is arg a, the angle at B is arg(a + 1), and the angle at A is arg(a/(a + 1)); all three lie between 0 and π, and since a = (a + 1) · a/(a + 1), the first is the sum of the other two. That is already I.32, which needs the parallel postulate; I.16 is the part of it that holds without.
