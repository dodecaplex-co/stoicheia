**"Depends only upon I. 27."** Heath: "The construction, be it observed, depends only upon I. 27, and might therefore have come directly after that proposition. Why then did Euclid postpone it until after I. 29 and I. 30? Presumably because he considered it necessary, before giving the construction, to place beyond all doubt the fact that only one such parallel can be drawn" (vol. 1, p. 316). Our proof bears out his first sentence. It rests on I.23 and I.27, as Euclid's does, and not on Postulate 5: in Book I, I.31 is the one proposition after I.28 that does without it.

**A parallel, or the parallel?** Proclus, Heath reports, draws the uniqueness from I.30: two straight lines through A, both parallel to BC, would be parallel to each other, though they meet at A. Heath thinks Euclid "deliberately determined that his own assumption was more appropriate to be made the subject of a Postulate than the assumption of the uniqueness of the parallel" (vol. 1, p. 316). That uniqueness later took John Playfair's name, though "it was of course not a new discovery. It is distinctly stated in Proclus' note to Eucl. I. 31" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 220). Our statement asks for a parallel, not the only one; that there is no other is `Prop30.unique`, proved from I.29.

**"At random on BC."** We take Euclid's D between B and C, as his figure does, so that B and C lie on either side of AD, and ask our I.23 for its angle on B's side. E then falls across AD from C, and the angles EAD, ADC are alternate, as I.27 asks.

**The modern way.** The parallel through A is the straight line of points A + t(C − B), for every real number t. Euclid's E is one of these with t negative, on B's side of AD, and F one with t positive.
