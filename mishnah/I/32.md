**Two angles carried to C.** Euclid draws CE through C parallel to AB, and I.29 carries the angle at A across to ACE and the angle at B up to ECD. Together they fill the exterior angle ACD; add the angle ACB to each, and I.13 makes the three angles two right angles. Without Postulate 5, I.17 lets us say only that any two of them fall short of two right angles. With it, the shortfall is exactly the third.

**The Pythagoreans' parallel.** Eudemus, Proclus reports, credited the theorem and its proof to the Pythagoreans, who drew the parallel to BC through A (Heath, vol. 1, p. 320). Aristotle knew Euclid's own construction, as J. L. Heiberg saw: "If then the parallel to the side had been *drawn up* (ἀνῆκτο), the fact would at once have been clear from merely looking at the figure" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 321). Heath adds that "'drawn *upwards*' applied to the parallel to a side can only indicate Euclid's" (vol. 1, p. 321).

**Which end of the parallel?** Euclid's figure puts E inside the angle ACD, and Lean asks for a reason. Our I.31 gives a point of the parallel on either side of C, and so on either side of BC, and we take the one on A's side (`Prop32.choose_end`). E then lies across AC from B, or the parallel would run into the triangle and cross AB (`AreParallel.oppositeSidesOf`). D is across AC from B too, so E lies within the angle ACD, and `angle_add_of_within` makes ACD the sum of ACE and ECD.

**A straight line that turns.** Bernhard Friedrich Thibaut turned a straight line about each corner of the triangle in turn: back on the straight line it began on, it has turned through four right angles, the three exterior angles together. Heath objects that it turns about different points: "it is necessary to assume as an axiom that the two motions are independent" (vol. 1, p. 321). Heinrich Christian Schumacher tried to make it a turning about one point, and Carl Friedrich Gauss replied that he had assumed a proposition which is, "in essence, the very proposition to be proved" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 321). Euclid moves his angles with I.29, and pays with Postulate 5.

**The modern way.** Put A, B, C anticlockwise in the complex plane. The angles at A, B, C are the arguments of (C − A)/(B − A), (A − B)/(C − B) and (B − C)/(A − C), each between 0 and π, and those three numbers multiply to −1. So the angles add up to an odd multiple of π, and as the sum lies between 0 and 3π, to π: two right angles.
