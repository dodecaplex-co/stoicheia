Euclid draws nothing here. He argues by division, as in I.19: if the angle BAC is not greater than the angle EDF, it is equal to it or less. Equal angles would give equal bases, by I.4, and a less angle a less base, by I.24; but BC is greater than EF. So I.25 is I.24 turned round, and the two together say that when two triangles have two sides equal to two sides, the greater angle and the greater base go together.

Heath passes on Augustus De Morgan's view: "this proposition (as also I. 8) is a purely *logical* consequence of I. 4 and I. 24 in the same way as I. 19 and I. 6 are purely *logical* consequences of I. 18 and I. 5" (vol. 1, p. 300). Our proof bears him out. It builds no figure, and beyond I.4 and I.24 it uses only the three tacit rules of magnitude that carried I.19's division, `lt_trichotomy`, `lt_trans` and `lt_irrefl`. The logic covers I.8 as well, but Euclid could not have proved I.8 that way, since I.24 itself rests on I.8, through I.23. Heath also reports two direct proofs from Proclus, by Menelaus of Alexandria and by Heron, "and they are both interesting" (vol. 1, p. 300); each has to show where a new point falls, so that one angle is a part of the other, and Euclid's division never looks at a figure.

**The modern way.** With the sides $b$ and $c$ fixed, the law of cosines gives the angle $\theta$ they contain from the base $a$: $\theta = \arccos\left(\frac{b^{2} + c^{2} - a^{2}}{2bc}\right)$. As $a$ grows the fraction falls, and since the cosine falls as $\theta$ grows from $0$ to $\pi$, the angle rises: the greater base has the greater angle. Read the other way, the same formula is I.24.
