**By division.** Euclid argues as in I.19, by division, as Proclus calls the method there: if the angle BAC is not greater than the angle EDF, it is equal to it or less. Equal angles would give equal bases by I.4, a less angle a less base by I.24, and BC is greater than EF. Heath passes on Augustus De Morgan's view of the pair: "this proposition (as also I. 8) is a purely *logical* consequence of I. 4 and I. 24 in the same way as I. 19 and I. 6 are purely *logical* consequences of I. 18 and I. 5" (vol. 1, p. 300). The logic covers I.8 as well, but Euclid could not have proved I.8 that way, since I.24 itself rests on I.8, through I.23.

**What Lean made us say.** The division is the tacit law of magnitudes `lt_trichotomy`, and Euclid's last step, "therefore the angle BAC is greater than the angle EDF", is `Classical.byContradiction`, as in I.19. Euclid's I.24 has the greater angle in its first triangle; here the angle supposed greater is EDF, so the proof cites `Prop24` with DEF first and ABC second, and reads AB equal to DE the other way round, DE equal to AB (`Eq.symm`). Each "but it is not" is a law of magnitudes: BC, being greater than EF, is not equal to it (`Magnitude.ne_of_lt`), nor less (`lt_trans`, `lt_irrefl`).

**What it rests on.** There is no figure to build and no helper to write: the proof's own steps rest on three tacit laws of magnitudes, `lt_trichotomy`, `lt_trans` and `lt_irrefl`, and nothing more. The certificate's longer list, Postulates 1 to 4, Definition 15 and `superposition` among it, comes in with I.4 and I.24. Postulate 5 is not on it.

**Two direct proofs.** Heath reports two more from Proclus, by Menelaus of Alexandria and by Heron, "and they are both interesting. Moreover both are *direct*" (vol. 1, p. 300). Heron sets off along EF produced a length equal to BC, and with two circles builds on DE a triangle with the three sides of ABC. Its angle at D is equal to the angle BAC (I.8), and the angle EDF is a part of it. A direct proof has to show where its new point falls, to make one angle a part of the other; Euclid's division looks at no figure at all.

**The modern way.** With b and c the equal sides and θ the angle they contain, the base a satisfies cos θ = (b² + c² − a²)/(2bc). With b and c fixed, the greater base gives the less cosine, and between nothing and two right angles the less cosine belongs to the greater angle.
