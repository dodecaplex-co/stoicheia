**"Either equal to it or less."** Euclid needs no construction here. He argues by division, as in I.19: if the angle BAC is not greater than the angle EDF, it is either equal to it or less. Equal angles would give equal bases by I.4, a less angle a less base by I.24, and BC is greater than EF. So I.25 is the converse of I.24, and the pair together say that when two triangles have two sides equal, the greater angle and the greater base go together.

**"As also I. 8."** Heath passes on Augustus De Morgan's view: "this proposition (as also I. 8) is a purely *logical* consequence of I. 4 and I. 24 in the same way as I. 19 and I. 6 are purely *logical* consequences of I. 18 and I. 5" (vol. 1, p. 300). The logic covers I.8 as well, but Euclid could not have proved I.8 that way, since I.24 itself rests on I.8, through I.23.

**Nothing to draw.** The Lean bears De Morgan out. We build no figure and write no helper: beyond I.4 and I.24, our proof needs only the three tacit rules of magnitude that carried I.19's division (`lt_trichotomy`, `lt_trans`, `lt_irrefl`).

**Can it be proved directly?** Heath reports two more proofs from Proclus, by Menelaus of Alexandria and by Heron, "and they are both interesting. Moreover both are *direct*" (vol. 1, p. 300). Heron sets off along EF produced a length equal to BC, and with two circles builds on DE a triangle with the three sides of ABC. Its angle at D is equal to the angle BAC (I.8), and the angle EDF is a part of it. A direct proof must show where its new point falls, so that one angle is a part of the other; Euclid's division never looks at a figure.

**The modern way.** I.24 says that, with the sides b and c fixed, the base a grows with the angle θ they contain; I.25 runs the law of cosines backwards, θ = arccos((b² + c² − a²)/(2bc)). As a grows the fraction falls, and as the fraction falls its arccos rises: the greater base has the greater angle.
