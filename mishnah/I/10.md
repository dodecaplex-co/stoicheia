**Where the bisector meets AB.** Euclid bisects the angle ACB by the straight line CD and at once treats D as a point of AB. Why should the bisector reach AB at all, and why between A and B rather than beyond one end? The figure makes it look too obvious to say. In Lean, I.9 hands back a straight line CF with F within the angle ACB, on B's side of CA and on A's side of CB; that is half of what "bisected by the straight line" means, and our I.9 says it for exactly this use. Then [`crossbar`](../../Elements/Figure/Angles.lean) in Book Zero, the "crossbar theorem" of the geometry books that follow Hilbert, finds the point D where the ray from C through F crosses AB. It is proved from the System E of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma: by triple incidence rule 1 the straight line CF separates A from B, so it crosses AB (Intersections 1), and Pasch's rules put the crossing between A and B, and on F's side of C.

**The halves are still the halves.** I.9 makes the angles ACF and FCB equal, but Euclid's equal angles are ACD and BCD. They are the same angles, because D lies on the ray from C through F, and an angle depends only on its rays (`angle_onRay_right`, `angle_onRay_left`, System E's diagram-angle rule 4). One turn of `angle_symm` reads FCB the other way round, as BCD.

**What else Lean made us say.** Very little. I.1 lets us name the side of AB on which the triangle is to stand; here no side matters, so it is given A, a point of AB itself. "AC is equal to CB" is read off Definition 20, since an equilateral triangle has AB equal to BC and BC to CA. "CD is common" is a length equal to itself. And before I.4 can be cited, CAD and CBD must be triangles: D is on AB and C is off it (`Triangle.of_line`), which is why Lean names the straight line AB at the start. No helper theorems were needed. The whole proof is I.1, I.9, `crossbar` and I.4, in Euclid's order, and it does not wait for I.9's own proof: a proof needs only the statements it cites.

**Apollonius' way.** Heath reports that "Apollonius, we are told (Proclus, pp. 279, 16–280, 4), bisected a straight line AB by a construction like that of I. 1": two circles about A and B with radius AB, meeting at C and D, and CD joined. Apollonius shows first, by I.8, that CD bisects the angle ACB, and then, by I.4, that it bisects AB. This is the construction taught with ruler and compass today. Heath's objection is a matter of method: "instead of assuming the bisection of the angle ACB, as already effected by I. 9, Apollonius goes a step further back and embodies a construction for bisecting the angle. That is, he unnecessarily does over again what has been done before, which is open to objection from a theoretical point of view" (vol. 1, p. 268). In Lean, citing I.9 is exactly assuming it.

**Lines without indivisible parts.** Proclus, Heath tells us, "warns us against being moved by this proposition to conclude that geometers assumed, as a preliminary hypothesis, that a line is not made up of indivisible parts" (vol. 1, p. 268). Following Geminus, Proclus answers that it is a common notion that whatever is continuous is divisible, while dividing without end is something geometers prove: "The writer of the Elements bisects a finite straight line, starting from the latter notion, and not from an assumption that it is divisible without limit." He adds that the proposition may also serve to refute Xenocrates' theory of indivisible lines (vol. 1, p. 268). Our foundations have the plain rule in the spirit of that common notion, that a point may be taken between any two points of a straight line (System E, Points 3). The proof of I.10 itself never calls on it, though: its D is simply where two straight lines cross.

**The modern way.** The midpoint of AB is (A + B)/2, in coordinates ((x₁ + x₂)/2, (y₁ + y₂)/2). Euclid's figure agrees: with A at −1 and B at 1 in the complex plane, C is √3 i, the bisector of the angle ACB is the imaginary axis, and it meets AB at 0, halfway between −1 and 1.
