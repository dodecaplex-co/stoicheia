**Where the bisector meets AB.** Euclid bisects the angle ACB by the straight line CD and at once treats D as a point of AB. Why should the bisector reach AB at all, and between A and B rather than beyond one end? In Lean, I.9 hands back a bisector that falls within the angle ACB, which our I.9 says for exactly this use. Book Zero's [`crossbar`](../../Elements/Figure/Angles.lean), the crossbar theorem of the geometry books that follow Hilbert, then finds D: the bisector's straight line separates A from B (System E, Triple incidence 1), so it crosses AB (Intersection 1), and Pasch's rules put the crossing between A and B, on the ray from C along the bisector. So the angles ACD and BCD are the halves I.9 made equal (`angle_onRay_right`, `angle_onRay_left`).

**What else Lean made us say.** Very little. No side of AB matters here, so I.1 is given A, a point of AB itself, as the side for its triangle. Before I.4 can be cited, CAD and CBD must be triangles: D is on AB and C is off it (`Triangle.of_line`), so the proof uses Postulate 1 to name AB at the start (I.1, I.4 and I.9 bring it in as well). The whole proof is I.1, I.9, `crossbar` and I.4, in Euclid's order.

**Apollonius' way.** Heath reports that "Apollonius, we are told (Proclus, pp. 279, 16–280, 4), bisected a straight line AB by a construction like that of I. 1" (vol. 1, p. 268): two circles about A and B with radius AB, meeting at C and D, and CD joined, which I.8 shows bisects the angle ACB and I.4 then shows bisects AB. It is the construction taught with ruler and compass today. Heath's objection is one of method: Apollonius "unnecessarily does over again what has been done before, which is open to objection from a theoretical point of view" (vol. 1, p. 268). In Lean, citing I.9 is exactly assuming it.

**Lines without indivisible parts.** Proclus, Heath tells us, "warns us against being moved by this proposition to conclude that geometers assumed, as a preliminary hypothesis, that a line is not made up of indivisible parts" (vol. 1, p. 268). Nor does our I.10 assume anything of the kind: its D is simply where two straight lines cross.

**The modern way.** The midpoint of AB is (A + B)/2. Euclid's figure agrees: with A at −1 and B at 1 in the complex plane, C is √3 i, the bisector of the angle ACB is the imaginary axis, and it meets AB at 0, halfway between −1 and 1.
