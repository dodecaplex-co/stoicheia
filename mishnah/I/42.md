**Half the base does it.** Here Euclid turns a triangle into a parallelogram of the same size, in any angle he is given. He halves the base BC at E (it is I.10, though Heath gives no bracket) and builds the parallelogram FECG on EC, in the given angle and between the same parallels. FECG is double of the triangle AEC, by I.41; and so is ABC, since its halves ABE and AEC are equal, by I.38. I.44 will carry such a parallelogram onto any straight line, and I.45 will make one equal to any rectilineal figure, any figure bounded by straight lines.

**Why are the two doubles equal?** Euclid gives no reason why FECG is equal to ABC. Some manuscripts give this a Common Notion of its own, that things double of the same thing are equal; Proclus excludes it, Heath reports, because it follows from Common Notion 1 (vol. 1, p. 223). We follow Proclus: each double is AEC added to itself, and Common Notion 1 makes them equal. Heath, agreeing with Paul Tannery, sees no reason to strike out the like words in I.47: "If they were interpolated, we should have expected to find the same interpolation in I. 42, where the axiom is *tacitly* assumed" (vol. 1, p. 224).

**Where does F fall?** Our I.23 makes the angle at E on A's side of EC, and F is where its arm, produced, meets the parallel through A. F lies on A's side of BC, so `OnRay.of_sameSideOf` puts it on the arm itself, and CEF is the very angle I.23 made. A may fall at F or at G, for I.41 asks only that A lie on the straight line FG.

**The modern way.** Put E at the midpoint of BC, let h be A's height above BC, and let u be the unit vector making the given angle θ with C − E on A's side. Then F = E + (h/sin θ)u and G = C + (F − E). The parallelogram has area |EC|·h = ½|BC|·h, the area of ABC.
