**What the complements fill.** The Greek word for the complements means, in Heath's rendering, "the figures put in to fill up (interstices)" (vol. 1, p. 341). The parallelograms EH and FG lie about the diameter AC, and BK and KD fill up the rest of ABCD. In Euclid's figure the two parallelograms meet at one point K of AC, and our statement asks for that. Proclus also lets them stand apart or overlap, and then, Heath notes, "the complements need not be parallelograms" (vol. 1, p. 341): when the parallelograms stand apart the complements have five sides, and when they overlap each is, as Heath counts it, a parallelogram less a triangle. In every case they are still equal, as Proclus shows.

**Equal wholes, equal parts.** Euclid uses I.34 three times: the diameter AC halves ABCD, AK halves EH, and KC halves FG. Common Notion 2 adds the small equal triangles, and Common Notion 3 takes those sums from the equal triangles ABC and ADC, leaving the complements equal. The certificate cites I.34 alone, Euclid's one bracket. The proof uses Postulate 1 to name AB, AC and BK for cutting the triangle (I.34 brings it in as well), and Postulate 5 comes in through I.34, which rests on I.29.

**What remains.** Euclid takes for granted that the triangle ABC is made up of the triangle AEK, the complement BK and the triangle KGC, so that BK is what remains. `Prop43.cut` proves it from the three betweennesses alone. Our `quadArea` measures EBGK by its diameter EG, but the cut runs along BK. BK separates E from G, so the two diameters cross (`OppositeSides.exists_between`), at a point between B and K (`Between.of_inside`) that has a name in Lean and no letter in the figure. Then either diameter measures the complement (`quadArea_rotate_of_cross`), and `area_cut` gives the three pieces. The same argument cuts ADC. Lean never needs the complements to be parallelograms, only their diameters to cross.

**The modern way.** Put A at the origin, AB = u, AD = v and K = su + tv, with 0 < s, t < 1. The complements have areas (1−s)t|u×v| and s(1−t)|u×v|, equal exactly when s = t, that is, when K lies on AC. Euclid proves that K on AC makes them equal; the converse is not his.
