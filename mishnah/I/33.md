**"The connecting link."** Euclid joins the ends of two equal parallels and finds the joining straight lines equal and parallel too. I.33 is, as Heath reports Proclus saying, "the connecting link between the exposition of the theory of parallels and the investigation of parallelograms", and Heath says why: it "gives, without expressing the fact, the construction or origin of the parallelogram, so that in the next proposition Euclid is able to speak of 'parallelogrammic areas' without any further explanation" (vol. 1, p. 323).

**"In the same directions."** The words in parentheses in the enunciation are Heath's own: "I have for clearness' sake inserted the words in brackets though they are not in the original Greek" (vol. 1, p. 323). Everything hangs on them: join A to D and B to C instead, and the joining straight lines cross, so they are never parallel. Our statement gives the words their force with `SameSideOf`: B and D lie on one side of AC. The same words put A and D on opposite sides of BC, as in I.32, and so make the angles there alternate.

**"He evidently prefers the alphabetical order."** Heath writes DC, CB and DCB where the Greek has BC, CD and BCD, for "Euclid is not always careful to write in corresponding order the letters denoting corresponding points in congruent figures. On the contrary, he evidently prefers the alphabetical order, and seems to disdain to alter it for the sake of beginners or others who might be confused by it" (vol. 1, p. 323). Our I.4 has no such freedom: it pairs corners by their places, the given angle's corner first, so the triangles go in as BAC and CDB, and the angle BCA it hands back, read from the other arm, is Euclid's ACB.

**The modern way.** As vectors, AB and CD, equal, parallel and running the same way, give B − A = D − C. Then C − A = D − B, so AC and BD are equal and parallel too; and A + D = B + C, so the diagonals AD and BC of the parallelogram ABDC bisect one another.
