**The halves of equal things.** Euclid completes two parallelograms, proves them equal by I.35, and bisects each by I.34. The sentence taking their halves is bracketed. Heath explains: "Here and in the next proposition Heiberg brackets the words 'But the halves of equal things are equal to one another' on the ground that, since the *Common Notion* which asserted this fact was interpolated at a very early date (before the time of Theon), it is probable that the words here were interpolated at the same time" (vol. 1, p. 332).

**The little argument.** Tannery, as Heath reports in his notes on the Common Notions, observes that the halves are no inference from Common Notion 3, as Heiberg had it, and would need a *reductio ad absurdum* through Common Notion 2. Heath adds that even then "a little argument would be necessary in addition to the reference to *Common Notion* 2" (vol. 1, p. 224). Book Zero supplies it in `Magnitude.eq_of_add_self_eq`. Were one half less than the other, its double would be less too, by `Magnitude.add_self_lt_add_self`. Equal doubles therefore have equal halves.

**Where the parallels meet.** Postulate 1 draws CA and BD, and draws BE and CF through the points I.31 supplies. That AD, meeting CA at A, meets BE, the parallel to CA through B, comes from I.30 (`Prop30.meet`), as Heath reports Proclus drawing it under I.31; likewise for CF. This accounts for the certificate's extra citation alongside I.31, I.34 and I.35. Postulate 5 enters through I.29 inside I.30, I.34 and I.35.

**The diameter Euclid names.** Our statement reads "in the same parallels" as two whole straight lines: A and D may be one point, and AD names the parallel itself. E and F lie on it; their order does not matter, since I.35 covers every arrangement. The area `quadArea` uses the diameter from the first corner to the third, which in EBCA is EC. To use Euclid's AB, `Parallelogram.rotate` starts the naming at B, and `Parallelogram.quadArea_rotate` proves that the area is unchanged. Then `Prop34.bisect` gives twice ABC. For DBCF it already uses DC.

**The modern way.** Write D = A + t(C − B) for a real number t. Replacing A by D changes the cross product (B − A) × (C − A) by a multiple of (C − B) × (C − B), which is zero. Half the absolute value of this cross product is the triangle's area, so this shear along BC preserves it.
