Euclid completes each triangle to a parallelogram on BC, with parallels through B and C. I.35 makes the parallelograms equal, I.34 makes each triangle half of its own, and so the triangles are equal. Heiberg brackets that last step, and Heath gives his reason: "Here and in the next proposition Heiberg brackets the words 'But the halves of equal things are equal to one another' on the ground that, since the *Common Notion* which asserted this fact was interpolated at a very early date (before the time of Theon), it is probable that the words here were interpolated at the same time" (vol. 1, p. 332).

**"A little argument."** Heiberg took the halves to follow from Common Notion 3. Paul Tannery, Heath reports, "acutely observes" that they are no inference from it: they want a *reductio ad absurdum* through Common Notion 2. Even then, Heath adds, "a little argument would be necessary in addition to the reference to *Common Notion* 2" (vol. 1, p. 224), though he thinks Euclid would have taken it as obvious. Our tacit rules of magnitude say nothing of halves, so we prove the little argument in [Book Zero](../../Elements/Figure), as `Magnitude.eq_of_add_self_eq`: were one half less than the other, its double would be less too. Bracketed or not, the step has to be taken.

The area stays while the sides stretch, for triangles as for parallelograms: "Thus the *perimeter* in parallelograms or triangles is of itself no criterion as to their area" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 332). Proclus tells of "describers of countries … who drew conclusions regarding the size of cities from their perimeters" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 332), and of members of communistic societies of his day "who cheated their fellow members by giving them land of greater perimeter but less area than they took themselves"; and Heath, after Moritz Cantor, adds that about 130 B.C. Polybius "said that there were people who could not understand that camps of the same periphery might have different capacities" (vol. 1, p. 333).

**What if A and D are one point?** Our statement reads "in the same parallels" as two whole straight lines, so A and D may coincide, as I.41 will want; the "AD" that Euclid produces is then the parallel through the vertices. He takes it from the figure that BE and CF meet it, and our I.35 covers wherever they fall.

**The modern way.** With the cross product of [I.35](35.md), twice the area of the triangle ABC is $|\mathrm{(C - B)} \times \mathrm{(A - B)}|$. Putting $\mathrm{D} = \mathrm{A} + t\mathrm{(C - B)}$ adds $t\mathrm{(C - B)} \times \mathrm{(C - B)} = 0$ to the cross product, so sliding the vertex along the parallel leaves the area alone.
