**The halves of equal things.** Euclid completes two parallelograms, proves them equal by I.35, and bisects each by I.34. The sentence taking their halves is bracketed. Heath explains: "Here and in the next proposition Heiberg brackets the words 'But the halves of equal things are equal to one another' on the ground that, since the *Common Notion* which asserted this fact was interpolated at a very early date (before the time of Theon), it is probable that the words here were interpolated at the same time" (vol. 1, p. 332).

**The little argument.** In his discussion of the Common Notions, Heath agrees with Tannery that equality of halves does not follow directly from Common Notion 2: "a little argument would be necessary in addition to the reference to *Common Notion* 2" (vol. 1, p. 224). Book Zero supplies it in `Magnitude.eq_of_add_self_eq`. Were one half less than the other, its double would be less too, by `Magnitude.add_self_lt_add_self`. Equal doubles therefore have equal halves.

**Where the parallels meet.** Postulate 1 supplies CA and BD, and the straight lines through the unnamed points supplied by I.31. That AD, meeting CA at A, meets BE, the parallel to CA through B, comes from I.30 (`Prop30.meet`), as Heath reports Proclus drawing it under I.31; likewise for CF. This accounts for the certificate's extra citation alongside I.31, I.34 and I.35. Postulate 5 enters through I.29 inside I.30, I.34 and I.35.

**The diameter Euclid names.** Lean reads "in the same parallels" as two whole straight lines: A and D may be one point, and AD names the parallel itself. E and F lie on it; their order is unnecessary because I.35 covers every arrangement. The area `quadArea` uses the diameter from the first corner to the third, which in EBCA is EC. To use Euclid's AB, `Parallelogram.rotate` starts the naming at B, and `Parallelogram.quadArea_rotate` proves that the area is unchanged. Then `Prop34.bisect` gives twice ABC. For DBCF it already uses DC.

**The modern way.** Write D = A + t(C − B) for a real number t. Replacing A by D changes the cross product (B − A) × (C − A) by a multiple of (C − B) × (C − B), which is zero. Half the absolute value of this cross product is the triangle's area, so this shear along BC preserves it.
