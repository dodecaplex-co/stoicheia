**A plumb-line.** Euclid's phrase, Heath explains, is "the full expression for a *perpendicular*, κάθετος meaning *let fall* or *let down*, so that the expression corresponds to our *plumb-line*" (vol. 1, p. 271). Proclus says the problem "was first investigated by Oenopides [5th cent. B.C.], who thought it useful for astronomy" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 271), and that Oenopides called the perpendicular "*gnomon-wise*", "because the gnomon is also at right angles to the horizon" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 272). The gnomon was a sundial's upright staff.

**"Some postulate of continuity."** Euclid never says why the circle meets AB, let alone twice. To satisfy us of this, Heath writes, "we need, as in I. 1, some postulate of continuity", such as Wilhelm Killing's: "If a point [here the point describing the circle] moves in a figure which is divided into two parts [by the straight line], and if it belongs at the beginning of the motion to one part and at another stage of the motion to the other part, it must during the motion cut the boundary between the two parts" (vol. 1, p. 272). Going both ways from D, it crosses AB twice.

**One perpendicular, or three?** Most editors miss a harder question, Heath says: "How do we know, says the supposed critic, that the circle does not cut AB in *three* or more points, in which case there would be not *one* perpendicular but *three* or more?" (vol. 1, p. 272). Proclus' three answers, Heath finds, are inconclusive. On a sphere it happens: "It is not possible in Euclidean space, but it is possible, under the Riemann hypothesis (where a straight line is a 'closed series' and returns on itself), in the case where C is the pole of the straight line AB" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 273). Heath's "Riemann hypothesis" is the sphere-like geometry now called elliptic, not the famous conjecture about prime numbers.

**Two points are enough.** Our rule, [`line_meets_circle_of_opposite_sides`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Diagram.lean), says that a straight line that separates two points on or inside a circle, here its centre C and the point D, cuts it twice. Like Euclid's, our I.12 asks only for a straight line at right angles to the whole infinite straight line AB. So our proof never needs to know whether the circle meets AB anywhere else.

**The modern way.** With vectors, the foot of the perpendicular from C to the straight line AB is H = A + t(B − A), where t = (C − A)·(B − A) / |B − A|², chosen so that (C − H)·(B − A) = 0. Euclid's H, the middle point (E + G)/2 of the chord EG, is the same point.
