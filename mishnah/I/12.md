**A plumb-line.** Euclid's phrase, Heath explains, is "the full expression for a perpendicular, κάθετος meaning *let fall* or *let down*, so that the expression corresponds to our *plumb-line*" (vol. 1, p. 271). The problem is old: Proclus says that it "was first investigated by Oenopides [5th cent. B.C.], who thought it useful for astronomy" (vol. 1, p. 271).

**The circle must cut AB.** Euclid never says why the circle meets AB in two points. Heath: "To satisfy us of this we need, as in I. 1, some postulate of continuity", and he offers Wilhelm Killing's: "If a point [here the point describing the circle] moves in a figure which is divided into two parts [by the straight line], and if it belongs at the beginning of the motion to one part and at another stage of the motion to the other part, it must during the motion cut the boundary between the two parts" (vol. 1, p. 272). Here that postulate is a named rule, [`line_meets_circle_of_opposite_sides`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Diagram.lean), System E's Intersection 2: a straight line that separates two points on or inside a circle, here its centre C and the point D, cuts it in two points.

**What else Lean made us say.** D, taken "at random on the other side of the straight line AB", comes from System E, Points 6 (`exists_oppositeSidesOf`). I.8 makes the adjacent angles CHG and EHC equal, and Definition 10 (`IsRightAngle`) calls each right. As in I.11, the proof then uses Postulate 4 to make each the one right angle `∟`, though Euclid doesn't cite it, and `OnRay.cases_of_between` gives a right angle with every point of AB, as "perpendicular to the given infinite straight line AB" asks.

**One perpendicular, or three?** Heath raises an objection that most editors miss: "How do we know, says the supposed critic, that the circle does not cut AB in three or more points, in which case there would be not one perpendicular but three or more?" (vol. 1, p. 272). Proclus' three answers, Heath finds, are inconclusive. On a sphere it really happens: "It is not possible in Euclidean space, but it is possible, under the Riemann hypothesis (where a straight line is a 'closed series' and returns on itself), in the case where C is the pole of the straight line AB" (vol. 1, p. 273). Our I.12, like Euclid's, asks only that a perpendicular be drawn, so the Lean never needs to know whether the circle meets AB anywhere else.

**The modern way.** With vectors, the foot of the perpendicular from C to the straight line through A and B is H = A + t(B − A), where t = (C − A)·(B − A) / |B − A|², chosen so that (C − H)·(B − A) = 0. Euclid's H, the middle point (E + G)/2 of the chord EG, is the same point.
