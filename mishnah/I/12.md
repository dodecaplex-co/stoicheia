**A plumb-line.** Heath explains that Euclid's phrase is the full form of our word: "This is the full expression for a perpendicular, κάθετος meaning *let fall* or *let down*, so that the expression corresponds to our *plumb-line*" (vol. 1, p. 271). The problem is old. "This problem," says Proclus, "was first investigated by Oenopides [5th cent. B.C.], who thought it useful for astronomy. He however calls the perpendicular, in the archaic manner, (a line drawn) gnomon-wise … because the gnomon is also at right angles to the horizon" (vol. 1, pp. 271–272): the gnomon being, in that earlier sense, the upright staff of a sundial.

**A point on the other side.** "On the other side of the straight line AB" is, Heath notes, "literally 'towards the other parts of the straight line AB'" (vol. 1, p. 271). That D can be taken there at all is System E's Points rule 6, which Book Zero gives as `exists_oppositeSidesOf`, set out with this very sentence in mind. Any such D will do, and D is not C, since C is not on the other side of AB from itself.

**The circle must cut AB.** Euclid then names the two points where the circle meets AB without a word about why there are two. Heath: "Attention is called by most editors to the assumption in this proposition that, if only D be taken on the side of AB remote from C, the circle described with CD as radius must necessarily cut AB in two points. To satisfy us of this we need, as in I. 1, some postulate of continuity," and he offers Wilhelm Killing's: "If a point [here the point describing the circle] moves in a figure which is divided into two parts [by the straight line], and if it belongs at the beginning of the motion to one part and at another stage of the motion to the other part, it must during the motion cut the boundary between the two parts" (vol. 1, p. 272). Here that postulate is a named rule, [`line_meets_circle_of_opposite_sides`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Diagram.lean), Intersection rule 2 of the System E of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma: a straight line that separates two points on or inside a circle (here its centre C and the point D) cuts it in two points. The F in "the circle EFG" is only a name; the proof never uses it. Nor, in Lean, the straight lines CG, CH and CE, which are drawn for the eye: only their ends.

**Right, and the right angle.** I.8 makes the adjacent angles CHG and EHC equal, and Euclid cites Definition 10 to call each of them right. In Lean, Definition 10 is `IsRightAngle`, and it says just that: the angle GHC is right because its neighbour CHE is equal to it. But the statement asks for ∟, the one right angle, and it is Postulate 4, "that all right angles are equal to one another", that lets every right angle go by that single name. So Postulate 4 is on the certificate, though Euclid doesn't cite it here.

**The whole infinite straight line.** Euclid proves two angles right, CHG and CHE; "perpendicular to the given infinite straight line AB" asks for a right angle with every point of AB. Lean wants the step between, and Book Zero's [`OnRay.cases_of_between`](../../Elements/Figure/Rays.lean) says it, as it did for I.11: H cuts the straight line into two rays, and every other point P of it lies on the ray from H through G or on the ray from H through E (System E, Between 7). Then the angle CHP is the angle CHG or the angle CHE, because an angle depends only on its rays (`angle_onRay_right` in Book Zero). The two triangles that I.8 compares come from `Triangle.of_line`: H, G and E are on AB, and C is off it.

**One perpendicular, or three?** Heath raises "a possible objection to the Euclidean statement of this problem which is much more difficult to dispose of at this stage … How do we know, says the supposed critic, that the circle does not cut AB in three or more points, in which case there would be not one perpendicular but three or more?" Proclus tries three ways to rule it out, and Heath, after following each with care, finds them inconclusive. On a sphere it really happens: "It is not possible in Euclidean space, but it is possible, under the Riemann hypothesis (where a straight line is a 'closed series' and returns on itself), in the case where C is the pole of the straight line AB" (vol. 1, pp. 272–273). Uniqueness can wait for I.16, Heath says, "since I. 12 is not used before that proposition is reached"; or it can be had at once "by means of this same invaluable I. 7" (vol. 1, p. 273). Our I.12, like Euclid's, asks only that a perpendicular be drawn, so the Lean proof never needs to know whether the circle meets AB anywhere else.

**The modern way.** With vectors, the foot of the perpendicular from C to the straight line through A and B is H = A + t(B − A), where t = (C − A)·(B − A) / |B − A|², chosen so that (C − H)·(B − A) = 0. Euclid's H, the middle point (E + G)/2 of the two places where the circle crosses the line, is the same point.
