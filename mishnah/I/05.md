**Thales, and a proof of Euclid's own.** Proclus credits the discovery to Thales, "who however is said to have spoken of the angles as being *similar*, and not as being equal" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 252). The proof, Heath infers from a passage of Aristotle, was Euclid's own (vol. 1, pp. 253–254). Generations of schoolchildren knew it as the *pons asinorum*, the bridge of asses.

**A chance point, but not too far out.** Euclid takes F "at random" on BD; the Greek, Heath notes, means "a chance point" (vol. 1, p. 252). Yet in the very next sentence AE is "the greater" and AF "the less", so F must not lie too far along BD. Book Zero's `exists_between_nearer` keeps it near enough, inside a circle about A through E that Euclid never draws (System E, Circle 2 and Intersections 4). Postulate 2 is not on the certificate, because the statement hands us D and E already produced.

**What Lean made us say.** Euclid's figure quietly settles the order of its points, and Lean wants each of those facts said. G lies beyond C, because AG, being equal to AF, is greater than AC (`OnRay.between_of_len_lt`). The angle FAG is "common" to the two triangles because B lies on AF and C on AG, and an angle doesn't change when an arm is traced through another point of its ray (`angle_onRay_left`, System E, Diagram-angle 4). The "whole angle ABG" is made up of ABC and CBG because C lies between A and G (`angle_add_of_between`, from Pasch 2 and Diagram-angle 2). Then the proof is Euclid's own: I.3, I.4 twice, and Common Notion 3 twice.

**Pappus' shortcut.** Proclus reports a proof by Pappus with no construction at all: "Let us conceive this one triangle as two triangles" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 254), ABC and ACB, and apply I.4. Heath thinks it "by far the best" (vol. 1, p. 254), and in Lean it is a single citation of `Prop4`. But it proves nothing about the angles under the base, which, Proclus explains, Euclid added "for the purpose of fore-arming the learner against a possible objection" to I.7 (vol. 1, p. 255). Our I.7 uses them just so, in `Prop7.within`.

**The modern way.** Put A at the origin and B, C at vectors b, c with |b| = |c|. Then cos B = b·(b − c)/(|b||b − c|) and cos C = c·(c − b)/(|c||c − b|), and both numerators equal |b|² − b·c. Or, in Pappus' spirit: the reflection in the line along b + c swaps b and c, and so carries each base angle onto the other.
