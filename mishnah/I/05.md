**Thales, and a proof of Euclid's own.** Proclus credits the discovery to Thales, "who however is said to have spoken of the angles as being *similar*, and not as being equal" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 252). The proof, though, seems to be new. A passage of Aristotle shows the older textbooks reaching this theorem through the "mixed" angles of circles, and Heath concludes: "It is a natural inference from the passage of Aristotle that Euclid's proof of I. 5 was his own" (vol. 1, pp. 253–254). Generations of schoolchildren knew it as the *pons asinorum*, the bridge of asses.

**A chance point, but not too far out.** Euclid takes F "at random" on BD; the Greek, Heath notes, means "a chance point" (vol. 1, p. 252). In the very next sentence he calls AE "the greater" and AF "the less", so F must not lie too far along BD. For Euclid that costs nothing, since the sides are produced as far as we please. Here D and E are given, so `Prop5.chance_point` keeps F inside a circle about A drawn a little smaller than AE: anywhere between B and D if D is inside that circle, or where BD leaves it if not (System E, Circle 2 and Intersections 4). That circle is why Postulate 3 and Definition 15 are on the certificate, though Euclid cites neither. Postulate 2 is not, because the statement hands us D and E already produced.

**What Lean made us say.** Euclid's figure quietly settles the order of its points, and Lean wants each of those facts said. G lies beyond C, because AG, being equal to AF, is greater than AC (`Prop5.beyond`). The angle FAG is "common" to the two triangles because B lies on AF and C on AG, and an angle doesn't change when an arm is traced through another point of the same ray (`Prop5.angle_along`, System E's diagram-angle transfer 4). The "whole angle ABG" really is made up of ABC and CBG, because C lies between A and G and so BC falls within it (`Prop5.angle_add`, from Pasch 2 and diagram-angle transfer 2). And one fact of order on a line, `Prop5.between_inner`, says that if B is between A and D and F between B and D, then B is between A and F. Every rule cited is one of Avigad, Dean and Mumma's, read with LeanEuclid's Lean version open alongside; Beeson, Narboux and Wiedijk's *Proof-checking Euclid* is a fine guide to how many such facts of order hide in Euclid's figures. With them in hand, the proof is Euclid's own: I.3 to cut off AG, I.4 twice, then Common Notion 3 twice.

**The base that is common.** "While the base BC is common to them" looks idle, and Todhunter, Heath tells us, "left out these words as being of no use and tending to perplex a beginner. But Euclid evidently chose to quote the conclusion of I. 4 exactly" (vol. 1, p. 252). Lean agrees with Euclid: the second use of I.4 hands back the equation ‖BC‖ = ‖CB‖, two bases that are equal because they are the same base.

**Pappus' shortcut.** Proclus reports a proof by Pappus with no construction at all: "Let us conceive this one triangle as two triangles," ABC and ACB, and apply I.4. Heath: "It seems to me that the form given to the proof by Pappus himself is by far the best" (vol. 1, p. 254). He has less patience for the classroom version in which the triangle is lifted and turned over: "There is the obvious difficulty that it supposes the triangle to be taken up and at the same time to remain where it is" (vol. 1, p. 254). In Lean, Pappus' proof is a single citation of I.4, with ABC as both triangles: `(Prop4 hABC hACB hAB hAB.symm (angle_symm …)).2.2.1`. It is also how Hilbert, who made side–angle–side an axiom, gets the base angles. But it "does not include the proof of the second part of the proposition about the angles under the base" (vol. 1, p. 255), and this book follows Euclid's own road.

**Why the angles under the base?** Heath admits the second part "is not necessary for any subsequent demonstration that is to be found in the original text of Euclid, but only for the interpolated second case of I. 7." He accepts Proclus' explanation, that it "was inserted for the purpose of fore-arming the learner against a possible objection" to I.7 as Euclid gives it, with one case only (vol. 1, p. 255).

**The modern way.** Put A at the origin and B, C at vectors b, c with |b| = |c|. Then cos B = b·(b − c)/(|b||b − c|) and cos C = c·(c − b)/(|c||c − b|), and both numerators equal |b|² − b·c. Or, in Pappus' spirit: the reflection in the line along b + c swaps b and c, and so carries each base angle onto the other.
