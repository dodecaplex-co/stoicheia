**"At the very threshold."** Proclus credits the discovery to Thales, "who however is said to have spoken of the angles as being *similar*, and not as being *equal*" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 252). Aristotle sketches an older proof, which made the equal sides radii of a circle and reasoned with the angles a straight line makes with an arc. From that passage Heath infers that Euclid's proof was his own, "and it would thus appear that his innovations as regards order of propositions and methods of proof began at the very threshold of the subject" (vol. 1, p. 254). Generations of schoolchildren knew it as the *pons asinorum*, the bridge of asses.

**"A chance point."** Euclid takes F "at random" on BD, and the Greek, Heath notes, means "a chance point" (vol. 1, p. 252). Yet at once AE is "the greater" and AF "the less", so F must not lie too far out: Book Zero's `exists_between_nearer` keeps it inside a circle about A through E that Euclid never draws. The rest of his figure quietly settles the order of its points, and Lean wants each of those facts said: G lies beyond C, since AG, equal to AF, is greater than AC; the angle FAG is "common" because B lies on AF and C on AG; and C lies between A and G, so the "whole angle ABG" is ABC and CBG together.

**"Let us conceive this one triangle as two triangles."** So begins Pappus' proof, which Proclus reports: with no construction at all, AB, AC are equal to AC, AB, the angle at A is common, and I.4 does the rest (Heath, vol. 1, p. 254). Heath thinks it "by far the best"; the editors' way, turning the triangle over onto itself, "supposes the triangle to be taken up and at the same time to remain where it is" (vol. 1, p. 254).

**Why produce the sides?** Pappus says nothing of the angles under the base, which, Proclus explains, Euclid added "for the purpose of fore-arming the learner against a possible *objection*" to I.7 (Heath, vol. 1, p. 255). Our I.7 uses them just so. Euclid produces the sides by Postulate 2; we state I.5 with D and E already given, so Lean needs none.

**The modern way.** Put A at the origin and B, C at vectors b, c with |b| = |c|: then cos B = b·(b − c)/(|b||b − c|) and cos C = c·(c − b)/(|c||c − b|), and both numerators equal |b|² − b·c. Or, in Pappus' spirit, the reflection in the line along b + c swaps b and c, and so carries each base angle onto the other.
