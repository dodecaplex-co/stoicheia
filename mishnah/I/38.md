**Equal halves of equal parallelograms.** Euclid draws BG parallel to CA and FH parallel to DE by I.31. The completed parallelograms GBCA and DEFH stand on the equal bases BC and EF between the same parallels, so I.36 makes their areas equal. Their diameters AB and DF halve them by I.34 (`Prop34.bisect`). The triangles are therefore equal in area. Heiberg brackets the assertion about halves here too, as Heath notes under I.37; our step is `Magnitude.eq_of_add_self_eq`.

**Where the parallels meet.** That AD meets BG and FH, the parallels drawn to CA and DE, comes from I.30 (`Prop30.meet`), as Heath reports Proclus drawing it under I.31. This explains the certificate's citation beyond Euclid's brackets. The construction uses Postulate 1 to draw CA, DE, BG and FH; it also enters through I.30, I.31, I.34 and I.36. Postulate 5 enters through I.29, reached through I.30 and the parallelogram propositions I.34 and I.36.

**What Lean made us say.** The vertices A and D may be one point, as I.42 needs when applying this result to triangles ABE and AEC. AD therefore names the given parallel itself. The bases may overlap, and B may be F; I.36 covers these arrangements. I.31 supplies unnamed points to specify the constructed straight lines; they have names in Lean but no letters in the figure. Our `quadArea` measures GBCA by GC, so `Parallelogram.quadArea_rotate` names it from B to use AB. The turn from Euclid's FED back to DEF is `area_cba`.

**The order of proof matters.** Proclus treats VI.1 as a proof embracing I.35–I.38, since triangles and parallelograms of one altitude are as their bases. Heath answers: "It is true that VI. 1 generalises these propositions, but it must be observed that it does not prove the propositions themselves, as Proclus seems to imply; they are in fact assumed in order to prove VI. 1" (vol. 1, p. 334). Equal areas between parallels must be established before they can support the theory of proportion.

**The modern way.** Put BF on the x-axis and AD on y = h, with h > 0: the triangle areas are ½·BC·h and ½·EF·h, equal because BC = EF. Euclid's VI.1 expresses the same relation as a ratio: triangles of one height are as their bases.
