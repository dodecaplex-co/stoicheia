**Two bases in place of one.** Euclid repeats I.37 with the triangles set apart on equal bases BC, EF. He completes the parallelograms GBCA and DEFH, which I.36 now makes equal, and the diameters AB and DF halve them (I.34). J. L. Heiberg brackets the sentence on halves here too, as Heath notes under I.37, and the little argument we gave there takes the step again.

**"Assumed in order to prove VI. 1."** Proclus thought Euclid had proved all four of I.35–I.38 at once in VI.1, where triangles and parallelograms of the same altitude are to one another as their bases, "for of course to be of the same altitude is equivalent to being in the same parallels"; most readers, he says, had missed it (Heath, vol. 1, p. 334). Heath answers: "It is true that VI. 1 generalises these propositions, but it must be observed that it does not prove the propositions themselves, as Proclus seems to imply; they are in fact assumed in order to prove VI. 1" (p. 334). The equalities come first, and Book VI's ratios are built on them.

**The triangles of I.24, by area.** Heron, in an-Nairīzī, places here a theorem Proclus gives under I.24, where we met it. When two triangles have two sides equal to two sides, "*if the sum of the two angles contained by the equal sides is equal to two right angles, the two triangles are equal to one another*" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 334); if the sum is less, the one with the greater angle is the greater, and if more, the one with the less. For the first case Heron copies one triangle onto a side of the other, its vertex on the parallel to that side through the other's, and I.37 makes them equal. I.24 compared the bases of such triangles; Heron compares their areas.

**The modern way.** Put BF on the x-axis and AD on the straight line y = h, with h positive: the triangles have areas ½·BC·h and ½·EF·h, equal because BC = EF. With the height fixed the area goes with the base, as VI.1 says.
