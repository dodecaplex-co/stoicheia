**One figure, and three more.** Euclid draws one figure, with the straight lines AD and CB crossing. The second case that Simson and the textbooks add "is not in the original text", Heath explains, Euclid's practice being to give "only one case, and that the most difficult" (vol. 1, p. 260). Lean can't leave the rest to the reader, so [`Prop7.cases`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop07.lean) sorts out where D can be. AD may cross CB, as in Euclid's figure; AC may cross DB, which is his figure with C and D exchanged; or D falls within the triangle ABC, or C within ABD. For those two the Lean follows Proclus (`Prop7.within`) and uses the angles under the base, the second part of I.5; under I.5, Heath agrees with Proclus that it was put there for just this case.

**What the figure shows.** Heath puts his finger on the gap: "here we need Postulates which shall satisfy us as to the relative positions of CA, CB, CD on the one hand and of DC, DA, DB on the other, in order that we may be able to infer that the angle BDC is greater than the angle ADC, and the angle ACD greater than the angle BCD" (vol. 1, p. 260). In `Prop7.figure` the point where AD and CB cross does that work: with Pasch's rules and System E, Diagram-angle 2, the angle ACD is made up of ACB and BCD, and CDB of CDA and ADB. Our I.5 is stated with the equal sides produced, so the proof uses Postulate 2 each time it cites I.5 (`Prop5.base`), though Euclid's own figure needs no line produced.

**What it is for.** "What I. 7 proves is that if, in addition to the base of a triangle, the length of the side terminating at each extremity of the base is given, only one triangle satisfying these conditions can be constructed on one and the same side of the given base" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 261). So I.7 is more than a step towards I.8: I.1 and I.22 build a triangle, and I.7 says there is only one on each side. Heath quotes Charles Dodgson, who put it memorably: "of all plane figures that can be made by hingeing rods together, the three-sided ones (and these only) are rigid" (vol. 1, p. 261).

**The modern way.** Put A at (0, 0) and B at (c, 0). A point at distance b from A and a from B has x² + y² = b² and (x − c)² + y² = a², so x = (b² + c² − a²)/2c and y = ±√(b² − x²). The two answers are mirror images in AB, one on each side.
