**One figure, and three more.** Euclid draws one picture, with the straight lines AD and CB crossing, and argues from it alone. Heath explains that the other case "is not in the original text (the omission being in accordance with Euclid's general practice of giving only one case, and that the most difficult, and leaving the others to be worked out by the reader for himself)" (vol. 1, p. 260). Lean can't leave anything to the reader, so [`Prop7.cases`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop07.lean) sorts out where D can be. AD may cross CB, which is Euclid's figure. AC may cross DB, which is the same figure with C and D exchanged, so the Lean proof just exchanges the letters. Otherwise D falls within the triangle ABC, or C within the triangle ABD.

**Proclus answers the objection.** The figure with D inside the triangle is Proclus' reply to an imagined objector, and he answers it with the second part of I.5, the angles under the base. Produce AC, AD to E, F. The angles ECD, FDC are equal; the angle ECD is greater than the angle BCD; so the angle BDC is "greater by far" than the angle BCD, and yet I.5 makes them equal. That is `Prop7.within`. Writing on I.5, Heath accepts Proclus' explanation, "which must apparently be the right one": the second part was put in "for the purpose of fore-arming the learner against a possible objection" to I.7 as Euclid gives it, with one case only (vol. 1, p. 255). In Lean, the second part of I.5 is used right here.

**What the figure shows.** Heath puts his finger on the gap: "here we need Postulates which shall satisfy us as to the relative positions of CA, CB, CD on the one hand and of DC, DA, DB on the other, in order that we may be able to infer that the angle BDC is greater than the angle ADC, and the angle ACD greater than the angle BCD" (vol. 1, p. 260). The postulates he asks for are the diagram rules of the System E of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma, and LeanEuclid's careful Lean version of them was our guide. In `Prop7.figure`, the point X where AD and CB cross does all the work. By Pasch's rules, A and B lie on one side of CD, D and B on one side of AC, and C and A on one side of BD. Then the rule that an angle with a straight line inside it is made up of the two parts (Diagram-angle 2) gives ACD as ACB and BCD together, and CDB as CDA and ADB together. Proclus' figure needs a different rule, triple incidence rule 1: since D is within the angle ACB, the straight line CD separates A from B.

**The steps Euclid leaves out.** Heath again: "It would be natural to insert here the step 'but the angle ACD is greater than the angle BCD. [C. N. 5.]'" (vol. 1, p. 260). The Lean proof does insert it. It cites Common Notion 5, the whole greater than the part, once for each angle, together with the fact that the part ACB is not nothing (if it were, B would lie on the straight line CA). And Heath's remark that "the case in which D falls on AC or BC does not require proof" turns out to need a short proof after all, `Prop7.triangle`. On the ray from A through C, D would make a length equal to itself with something added; on the other ray, AB would separate C from D.

**Producing the sides.** Euclid cites only the first part of I.5 in his own figure, the angles at the base. Our I.5 is stated the way Euclid states it, with the equal sides produced, so the Lean proof produces AC, AD and BC, BD in order to cite it. That's why Postulate 2 is on the certificate, though Euclid's argument never uses it.

**What it is for.** "What I. 7 proves is that if, in addition to the base of a triangle, the length of the side terminating at each extremity of the base is given, only one triangle satisfying these conditions can be constructed on one and the same side of the given base" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 261). So I.7 is more than a lemma for I.8. It also completes I.1 and I.22: they build a triangle, and I.7 says there is only one on each side. Heath quotes Charles Dodgson, who put it memorably: "of all plane figures that can be made by hingeing rods together, the three-sided ones (and these only) are rigid". Anyone checking Book I by machine has to settle cases like these, and Michael Beeson, Julien Narboux and Freek Wiedijk's *Proof-checking Euclid*, which fills Euclid's gaps across the whole of Book I, is a friendly guide to them.

**The modern way.** Put A at (0, 0) and B at (c, 0). A point at distance b from A and a from B has x² + y² = b² and (x − c)² + y² = a². Subtracting gives x = (b² + c² − a²)/2c, and then y = ±√(b² − x²). The two answers are mirror images in AB, one on each side, so each side has only one.
