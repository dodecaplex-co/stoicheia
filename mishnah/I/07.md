**"Language apparently so obscure."** Euclid's enunciation is hard going, and the words in parentheses are Heath's, put in "in order to make it intelligible" (vol. 1, p. 259). Euclid could write so, Heath explains, because "the phraseology was traditional and therefore, vague as it was, had a conventional meaning which the contemporary geometer well understood" (vol. 1, p. 259). The meaning is plain: given the base and the two sides, "only one triangle satisfying these conditions can be constructed on one and the same side of the given base" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 261). I.1 and I.22 build such a triangle, and I.7 says there is just one on each side.

**Why keep it?** Some editors used I.7 only to prove I.8, and some dropped it for Philo's proof of I.8. Heath quotes Charles Dodgson, who saw more in it: "of all plane figures that can be made by hingeing rods together, the *three*-sided ones (and these only) are *rigid*" (vol. 1, p. 261). Heath adds a story from Proclus: "It was said that astronomers used it to prove that three successive eclipses could not occur at equal intervals of time" (vol. 1, p. 261).

**"Not in the original text."** Euclid draws one figure, with AD crossing CB. The second case that Robert Simson and the textbooks add "is not in the original text", Heath explains (vol. 1, p. 260): as under I.2, Euclid gives one case and leaves the rest to the reader. Proclus answered that case with the angles under the base, which I.5 proved for just this purpose.

**Four places for D.** Lean has no reader to leave cases to, and we find D in four places. AD may cross CB, as in Euclid's figure, or AC may cross DB, his figure with C and D exchanged; or D or C falls within the triangle the other makes with AB, and that we answer as Proclus does. Our proof must also show that there is no fifth (`Prop7.cases`). Where BC separates D from A, AD crosses BC on C's side of AB, either within CB, as in Euclid's figure, or beyond C, and then C lies within the triangle ABD; where AC separates D from B, the same holds with A and B exchanged; where neither does, D lies within the triangle ABC. Euclid had no call to count the places. His figure shows a D that cannot exist, drawn to be refuted, and he refutes it where he draws it; a reader who drew it elsewhere had that case to answer, as Proclus answered one. For Euclid's figure Heath asks for "Postulates which shall satisfy us as to the relative positions" of the straight lines at C and at D (vol. 1, p. 260). The point where AD and CB cross does that work: it puts CB within the angle ACD, and DA within the angle CDB, so that each angle is made up of two.

**The modern way.** Put A at (0, 0) and B at (c, 0). A point at distance b from A and a from B has x² + y² = b² and (x − c)² + y² = a², so x = (b² + c² − a²)/(2c) and y = ±√(b² − x²): two answers, mirror images in AB, one on each side.
