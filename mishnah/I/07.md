**"Language apparently so obscure."** Euclid's enunciation is hard going, and the words in parentheses are Heath's, put in "in order to make it intelligible" (vol. 1, p. 259). Euclid could write so, Heath explains, because "the phraseology was traditional and therefore, vague as it was, had a conventional meaning which the contemporary geometer well understood" (vol. 1, p. 259). The meaning is plain: given the base and the two sides, "only one triangle satisfying these conditions can be constructed on one and the same side of the given base" (vol. 1, p. 261). I.1 and I.22 build such a triangle, and I.7 says there is just one on each side.

**Why keep it?** Some editors used I.7 only to prove I.8, and some dropped it for Philo's proof of I.8. Heath quotes Charles Dodgson, who saw more in it: "of all plane figures that can be made by hingeing rods together, the *three*-sided ones (and these only) are *rigid*" (vol. 1, p. 261). Heath adds a story from Proclus: "It was said that astronomers used it to prove that three successive eclipses could not occur at equal intervals of time" (vol. 1, p. 261).

**"Only one case, and that the most difficult."** Euclid draws one figure, with the straight lines AD and CB crossing. The second case that Robert Simson and the textbooks add "is not in the original text", Heath explains, since Euclid leaves the others "to be worked out by the reader for himself" (vol. 1, p. 260). Proclus answered that case with the angles under the base, which I.5 proved for just this purpose.

**Where can D be?** Lean leaves nothing to the reader, and `Prop7.cases` finds four places. AD may cross CB, as in Euclid's figure; AC may cross DB, his figure with C and D exchanged; or D falls within the triangle ABC, or C within ABD, and those two we answer as Proclus does (`Prop7.within`). For Euclid's figure Heath asks for "Postulates which shall satisfy us as to the relative positions" of the straight lines at C and at D (vol. 1, p. 260). We let the point where AD and CB cross do that work (`Prop7.figure`): with Pasch's rules and System E, Diagram-angle 2, the angle ACD is made up of ACB and BCD, and CDB of CDA and ADB.

**The modern way.** Put A at (0, 0) and B at (c, 0). A point at distance b from A and a from B has x² + y² = b² and (x − c)² + y² = a², so x = (b² + c² − a²)/2c and y = ±√(b² − x²): two answers, mirror images in AB, one on each side.
