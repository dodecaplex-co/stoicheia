**A problem with a condition.** Not every three straight lines make a triangle, as the enunciation says. Heath notes: "This is the first case in the *Elements* of a διορισμός to a problem in the sense of a statement of the conditions or limits of the possibility of a solution. The criterion is of course supplied by the preceding proposition" (vol. 1, p. 293). We state the three conditions as hypotheses; the proof needs no I.20, which shows only that they cannot be dropped.

**"Who is so dull?"** "It was early observed," writes Heath, "that Euclid assumes, without giving any reason, that the circles drawn as described will meet" when the condition holds (vol. 1, p. 293). Robert Simson was impatient: "For who is so dull, though only beginning to learn the Elements, as not to perceive that the circle described from the centre F, at the distance FD, must meet FH betwixt F and H, because FD is less than FH" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 293). Lean is exactly that dull.

**"Only to invoke the Principle of Continuity."** Heath's answer: the circle about G has a point outside the circle about F and a point inside it. H is outside, FH being B and C together, greater than A. The point M where GF, produced if need be, meets the circle about G is inside, since taking the same thing from two unequals leaves FM less than A; our proof also takes the case he passes over, where M is F itself. Then "we have only to invoke the Principle of Continuity, as we have to do in I. 1" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 294). Our rule here is System E, Intersection 4, in the corrected form LeanEuclid gives it.

**"Of infinite length in the direction of E."** Euclid sets out DE "terminated at D but of infinite length in the direction of E", so that I.3 has room to cut DF, FG and GH off it. But I.3 cuts only from a greater finite straight line, so we say how far to go: I.2 sets out a straight line equal to the one to be cut off, and the straight line to be cut from is produced further, as in I.11.

**The modern way.** In the complex plane put F at 0 and G at b > 0, and let K = x + iy with x = (a² + b² − c²)/(2b) and y = √(a² − x²). Then |K| = a and |K − b| = c, and y is real and positive exactly when |a − b| < c < a + b: the three conditions of I.22, in one line.
