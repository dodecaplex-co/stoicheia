**The first condition on a problem.** Euclid's enunciation does not just set the task; it says when the task can be done. Heath: "This is the first case in the *Elements* of a διορισμός to a problem in the sense of a statement of the conditions or limits of the possibility of a solution. The criterion is of course supplied by the preceding proposition" (vol. 1, p. 293). That is the bracket [I. 20]: in any triangle two sides together are greater than the third, so nothing less will do. He also corrects the usual English: the words are "usually translated (e.g. by Williamson and Simson) 'but it is necessary,' which is however inaccurate"; they mean "thus it is necessary", just as "thus it is required" introduces what a construction is to do (vol. 1, p. 293). In Lean the condition is three hypotheses, one for each pair, and the proof uses every one of them. I.20 itself is not among the propositions the proof cites: it shows that the condition cannot be dropped, which is a different matter from showing that it is enough.

**The circles that must meet.** "It was early observed," writes Heath, "that Euclid assumes, without giving any reason, that the circles drawn as described will meet if the condition that any two of the straight lines A, B, C are together greater than the third be fulfilled" (vol. 1, p. 293). Proclus argued it by *reductio ad absurdum*, ruling out circles that touch outside and circles that stand apart, but, Heath notes, "He should have considered also the hypothesis (γ) of one circle touching the other internally or lying entirely within the other without touching." Robert Simson had less patience with the critics: "For who is so dull, though only beginning to learn the Elements, as not to perceive that the circle described from the centre F, at the distance FD, must meet FH betwixt F and H, because FD is less than FH" (vol. 1, p. 293). Lean is exactly that dull, and so [`Prop22.meet`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop22.lean) follows Heath's own answer: "We have in fact only to satisfy ourselves that one of the circles, e.g. that with centre G, has at least one point of its circumference outside the other circle and also at least one point of its circumference inside the same circle" (vol. 1, p. 294). H is outside the circle about F, because FH is B and C together, which is greater than A. The point M where GF, produced if need be, meets the circle about G is inside it: if M falls between G and F, FM and C together make B, which is less than A and C, so FM is less than A; if M falls beyond F, FM and B together make C, which is less than A and B, so again FM is less than A. Then, Heath says, "we have only to invoke the Principle of Continuity, as we have to do in I. 1." Here that principle is System E's Intersection rule 4, `circles_meet_of_on_inside_outside`, in the corrected form that LeanEuclid's authors spotted was needed. Lean also wanted one case Heath passes over, the case where GM is neither less nor greater than GF: then M is F itself, the centre, and inside by System E's Generalities 3.

**Room on the straight line.** Euclid sets out DE "terminated at D but of infinite length in the direction of E", so that DF, FG and GH can all be cut off from it by I.3. A straight line in Lean has no length to cut from, and I.3 cuts off only from a greater finite straight line, so [`Prop22.room`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop22.lean) finds one: at F place a straight line equal to the one to be cut off (I.2), describe a circle with centre F through its end (Post. 3), let the straight line already drawn through F be produced beyond the centre until it cuts that circle (System E, Intersections 5), and produce it a little further still (Post. 2). It is the same step as `Prop11.produced` and `Prop16.beyond`, except that what has to fit is a given straight line, not a part of the figure, and that is why I.2 appears among the propositions I.22 cites, beside the I.3 that Euclid names. Book Zero's `between_inner` then puts D, F, G, H in that order along DE, as in Euclid's figure.

**What else Lean made us say.** Two small facts about magnitudes. `Prop22.ne_zero`, in the file: none of A, B, C can be nothing, for then each of the other two would be greater than the other; Euclid calls them straight lines and never needs to ask, but in Lean a straight line may have both ends at one point, and I.3 wants them apart. And `Magnitude.lt_of_add_lt_add_right`, in Book Zero: take the same thing from two unequal magnitudes, and the less remains the less, which is how "FM and C together are less than A and C" becomes "FM is less than A". Our statement also asks more than Euclid's, as I.23 will need: the corner F is a given point, FG lies along a given ray from F, and K falls on the side of a given point P. Euclid's DE is then set out through F, with D behind it, and K is taken where the circles cut one another on P's side of DE (System E, Intersections 8). Every rule here is one of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma's, and LeanEuclid's Lean version of System E, by Logan Murphy, Kaiyu Yang, Jialiang Sun, Zhaoyu Li, Anima Anandkumar and Xujie Si, was open beside us the whole way; our thanks to them all.

**Only two triangles.** Heath ends his note: "That the construction of the proposition gives only two points of intersection between the circles, and therefore only two triangles satisfying the condition, one on each side of FG, is clear from I. 7" (vol. 1, p. 294). The Lean takes the one on P's side and has no need of the other.

**The modern way.** Put F at 0 and G at b on the real line of the complex plane, and let K = x + iy with x = (a² + b² − c²)/2b and y = √(a² − x²). Then |K| = a and |K − b| = c, and y is real exactly when |a² + b² − c²| < 2ab, that is, when |a − b| < c < a + b: the three conditions of I.22, in one line.
