**"Which is absurd."** Euclid proves I.6 by supposing it false. Were AB the greater, cutting off DB equal to AC would make the triangle DBC equal, by I.4, to the whole ACB, "the less to the greater". "We have here used, for the first time in the *Elements*, the method of *reductio ad absurdum*," Heath notes (vol. 1, p. 256). The case in which AC is the greater is the same with B and C exchanged, so we give the argument once and use it both ways round. Euclid cites nothing here: the Lean cites I.3 for cutting off DB, from the end B, and I.4 for the base.

**Is the part really less?** Heath: "Euclid assumes that, because D is between A and B, the triangle DBC is less than the triangle ABC. Some postulate is necessary to justify this tacit assumption" (vol. 1, p. 256). He sketches one, seeing the angle at C as "a cluster of rays issuing from C", each meeting AB in one point. We prove it as `Prop6.less`, by another road: the straight line CD cuts the triangle into DBC and ADC (System E, Diagram-area 2); ADC has some area, because C is not on AB (Diagram-area 1); and the whole is greater than the part (Common Notion 5).

**A converse, but not a logical one.** I.6 turns the first part of I.5 round, hypothesis and conclusion changing places exactly: the kind of converse Proclus calls leading. Yet "I. 6 is the geometrical, but not the logical, converse of I. 5," Heath explains (vol. 1, p. 256), for logic alone gives only that some triangles with two equal angles are isosceles. Augustus De Morgan saw that I.6 is all the same "a purely *logical* deduction from I. 5 and I. 18 taken together" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 256), as Heath shows under I.19.

**Why not the second part too?** "Why, asks Proclus, did not Euclid convert the *second* part of I. 5 as well?" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 257). His answer: the converse is not wanted even to meet an objection, and it follows from I.6 at any time after I.13. Isaac Todhunter points out that "I. 6, not being wanted till II. 4, could be postponed till later and proved by means of I. 26" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 258).

**The modern way.** Put B at (−1, 0), C at (1, 0) and A at (x, y). Equal angles at B and C make the slopes of BA and CA equal and opposite, y/(x + 1) = y/(1 − x), so x = 0: A lies on the perpendicular bisector of BC, and AB = AC = √(1 + y²).
