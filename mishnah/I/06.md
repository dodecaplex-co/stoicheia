**The first reductio.** "We have here used, for the first time in the *Elements*, the method of *reductio ad absurdum*," Heath notes (vol. 1, p. 256). Euclid supposes AB greater than AC and lets the figure contradict itself. Both ends of the argument are rules of reasoning rather than geometry. "If AB is unequal to AC, one of them is greater" is the tacit law of magnitudes `lt_trichotomy` (System E §3.5), and the last step, from "not unequal" to "equal", is Lean's `Classical.byContradiction`.

**The less and the greater.** Heath again: "Euclid assumes that, because D is between A and B, the triangle DBC is less than the triangle ABC. Some postulate is necessary to justify this tacit assumption" (vol. 1, p. 256). Here it is `Prop6.less`, and it comes from three rules. The straight line CD cuts the triangle into DBC and ADC (`area_add_of_between`, System E, Diagram-area 2); ADC has some area, because C is not on AB (`on_line_of_area_eq_zero`, Diagram-area 1); and the whole is greater than the part (Common Notion 5).

**What else the figure says.** DBC is a triangle at all, so I.4 may be applied to it (`Prop6.figure`), and the angle DBC is the angle ABC itself, because D lies on BA (`angle_onRay_left`, from Diagram-angle 4). Euclid treats only the case in which AB is the greater. The other is the same with B and C exchanged, so the Lean gives the argument once and uses it both ways round. Heath's text of this proof has no brackets at all, but Lean cites what Euclid uses: cutting off DB is I.3's work, from the end B, and "the base DC is equal to the base AB" is I.4.

**A converse.** I.6 turns the first part of I.5 around. Heath, following Proclus, calls this the "leading" kind of conversion, in which hypothesis and conclusion change places exactly, and adds that "I. 6 is the geometrical, but not the logical, converse of I. 5" (vol. 1, p. 256). De Morgan observed that I.6 is all the same "a purely logical deduction from I. 5 and I. 18 taken together" (vol. 1, p. 256), as Heath recalls again under I.19.

**The modern way.** Put B at (−1, 0), C at (1, 0) and A at (x, y). Equal angles at B and C make the slopes of BA and CA equal and opposite, y/(x + 1) = y/(1 − x), so x = 0: A lies on the perpendicular bisector of BC, and AB = AC = √(1 + y²).
