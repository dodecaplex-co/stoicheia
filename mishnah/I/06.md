**"Which is absurd."** Euclid proves I.6 by supposing it false. Were AB the greater, cutting off DB equal to AC would make the triangle DBC equal, by I.4, to the whole ACB, "the less to the greater". "We have here used, for the first time in the *Elements*, the method of *reductio ad absurdum*," Heath notes (vol. 1, p. 256).

**Is the part really less?** Heath points out the step: "Euclid assumes that, because D is between A and B, the triangle DBC is less than the triangle ABC. Some postulate is necessary to justify this tacit assumption" (vol. 1, p. 256). He sketches one, seeing the angle at C as "a cluster of rays issuing from C", each meeting AB in one point. We prove it by another road, with areas: the whole triangle is greater than its part (`Prop6.less`).

**A converse, but not a logical one.** I.6 turns the first part of I.5 round, hypothesis and conclusion changing places exactly: the kind of converse Proclus calls leading. Yet "I. 6 is the geometrical, but not the logical, converse of I. 5," Heath explains (vol. 1, p. 256), for logic alone gives only that some triangles with two equal angles are isosceles. As Augustus De Morgan points out, I.6 is all the same "a purely *logical* deduction from I. 5 and I. 18 taken together" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 256); Heath gives the general argument under I.19, where we meet it again.

**Why not the second part too?** "Why, asks Proclus, did not Euclid convert the *second* part of I. 5 as well?" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 257). His answer: the converse is not wanted even to meet an objection, and it follows from I.6 at any time after I.13. Isaac Todhunter points out that "I. 6, not being wanted till II. 4, could be postponed till later and proved by means of I. 26" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 258).

**The modern way.** Put B at (−1, 0), C at (1, 0) and A at (x, y). Equal angles at B and C make the slopes of BA and CA equal and opposite, y/(x + 1) = y/(1 − x), so x = 0: A lies on the perpendicular bisector of BC, and AB = AC = √(1 + y²).
