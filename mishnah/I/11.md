**"Let CE be made equal to CD."** Euclid cites I.3 for it. But I.3 cuts a less straight line off a greater, and nothing says CB is longer than CD; C might even be B. Postulate 2 produces a straight line "continuously", but never says how far. So Book Zero's [`produce_longer`](../../Elements/Figure/Lines.lean) makes room: DC, produced beyond C, cuts the circle about C through D again, and a little further on it is greater than CD. I.3 cuts CE off it, and C then lies between D and E, which is what makes the angles at C adjacent.

**"Each of the angles DCF, FCE is right."** There Euclid stops, with Definition I.10: an angle equal to its neighbour is right. Our statement goes one step further, to `∟`, the one size that all right angles share, and here Postulate 4, that all right angles are equal, does its first work. From now on two right angles are `∟ + ∟` in the Lean, whoever drew them, which answers Isaac Todhunter's worry under I.14.

**I.9 in disguise.** Augustus De Morgan remarks that this proposition, which is "to bisect the angle made by a straight line and its continuation", should be a particular case of I.9. Heath agrees, with a doubt: "This is certainly worth noting, though I doubt the advantage of rearranging the propositions in consequence" (vol. 1, pp. 269–270). In Lean the rearrangement is not on offer: our I.9 asks for a triangle BAC, and a straight line with its continuation makes none.

**Faulty for a treatise, fine for practice.** Apollonius took D and E as Euclid does, then drew circles "in the manner of I. 1 meeting at F" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 270), and so had to prove DF equal to FE all over again. Heath is generous: "While however the substitution of Apollonius' constructions for I. 10 and 11 would show faulty arrangement in a theoretical treatise like Euclid's, they are entirely suitable for what we call *practical* geometry" (vol. 1, p. 270).

**The modern way.** Put C at the origin and let u point along AB, so that D and E are −u and u. Then F is as far from one as from the other, |f + u|² = |f − u|², and that simplifies to f·u = 0: CF is perpendicular to AB. In the complex plane it is shorter still: multiplying by i turns AB through a right angle.
