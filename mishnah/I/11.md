**Room to cut off CE.** "Let CE be made equal to CD," says Euclid, citing I.3. Heath notes that the Greek verb, κείσθω, does duty for the passive of τίθημι, "to place", a word "constantly used in the sense of making, e.g., one straight line equal to another straight line" (vol. 1, p. 269). But I.3 cuts a less straight line off a greater one, and which greater? In the figure E falls on CB, yet nothing says CB is longer than CD, and C might even be B. Postulate 2 produces a straight line "continuously", but never says how far. So Book Zero's [`produce_longer`](../../Elements/Figure/Lines.lean) makes room: a circle about C through D (Post. 3); the straight line DC, produced beyond the centre, cuts that circle again (System E, Intersections 5); and produced a little further still (Post. 2), it is greater than CD (Def. 15). I.3 cuts CE off it, and then C lies between D and E (`between_cut`), which is what makes the angles at C adjacent. The circle has in fact already marked E, so Lean could do without I.3, but we cite it where Euclid does. The same step comes back in I.16, I.20 and I.22; in I.24 a length is laid along a ray instead ([`OnRay.exists_len_eq`](../../Elements/Figure/Rays.lean)).

**A point at random on AC.** In Euclid's figure C lies between A and B, and D between A and C. In Lean C may be any point of the straight line AB, even A itself, and then there is no AC to take D on: B does instead. Otherwise D is taken between A and C (System E, Points 3). Nothing later cares where D is, so long as it is a point of AB other than C.

**On which side.** Euclid's FC could stand up on either side of AB. Our I.11 puts it on the side of a given point Q, because I.46 and I.48 will need the perpendicular on a particular side. I.1 builds its triangle on whichever side we name, so this costs one extra argument to I.1: the point Q, which is off DE because DE and AB are one straight line (System E, Generalities 1). Being on Q's side, F is off AB, so CDF and CEF really are the two triangles that I.8 compares (`Triangle.of_line`).

**Right, and the right angle.** Definition 10 says when an angle is right: when it is equal to the angle adjacent to it. In Lean, `IsRightAngle D C F` asks for a point on DC produced beyond C (here E) with the angle DCF equal to the angle FCE. The statement, though, speaks of `∟`, the one size that all right angles share, and it is Postulate 4, "that all right angles are equal to one another", that turns "right" into "equal to ∟". So Postulate 4 is on I.11's certificate, though Euclid cites only Def. 10.

**At right angles to the whole of AB.** Euclid proves two angles right, DCF and FCE, and concludes that CF is at right angles to AB. Our statement says it of every point P of AB other than C: the angle PCF is right. Two small facts bridge the gap. Every such P lies on the ray from C through D or on the ray from C through E ([`OnRay.cases_of_between`](../../Elements/Figure/Rays.lean) in Book Zero, from System E's Between 7: C can't lie both between P and D and between P and E, since it lies between D and E). And an angle doesn't change when an arm is traced through another point of the same ray (`angle_onRay_left` in Book Zero). Every rule cited is one of Avigad, Dean and Mumma's, read with LeanEuclid's careful Lean version open alongside; our thanks to them all.

**I.9 in disguise, and Apollonius.** De Morgan remarks that this proposition, which is "to bisect the angle made by a straight line and its continuation", should be a particular case of I.9, the constructions being the same. Heath: "This is certainly worth noting, though I doubt the advantage of rearranging the propositions in consequence" (vol. 1, pp. 269–270). In Lean the rearrangement isn't on offer: our I.9 asks for a triangle BAC, and a straight line with its continuation makes none. Apollonius, Proclus reports, drew the two circles of I.1 about D and E instead of citing it. Heath explains what Euclid gains: "This necessitates proving again that DF is equal to FE; whereas Euclid's assumption of the construction of I. 1 in the words 'let the equilateral triangle FDE be constructed' enables him to dispense with the drawing of circles and with the proof that DF is equal to FE at the same time" (vol. 1, p. 270). Lean agrees with Euclid: DF equal to FE is read straight off the statement of I.1. Heath adds, generously, that Apollonius' constructions "are entirely suitable for what we call practical geometry".

**Heron's perpendicular at the end.** Proclus also gives a construction for a perpendicular at the *end* of a straight line, "it being supposed (for the sake of argument) that we are not permitted to produce the straight line", and the commentary of an-Nairīzī attributes it to Heron (vol. 1, p. 270). Take any point C on AB, set up CE at right angles to AB as here, cut off CD equal to AC, bisect the angle ACE by CF (I.9), draw DF at right angles to CE, and join FA: by I.4 the angle at A equals the right angle at D.

**The modern way.** Put C at the origin and let u point along AB, so that D and E are −u and u. Then F is as far from one as from the other, |f + u|² = |f − u|², and that simplifies to f·u = 0: CF is perpendicular to AB. In the complex plane it is shorter still: multiplying by i turns AB through a right angle.
