Heath's reference for "let CE be made equal to CD" is I.3. But I.3 cuts a less straight line off a greater, and nothing says CB is longer than CD; C might even be B. Postulate 2 produces a straight line "continuously", but never says how far. So [Book Zero](../../Elements/Figure)'s [`produce_longer`](../../Elements/Figure/Lines.lean) makes room: DC, produced beyond C, cuts the circle about C through D again, and a little further on it is greater than CD. I.3 cuts CE off it, and C then lies between D and E, which is what makes the angles at C adjacent. Euclid then stops at "each of the angles DCF, FCE is right", with Definition I.10: an angle equal to its neighbour is right. Our statement goes one step further, to `∟`, the one size that all right angles share, and here Postulate 4, that all right angles are equal, does its first work. From now on two right angles are `∟ + ∟` in the Lean, whoever drew them, which meets in advance the point Isaac Todhunter makes under I.14, that one pair of right angles can stand for another only by Postulate 4.

Augustus De Morgan, Heath reports, remarks that this proposition, which is "to bisect the angle made by a straight line and its continuation", should be a particular case of I.9 (vol. 1, p. 269). Heath agrees, with a doubt: "This is certainly worth noting, though I doubt the advantage of rearranging the propositions in consequence" (vol. 1, pp. 269–270). For us the rearrangement is not on offer, since our I.9 asks for a triangle BAC, and a straight line with its continuation makes none. Apollonius drew circles "in the manner of I. 1 meeting at F" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 270), and so had to prove DF equal to FE all over again. Heath, sterner under I.10, is generous here: "While however the substitution of Apollonius' constructions for I. 10 and 11 would show faulty arrangement in a theoretical treatise like Euclid's, they are entirely suitable for what we call *practical* geometry" (vol. 1, p. 270).

**The modern way.** Put C at the origin and let $\mathbf{u}$ point along AB, so that D and E are $-\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{u}$. F, at $\mathbf{f}$, is as far from one as from the other, $|\mathbf{f} + \mathbf{u}|^2 = |\mathbf{f} - \mathbf{u}|^2$, and that simplifies to $\mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0$: CF is at right angles to AB. In the complex plane it is shorter still, since multiplying by $i$ turns a direction through $\pi/2$.
