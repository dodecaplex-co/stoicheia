**Found by Thales, proved by Euclid.** Where two straight lines cross, the opposite angles are equal, and I.16 needs this at once. "This theorem, according to Eudemus, was first discovered by Thales, but found its scientific demonstration in Euclid (Proclus, p. 299, 3–6)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 278). Where Euclid cites [Post. 4 and C.N. 1], Isaac Todhunter's remark under I.14 applies, and so does our answer there: in Lean, Common Notion 1 is enough.

**Why "vertical"?** Nothing to do with up and down. Two straight lines that cross, Proclus explains, "make two pairs of *vertical* angles (which are more clearly described as *vertically opposite* angles), and which are so called because their convergence is from opposite directions to one point (the intersection of the lines) as vertex (κορυφή)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 278). Where one only meets the other, the angles are adjacent, as in I.14.

**Do the straight lines really cross?** Our statement places E between A and B and between C and D, with C off AB. Yet each use of I.13 needs a straight line standing on another with its far end off it, A off CD and D off AB, as Euclid's figure shows at a glance. So `Prop15.figure` proves it: had AB and CD a second point in common, they would be one straight line (System E, Generalities 1), and C would lie on AB. Euclid's "similarly" hides a third use of I.13, for the angles AEC, CEB, and then Common Notion 3 must be told that the angle AEC is the angle CEA.

**Is the Porism Euclid's?** Heath, after J. L. Heiberg, brackets it, weighing the manuscripts: "The balance of MS. authority seems to be against the genuineness of this *Porism*, but Proclus and Psellus both have it" (vol. 1, p. 278). A porism here is a corollary, "appearing as it were by chance without any additional labour, and constituting, as Proclus says, a sort of *windfall* (ἕρμαιον) and *bonus* (κέρδος)" (vol. 1, p. 278). Proclus says it underlies "that paradoxical theorem" of the Pythagoreans: of regular figures, only six equilateral triangles, four squares or three hexagons fill the space round a point (Heath, vol. 1, p. 279). We prove it apart from `Prop15`, as `Prop15.porism`, adding I.13's two pairs by Common Notion 2.

**The modern way.** Put E at the origin and A, C at vectors a, c, so that B and D lie along −a and −c: the cosine of the angle DEB is (−c)·(−a)/(|c||a|) = a·c/(|a||c|), the cosine of the angle AEC. In the complex plane, the half-turn z ↦ −z about E carries each angle onto the one opposite it.
