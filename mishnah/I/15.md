**Thales, and Euclid.** "This theorem, according to Eudemus, was first discovered by Thales, but found its scientific demonstration in Euclid (Proclus, p. 299, 3–6)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 278). The demonstration is short: I.13 twice, then Common Notions 1 and 3. Heath explains the name through Proclus. When one straight line meets another and is produced through it, "so that the lines cross at the point, they make two pairs of vertical angles (which are more clearly described as vertically opposite angles), and which are so called because their convergence is from opposite directions to one point (the intersection of the lines) as vertex" (vol. 1, p. 278).

**That the straight lines really cross.** Each use of I.13 needs one straight line standing on another, with its far end off it: for "AE stands on CD", A must be off CD, and for "DE stands on AB", D must be off AB. Euclid's figure shows this at a glance. Our statement gives only that E lies between A and B and between C and D, with C off AB, so [`Prop15.figure`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop15.lean) says the rest. If AB and CD shared a second point besides E, they would be one straight line (Generalities 1 in the System E of Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma), and C would lie on AB. Book Zero's `Triangle.of_line` then hands I.13 its triangles. To say "off AB" at all, Lean must first name the straight lines AB and CD, so Postulate 1 joins the list of what I.15 rests on, though Euclid draws nothing here.

**Post. 4 and C.N. 1.** Writing on the same step in I.14, Heath says: "Todhunter points out that for the inference in this line Post. 4, that all right angles are equal, is necessary as well as the Common Notion that things which are equal to the same thing (or rather, here, to equal things) are equal. A similar remark applies to steps in the proofs of I. 15 and I. 28" (vol. 1, p. 277). In Lean, Postulate 4 has done its work before I.15 begins. It is what lets every right angle be measured by one magnitude, `∟`, and I.13 states its conclusion as `∟ + ∟`. So the angles CEA, AED and the angles AED, DEB are equal to one and the same thing, and Common Notion 1, in its plainest form, joins them.

**What Lean made us say.** Euclid names each angle whichever way round suits the sentence: AEC in the enunciation but CEA in the proof, DEB and then BED. Lean turns an angle round only when told to, by `angle_symm` (System E, Metric 4). This matters most in the "similarly", which Lean writes out in full. There CE stands on AB, so I.13 is cited a third time, for the angles AEC, CEB; and then the angle AEC is subtracted from one pair and the angle CEA from the other, so Common Notion 3 has to be told that these are one angle. Nothing else is needed: no construction, no point beyond the five Euclid names.

**The Porism.** Heath, after Heiberg, prints it in brackets: "The balance of MS. authority seems to be against the genuineness of this Porism, but Proclus and Psellus both have it" (vol. 1, p. 278). Here the word means a corollary, "a result not directly sought but appearing as it were by chance without any additional labour, and constituting, as Proclus says, a sort of windfall (ἕρμαιον) and bonus (κέρδος)" (vol. 1, p. 278). The enunciation doesn't include it, so neither does `Prop15`, but the windfall is cheap in Lean too: [`Prop15.porism`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop15.lean) adds the two pairs that I.13 gives, the angles AEC, CEB and the angles BED, DEA, by Common Notion 2. Proclus saw in it the basis of "that paradoxical theorem which proves that only the following three (regular) polygons can fill up the whole space surrounding one point, the equilateral triangle, the square, and the equilateral and equiangular hexagon", a theorem he credits to the Pythagoreans (vol. 1, p. 279).

**A converse.** Proclus also gives one, which Heath states thus: "If a straight line is met at one and the same point intermediate in its length by two other straight lines on different sides of it and such as to make the vertical angles equal, the latter straight lines are in a straight line with one another." Heath: "The proof need not be given, since it is almost self-evident, whether (1) it is direct, by means of I. 13, 14, or (2) indirect, by reductio ad absurdum depending on I. 15" (vol. 1, p. 278).

**The modern way.** Put E at the origin, A and C at vectors a and c; then B and D lie along −a and −c. The cosine of the angle DEB is (−c)·(−a)/(|c||a|) = a·c/(|a||c|), which is the cosine of the angle AEC. In the complex plane it is one map: z ↦ −z, the half-turn about E, carries each angle onto the one vertically opposite it.
