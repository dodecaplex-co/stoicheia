Where two straight lines cross, the opposite angles are equal, and I.16 needs this at once. "This theorem, according to Eudemus, was first discovered by Thales, but found its scientific demonstration in Euclid (Proclus, p. 299, 3–6)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 278). The name has nothing to do with up and down. Two straight lines that cross, Proclus explains, "make two pairs of *vertical* angles (which are more clearly described as *vertically opposite* angles), and which are so called because their convergence is from opposite directions to one point (the intersection of the lines) as vertex (κορυφή)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 278). Where one only meets the other, the angles are adjacent, as in I.14.

Each use of I.13 needs a straight line standing on another, with its far end off it: A off CD, and D off AB. Euclid's figure shows it, and we prove it from the rule that two points fix one straight line. His "similarly" hides a third use of I.13, for the angles AEC, CEB, and there Common Notion 3 must be told that the angle AEC is the angle CEA. Where Heath's reference is to Postulate 4 and Common Notion 1, Common Notion 1 is enough for our proof, as in I.14.

Is the Porism Euclid's? Heath, after J. L. Heiberg, brackets it, weighing the manuscripts: "The balance of MS. authority seems to be against the genuineness of this *Porism*, but Proclus and Psellus both have it" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 278). A porism here is a corollary, "appearing as it were by chance without any additional labour, and constituting, as Proclus says, a sort of *windfall* (ἕρμαιον) and *bonus* (κέρδος)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 278). Proclus says it underlies "that paradoxical theorem" of the Pythagoreans: of regular figures, only six equilateral triangles, four squares or three hexagons fill the space round a point (Heath, vol. 1, p. 279). We prove it apart from `Prop15`, as `Prop15.porism`, adding I.13's two pairs by Common Notion 2.

**The modern way.** With E at $0$ in the complex plane, the half-turn $z \mapsto -z$ about E carries A to a point of EB, C to a point of ED, and so the angle AEC onto DEB.
