**Thales, and Euclid.** "This theorem, according to Eudemus, was first discovered by Thales, but found its scientific demonstration in Euclid (Proclus, p. 299, 3–6)" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 278). The demonstration is short: I.13 twice, then Common Notions 1 and 3. At the bracket [Post. 4 and C.N. 1], Todhunter's remark under I.14 applies here too, and so does our answer: Postulate 4 has done its work inside I.13, whose two right angles come back as `∟ + ∟`, so Common Notion 1 is enough.

**That the straight lines really cross.** Each use of I.13 needs one straight line standing on another, with its far end off it: A off CD, and D off AB. Euclid's figure shows this at a glance. Our statement gives only that E lies between A and B and between C and D, with C off AB, so [`Prop15.figure`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop15.lean) says the rest: if AB and CD shared a second point besides E, they would be one straight line (System E, Generalities 1), and C would lie on AB. Naming those straight lines is Postulate 1, used here though Euclid draws nothing (I.13 brings it in as well). And Euclid's "similarly" hides a third use of I.13, for the angles AEC, CEB, after which Common Notion 3 must be told that AEC is the angle CEA (`angle_symm`, System E, Metric 4).

**The Porism.** Heath, after Heiberg, prints it in brackets: "The balance of MS. authority seems to be against the genuineness of this *Porism*, but Proclus and Psellus both have it" (vol. 1, p. 278). Here the word means a corollary, "a result not directly sought but appearing as it were by chance without any additional labour, and constituting, as Proclus says, a sort of *windfall* (ἕρμαιον) and *bonus* (κέρδος)" (vol. 1, p. 278). The enunciation doesn't include it, so neither does `Prop15`, but the windfall is cheap in Lean too: [`Prop15.porism`](../../Elements/BookI/Prop15.lean) adds the two pairs that I.13 gives, by Common Notion 2.

**The modern way.** Put E at the origin, A and C at vectors a and c; then B and D lie along −a and −c. The cosine of the angle DEB is (−c)·(−a)/(|c||a|) = a·c/(|a||c|), which is the cosine of the angle AEC. In the complex plane it is one map: z ↦ −z, the half-turn about E, carries each angle onto the one vertically opposite it.
