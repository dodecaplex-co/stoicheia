**The circles that must meet.** Euclid's very first step uses something none of his postulates says: that the two circles cut one another. Heath puts it plainly: "It is a commonplace that Euclid has no right to assume, without premising some postulate, that the two circles will meet in a point C" (vol. 1, p. 242). Here the missing postulate is a named rule, [`circles_meet`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Diagram.lean), Intersection rule 5 of the System E of Avigad, Dean and Mumma: if each circle passes through a point inside the other, they cross. It is on the list of things I.1 rests on.

**What the picture shows.** Euclid also never says that C is off the straight line AB, which is what makes ABC a triangle at all. Lean needs it, so `Prop1.figure` proves it: if C were on AB, one of the three points would lie between the other two, and some length would equal itself taken twice.

**The lines CA and CB.** Euclid joins them, citing Postulate 1. Lean needs only the point C, so Postulate 1 is not among the things the Lean proof rests on.

**The modern way.** Put A at 0 and B at 1 in the complex plane. Then C is 1/2 + (√3/2)i, the point you reach by turning AB through 60°. That √3 tells the whole story. In the plane of points with rational coordinates, all five of Euclid's postulates hold, yet the two circles of I.1 slip past each other without meeting, because √3 is not rational. (Robin Hartshorne's *Geometry: Euclid and Beyond* tells this story beautifully.) So the tacit rule is doing real work: it says the plane has the square roots that ruler and compass need.
