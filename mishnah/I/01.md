**The circles that must meet.** Euclid's very first step uses something none of his postulates says: that the two circles cut one another. Heath puts it plainly: "It is a commonplace that Euclid has no right to assume, without premising some postulate, that the two circles will meet in a point C" (vol. 1, p. 242). Here the missing postulate is a named rule, [`circles_meet`](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Diagram.lean) (System E, Intersection 5): if each circle passes through a point inside the other, they cross. It is on the list of things I.1 rests on.

**What the picture shows.** Euclid also never says that C is off the straight line AB, which is what makes ABC a triangle at all. Lean needs it, so `Prop1.figure` proves it: if C were on AB, one of the three points would lie between the other two, and some length would equal itself taken twice.

**Which side, and the straight lines CA and CB.** The two circles cross twice, once on each side of AB, and Euclid simply takes one of the points. Later propositions (I.9 and I.11) need the triangle on a particular side, so our I.1 lets the side be named, and naming a side means naming the straight line AB. The proof therefore uses Postulate 1 for AB, together with the rule that two circles cross on each side of the straight line through their centres (System E, Intersections 8). Euclid cites Postulate 1 for joining CA and CB, but those straight lines Lean never needs: only the point C.

**The modern way.** Put A at 0 and B at 1 in the complex plane, and C is 1/2 + (√3/2)i, the point reached by turning AB through 60°. That √3 tells the whole story: in the plane of points with rational coordinates all five of Euclid's postulates hold, yet the two circles of I.1 slip past each other without meeting, as Robin Hartshorne's *Geometry: Euclid and Beyond* explains beautifully. So the tacit rule is doing real work: it says the plane has the square roots that ruler and compass need.
