**"Let DB be joined."** Euclid cuts the figure into two triangles: ABD goes to I.42, and DBC to I.44, which applies a parallelogram equal to it to GH, a side of the first. More sides mean more triangles, each applied in turn to the side the last one left. So our main theorem gives the figure as Euclid cuts it, the sum of ABD and DBC, for our `quadArea` would read ABCD along the other diameter, AC, which falls outside the figure when it has a corner pointing inwards at B or D.

**A figure that comes with its triangles.** The companions take a figure of any number of sides, given with the triangles it is cut into, and check the cutting: triangles that lie apart, no point inside two of them, so that the figure may even have a hole (`Prop45.apart`); or, as Euclid and Oliver Byrne cut it, the triangles from one corner, checked to fit together (`Prop45.fan`). The parallelogram is equal to those triangles taken together. That another cutting gives the same whole, AC in place of DB, is the step Euclid takes for granted; David Hilbert proved it, for his measure of area.

**Do the triangles fit?** In Euclid's figure they plainly do: A and C lie on either side of DB, and ABD and DBC meet along it and nowhere else. Were A and C on one side of DB, the two triangles would overlap. With more corners, the straight lines from one corner can fold back in the same way, or come round on themselves, so that a later triangle lies over an earlier one or reaches outside the figure, and the triangles together are no longer the figure. So the check of `Prop45.fan` asks two things: that each two neighbouring triangles lie on either side of the straight line they share, and that their angles at the corner together make less than four right angles. That these are enough is the hardest step of our I.45 (`Fitted.fan`). It follows the triangles round the corner one at a time and measures how far round each point lies; and since no angle of ours is greater than two right angles, a point that has turned further is measured as two right angles and something more. Euclid had none of this to do: his figure has four sides and two triangles, and they lie apart where anyone can see them.

**Dividing with a straight line.** Heath takes stock: "We have now learnt how to represent any rectilineal area, which can of course be resolved into triangles, by a single parallelogram having one side equal to any given straight line and one angle equal to any given rectilineal angle" (vol. 1, p. 347). A rectangle stands for a product, so applying one to a given straight line is, in his words, "the geometrical equivalent of algebraical *division*" (p. 347). The last step, a square equal to the figure, waits for II.14.

**"The squaring of the circle as well."** Proclus guesses that "it was in consequence of this problem that the ancient geometers were led to investigate the squaring of the circle as well" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 347): if any straight-sided figure can be made a parallelogram, why not a circle? Archimedes, he adds, proved a circle equal to the right-angled triangle whose sides about the right angle are the circle's radius and perimeter. But no construction with straight lines and circles can lay that perimeter out straight, as Ferdinand von Lindemann showed in 1882.

**The modern way.** The figure's area is ½|(B − A) × (D − A)| + ½|(B − D) × (C − D)|, where u × v is the determinant of u and v. The parallelograms share the side a = KF = GH and the angle E, so their breadths KH and HM, each a triangle's area divided by a sin E, add up to KM.
