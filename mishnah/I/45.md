Euclid divides the figure into two triangles with DB. ABD goes to I.42, and DBC to I.44, which applies a parallelogram equal to it to GH, a side of the first. More sides mean more triangles, each applied in turn to the side the last one left. Our main theorem takes Euclid's two triangles, ABD and DBC, and constructs in the angle E a parallelogram KFLM equal to their sum. It does not check that they make one figure; they do when A and C lie on opposite sides of DB, as in Euclid's figure. Naming the figure by its corners, as our `quadArea A B C D` does, would be no safer: that reads it along the other diameter, AC, which falls outside the figure when the corner at B or D points inwards, just as DB falls outside when the corner at A or C does.

**A figure that comes with its triangles.** Three of the companions take a figure given with a checked division into triangles: triangles that do not overlap, no point inside two of them, so that the figure may even have a hole (`Prop45.apart`); triangles glued edge to edge in a suitable order, for a figure with one boundary (`Prop45.figure`); or, as Euclid and Byrne divide it, triangles from one corner, checked to fit together (`Prop45.fan`). Equal to the figure here means equal to those triangles together. Our scope is restricted. Not yet proved ([#116](https://github.com/jackChallis/stoicheia/issues/116)): that every rectilineal figure, given only by its boundary, has such a division, and that another division gives the same area. Euclid takes the second for granted, and Hilbert proved it, for his measure of area.

**Do the triangles fit?** In Euclid's figure, A and C lie on opposite sides of DB, so ABD and DBC meet along DB and nowhere else. Put A and C on the same side and the triangles overlap. With more corners, the straight lines from one corner can fold back or come round on themselves. A later triangle may then lie over an earlier one or reach outside the figure, so the triangles together no longer give the figure. For `Prop45.fan` we check two things: neighbouring triangles lie on opposite sides of the straight line they share, and their angles at the corner together make less than four right angles. The hardest step, `Fitted.fan`, proves that these conditions let the triangles fit together without overlap. The proof follows the triangles round the corner one at a time, measuring how far round each point lies. Our angles are never greater than two right angles, so a point beyond that turn is measured as two right angles and something more. Euclid leaves this to his figure: four sides, two triangles, plainly apart.

Heath takes stock: "We have now learnt how to represent any rectilineal area, which can of course be resolved into triangles, by a single parallelogram having one side equal to any given straight line and one angle equal to any given rectilineal angle" (vol. 1, p. 347). A rectangle stands for a product, so applying one to a given straight line is, in his words, "the geometrical equivalent of algebraical *division*" (vol. 1, p. 347). The last step, a square equal to the figure, waits for II.14.

Proclus guesses that "it was in consequence of this problem that the ancient geometers were led to investigate the squaring of the circle as well" (Heath, vol. 1, p. 347): if any straight-sided figure can be made a parallelogram, why not a circle? Archimedes, he adds, proved a circle equal to the right-angled triangle whose sides about the right angle are the circle's radius and perimeter. But no construction with straight lines and circles can lay that perimeter out straight, as Ferdinand von Lindemann showed in 1882.

**The modern way.** With the cross product of [I.35](35.md), and with A and C on opposite sides of DB, as in Euclid's figure, the figure's area is $\tfrac{1}{2}|\mathrm{(B - A)} \times \mathrm{(D - A)}| + \tfrac{1}{2}|\mathrm{(B - D)} \times \mathrm{(C - D)}|$. Write $\varepsilon$ for the given angle E and $a$ for the common side $\mathrm{KF} = \mathrm{GH}$. A parallelogram with a side $a$ in the angle $\varepsilon$ has area $a$ times its other side times $\sin\varepsilon$, so the breadths KH and HM are the areas of the two triangles, each divided by $a\sin\varepsilon$, and KM, their sum, is the area of the whole figure divided by $a\sin\varepsilon$: Heath's division, done once for each triangle.
