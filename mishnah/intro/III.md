**From the centre to the tangent.** Book I drew circles to find points; Book III studies the circle itself: its centre, the straight lines in it, those that touch it, the angles in its segments, the right angle in a semicircle among them (III.31), and the circumferences those angles stand on. A diameter, in Books I and II often a parallelogram's diagonal, is the circle's again (Definition I.17). The pages are laid out as in Book I, whose [introduction](../I/index.md) walks through one, and the decisions behind our statements and words are gathered in [Book III's brief](../../docs/briefs/0004-book-III.md).

**"Let the centre of the circle ABC be taken."** Euclid says it in III.2, where Heath's bracket gives III.1, and often after. In our foundations a given circle has a centre only by hypothesis. Where Heath's bracket gives III.1 for it, the Lean cites III.1 too: a circle has only one centre (`centre_unique`), so III.1 finds the one given. Later Euclid often takes the centre with no bracket, and the Lean takes it straight from the hypothesis, or, in III.35 to III.37, by III.1 all the same. He also bisects a straight line, draws one at right angles or copies an angle with no bracket for I.10, I.11, I.12 or I.23; where the Lean takes those steps, it cites those propositions, and the table shows both. One rule of System E, Circle 2, already contains III.2, and so does Circle 3, the same rule turned round; no proof here rests on either. III.1 puts its middle point D within the circle by Euclid's own argument for III.2 (`Prop1.within`), and the later proofs take that step by III.2, its corollaries or Definition I.15.

**Words, not axioms.** Book III's words are definitions in [`DefinitionsIII.lean`](../../Elements/Foundations/DefinitionsIII.lean), none an axiom. Equal circles (`EqualCircles`) have equal radii, and Heath finds "nothing technically wrong in saying 'By *equal circles* I mean circles with equal radii'" (vol. 2, p. 2). A straight line touches a circle (`Touches`) "which, meeting the circle and being produced, does not cut the circle" (Definition III.2): it meets the circle, but never in two points. We read circles touching one another (`TouchesInternally`, `TouchesExternally`) as Isaac Todhunter does, by Heath's report: they cut nowhere at all.

**How long is a circumference?** Our foundations have no magnitude for a curve, so we measure a circumference by twice the angle standing on it at the circumference (Definition III.9; `circumference`, `standing`): its angle at the centre, when it is less than a semicircle (III.20). Euclid compares circumferences only in one circle or in equal circles, and there this measure says exactly when two are equal. In unequal circles it would call two circumferences equal whenever their angles are, though one is longer than the other, so every statement keeps his "in equal circles". In III.27 the conclusion would hold without it, since the measure carries no radius, and the proof keeps it on Euclid's route; in III.26 and III.28, which conclude that the circumferences coincide as well, and in III.29, where the measures meet straight lines, it does real work.

**When are two segments equal?** When they coincide, as Euclid's III.24 has them: laid one on the other, end on end and circumference on circumference, with every distance kept (`Arc.Congruent`). A segment is named, as Heath names it, by its circle, the ends of its base and a point on its side (`Arc`). Our foundations move no figure but in I.4 and I.8, so laying one segment on another is proved from them, once, for III.24 (`placement`). III.26 and III.28 then conclude that equal circumferences coincide too, and the theorems after III.29, in [`Arcs.lean`](../../Elements/BookIII/Arcs.lean), say what equal measures come to.

**"Therefore EF is equal to EG."** III.14 reads it off the equal squares on EF and EG, one of the steps Euclid takes for granted again and again. We prove each once, and the line under each page's table names those its proof calls on. In [`Squares.lean`](../../Elements/BookIII/Squares.lean), equal squares are on equal straight lines (`BookI.Prop46.sq`), and the perpendicular from the centre falls between the ends of a straight line in the circle (`foot_between`). I.47 is said of the squares on straight lines, as in Book II. With the squares of I.46 and I.47, Postulate 5 first comes into Book III, at III.14.

**Two circumferences make a circle.** What Euclid assumes of circumferences from III.24 on is proved once, from III.20 to III.22, in [`Circumferences.lean`](../../Elements/BookIII/Circumferences.lean). A circumference may be measured at any point of the circle on the other side of its straight line, since the angles there are all equal, or by its angle at the centre, when the centre lies beyond it (`Circumference.eq`). The two circumferences on one straight line make up the whole circle (`Circumference.whole`). And a circumference is a semicircle, less than one, or greater, as the centre lies on its straight line, beyond it, or on its side. Through I.32, every fact about circumferences rests on Postulate 5.

**"From this it is manifest."** So Euclid opens the porisms to III.1 and III.16, and later proofs cite them: the centre lies on a straight line bisecting a straight line in the circle at right angles (III.9, III.10), and the straight line at right angles to a diameter at its end touches the circle (III.17, III.33, III.37).

**How our statements are worded.** Two whole statements are worded for our foundations: III.1 is stated for a circle that has a centre, and proves that the point it constructs is that centre; and III.9 leaves out "within", which its proof never uses and III.25 cannot grant. III.12, Heron's addition to III.11 in Heath's square brackets, is kept, and stated and proved like the rest. Six statements are restricted. III.7 and III.8 compare straight lines on one side of the diameter only, as Euclid's figures do; III.14 asks, as his proof does, that neither straight line pass through the centre, and so says nothing of diameters; III.15 proves the diameter greater than another straight line only beside one more remote from the centre, which his proof uses; III.17 starts from a point outside the circle; and our representation of a segment includes its circle, so III.25 is given the circle, a straight line in it and the segment's side, not the segment alone. Two are proved in part: our angles are made by two straight lines, so III.16 and III.31 leave their mixed angles, "contained by a straight line and a circumference" (Definition III.7), to Euclid's words. III.26 and III.28 read equal circumferences as equal in measure and coinciding, which says more than Heath's words. Each of these pages tells why.
