**From the centre to the tangent.** Book I drew circles to find points; Book III studies the circle itself: its centre, the straight lines in it, those that touch it, the angles in its segments, the right angle in a semicircle among them (III.31), and the circumferences those angles stand on. A diameter, in Books I and II often a parallelogram's diagonal, is the circle's again (Definition I.17). Each page reads as Book I's do, and [Book I's introduction](../I/index.md) says how.

**"Let the centre of the circle ABC be taken."** Euclid says it in III.2, citing III.1, and often after. In Lean a given circle has a centre only by hypothesis, and the proofs take it by III.1, as Euclid does: a circle has only one centre (`centre_unique`), so III.1 finds the one given. Later Euclid often takes the centre without citing III.1, just as he bisects a straight line, draws one at right angles or copies an angle without citing I.10, I.11, I.12 or I.23. The Lean cites them, and the table shows both. One rule of System E, Circle 2, is III.2 itself, and III.1 calls on it before III.2 is proved, as III.2's page tells.

**Words, not axioms.** Book III's words are definitions in [`DefinitionsIII.lean`](../../Elements/Foundations/DefinitionsIII.lean), none an axiom. Equal circles (`EqualCircles`) have equal radii, and Heath finds "nothing technically wrong in saying 'By *equal circles* I mean circles with equal radii'" (vol. 2, p. 2). A straight line touches a circle (`Touches`) "which, meeting the circle and being produced, does not cut the circle" (Definition III.2): it meets the circle, but never in two points. Circles touching one another (`TouchesInternally`, `TouchesExternally`) we read as Isaac Todhunter does, by Heath's report: they cut nowhere at all, as III.11's page tells.

**How long is a circumference?** Lean has no magnitude for a curve, so we measure a circumference by twice the angle standing on it at the circumference (Definition III.9; `circumference`, `standing`): its angle at the centre, when it is less than a semicircle (III.20). Euclid compares circumferences only in one circle or in equal circles, and there this measure says exactly when two are equal. In unequal circles it would call two circumferences equal whenever their angles are, though one is longer than the other, so every statement keeps his "in equal circles".

**What Book III takes for granted.** We prove once what Euclid passes over again and again, and the line under each page's table names the steps its proof calls on:

- In [`Squares.lean`](../../Elements/BookIII/Squares.lean), equal squares are on equal straight lines (`BookI.Prop46.sq`), Euclid's "therefore EF is equal to EG" (III.14), and the perpendicular from the centre falls between the ends of a straight line in the circle (`foot_between`), as III.14 tells. I.47 is said of the squares on straight lines, as in Book II. With the squares of I.46 and I.47, Postulate 5 first comes into Book III, at III.14.
- In [`Circumferences.lean`](../../Elements/BookIII/Circumferences.lean), proved from III.20 to III.22, is what Euclid assumes of circumferences from III.24 on. A circumference may be measured at any point of the circle on the other side of its straight line, since the angles there are all equal (`Circumference.eq`), or by its angle at the centre, when the centre lies beyond it. The two circumferences on one straight line make up the whole circle (`Circumference.whole`). And a circumference is a semicircle, less than one, or greater, as the centre lies on its straight line, beyond it, or on its side. Through I.32, every fact about circumferences rests on Postulate 5.

**"From this it is manifest."** So Euclid opens the porisms to III.1 and III.16, and later proofs cite them: the centre lies on a straight line bisecting a straight line in the circle at right angles (III.9, III.10), and the straight line at right angles to a diameter at its end touches the circle (III.17, III.33, III.37). Our tables count a citation of either porism as a citation of its proposition.

**Where our statements part from Euclid's.** III.1 is stated for a circle that has a centre, and proves that the point it constructs is that centre. III.9 leaves out "within", which its proof never uses and III.25 cannot grant. III.12, Heron's addition to III.11 in Heath's square brackets, is stated and proved like the rest. Our angles are made by two straight lines, so III.16 and III.31 leave their mixed angles, "contained by a straight line and a circumference" (Definition III.7), to Euclid's words. Lean cannot give a segment without its circle, so III.25 is given the circle, a straight line in it and the segment's side. Each of these pages tells why.
