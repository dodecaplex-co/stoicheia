**From a triangle to Pythagoras.** Book I opens with an equilateral triangle on a given straight line and closes with Pythagoras' theorem and its converse (I.47, I.48). Heath sees three sections in it (vol. 1, p. 308): triangles, and how their sides and angles compare, up to I.26; parallels, from I.27, with the angles of a triangle (I.32) and the first parallelograms (I.33, I.34); and from I.35 on, areas that are equal without being congruent. Euclid holds his parallel postulate back until I.29, and so do our proofs. The decisions behind our statements, words and shared lemmas are gathered in [Book I's brief](../../docs/briefs/book-I.md).

**What each page holds.** First Euclid's enunciation and proof, in Sir Thomas Heath's 1908 translation, with a figure at a glance in Oliver Byrne's red, yellow and blue, and Euclid's own figure, both with Heath's letters. Heath's square brackets, such as [I. 3], are his references, pointing to the earlier result a step uses; Euclid himself seldom names one, for, as Heath remarks, "specific quotations of earlier propositions are rare in Euclid" (vol. 1, p. 83). Q. E. D. (*quod erat demonstrandum*, which was to be proved) closes a theorem, and Q. E. F. (*quod erat faciendum*, which was to be done) a problem. Then the Lean: Euclid's sentences are its `--` comments, word for word, with the Lean that carries them out beneath them, and our own notes are in `/- … -/`. A table of what the proof rests on follows; then the mishnah, our commentary, and a few books and papers to read alongside.

**What does the figure say?** That two circles cross, that a point lies between two others, which side of a straight line a point is on: Euclid reads all this off his figure, and our proofs say each one. We name these steps as tacit rules. Forty-two remain axioms; [forty-one are now proved](../../docs/FORMALIZATION.md#rules-now-proved) from the remaining foundations, in Book Zero or after the propositions they use. The rules follow System E, the list of what Euclid reads off his figures drawn up by Jeremy Avigad, Edward Dean and John Mumma, and every page's footer thanks them and the authors of LeanEuclid, which carried System E into Lean. "Intersection 5" and its kin are names from System E: an Intersection rule says when straight lines and circles must meet, and an Intersections rule lets us take a point where they do. Book Zero, in [`Elements/Figure`](../../Elements/Figure), is our small library of facts every figure needs, proved once from the foundations and citing no proposition.

**A straight line is whole.** In our foundations a straight line is the whole straight line, named by any two of its points (Postulate 1). I.2 finds its points on those whole lines without Postulate 2. I.5 takes its produced sides as given, but still rests on Postulate 2 through proved rules: in its own Lean, the order rule beneath `OnRay.between_of_len_lt`, and Metric 8, `area_swap`; and through I.4, which it cites. Where a proof must cut off with I.3 from a straight line produced, as I.11 does, `produce_longer` first makes it long enough. Superposition, Euclid's picking up of a triangle, is a restricted inference in System E's style, used directly only in I.4 and I.8. It lets a proof imagine one triangle laid on another, but the proof draws nothing in that imagined figure, and takes from it only equalities about the figures already given ([its file](../../Elements/Foundations/Tacit/Superposition.lean) says more). I.5 speaks of the equal sides produced, so a proof that wants only the angles at the base cites it through `Prop5.base`, which produces the sides anywhere. Where Euclid says "similarly", we write his argument once, for any letters, and use it in each place. Each page says which cases our statements cover.

**When must two straight lines meet?** Euclid first calls on Postulate 5 in I.29, and again in I.44. Our proofs of I.1 to I.28 never use it, nor does our proof of I.31, which draws its parallel with I.27 alone; every other proof from I.29 on rests on it. From I.37 on, Euclid often draws a parallel and names the point where it meets another straight line, reading the meeting off his figure. That a straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other, Proclus states in his attempt to prove the postulate (Heath, vol. 1, p. 207), and under I.31 he infers from I.30 that the parallel through a point is unique (vol. 1, p. 316). Our `Prop30.meet` proves it from I.30, so wherever it is used the Lean cites I.30.

**From triangles to a parallelogram.** Euclid's I.45 divides a figure into triangles and makes a parallelogram equal to their sum, taking for granted that the figure can be so divided, and that every division gives the same sum. Book Zero proves division existence separately and proves division independence after David Hilbert's theory of content. A figure is given by its outline alone, finitely many closed lines of straight sides, so that it may have holes or separate pieces, and whether a point lies within it is read from the sides alone, by counting the sides a ray from the point crosses. Every such figure has a division into triangles that do not overlap, no point inside two of them, and any two divisions that cover the same points have the same sum, so the figure has one area. Our I.45 makes a parallelogram equal to it for any rectilineal figure (`Prop45.outline`), and II.14 squares it. The main theorem keeps Euclid's two triangles, unchecked, and the companions that take a figure given with a checked division into triangles stay beside the outline's. The work is told in [brief 0013](../../docs/briefs/0013-figures.md), and its history under [#116](https://github.com/jackChallis/stoicheia/issues/116).

**Why does the Lean cite more?** The column "Heath's references" gives the propositions in Heath's brackets, and "Lean cites" those the Lean proof cites, which may be more, and now and then fewer; the table shows both. A bracket is Heath's pointer, not a reconstruction of the argument, and where there is none Euclid may still have had his reason. A shared step, a lemma proved once and used by many proofs, brings in the propositions it rests on, and the line under each table names those steps, with the propositions each brings in. The tacit rules taken as axioms are listed in full beneath it; when a proof calls on a proved rule, that rule contributes the axioms its proof uses. Intersection 2, though proved, has no user in the published proofs. I.12 uses the proved Intersection 3: producing the straight line from an interior point to the circumference in both directions gives two distinct crossings.
