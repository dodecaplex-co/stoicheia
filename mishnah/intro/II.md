**Algebra without numbers.** Book II is the book of rectangles and squares on cut straight lines, sometimes called Euclid's geometrical algebra: what we would write as a(b + c + d) = ab + ac + ad, (a + b)² = a² + b² + 2ab and their cousins, done with figures alone. It ends with three famous results: the cut later called the golden section (II.11), the two partners of I.47 that are now the law of cosines (II.12, II.13), and a square equal to any straight-sided figure (II.14). Each page reads as Book I's do, and [Book I's introduction](../I/index.md) says how. The decisions behind our statements and words are gathered in [Book II's brief](../../docs/briefs/book-II.md).

**"The rectangle contained by AB, BC."** Book II has two definitions of its own. A rectangle is said to be contained by the two straight lines about its right angle, and Euclid often names it by them alone, "the rectangle AB, BC" (II.2). A gnomon is one of the parallelograms about the diameter of a parallelogram, Euclid's word for its diagonal, with the two complements (II.5). In our foundations, the rectangle contained by two straight lines, `rect`, and the square on one, `sq`, are areas, defined from the lengths rather than assumed. So we state each proposition as Euclid does, as a sum of areas with no numbers anywhere, though two of them say less than his enunciations: II.1 is proved for two points of section, the case his proof draws, where his enunciation allows any number; and II.14 for a figure of two triangles, the figure of I.45's proof, where his allows any rectilineal figure.

**What Book II borrows from Book I.** Page after page, Euclid takes the same few things for granted. We prove each once, in [`Rectangles.lean`](../../Elements/BookII/Rectangles.lean), and the line under each page's table names those its proof calls on. A rectangle he draws is the rectangle contained by its sides, or by straight lines equal to them, by I.4 and I.34 (`Rectangle.eq_rect`). One right angle makes a parallelogram a rectangle, by I.29, which, Heath remarks under II.4, I.46 has already shown (`Rectangle.of_right`). And I.47 is said of the squares on straight lines, for II.9 to II.14 (`BookI.Prop47.sq`).

**"For the same reason."** With these words II.4 passes from one parallelogram about the diameter of a square to the other: both are squares, and we prove it once (`Square.about_diameter`). The square I.46 describes is the square on its side (`Square.described`). It stands on one side of its straight line, and a point there, with no letter in Heath's figure, picks the side: `exists_off_line` finds one with I.1's two circles. II.7's "let the figure be drawn" is I.43's figure, the parallels through a point of the diameter (`Parallelogram.figure`).

**Will they meet?** Book II draws parallels as Book I did, and names the points where they meet other straight lines. A straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other, after Proclus (`BookI.Prop30.meet`, from I.30). [Book Zero](../../Elements/Figure) proves that parallels keep the order of points (`Parallel.between`), and that parallelograms side by side make up the whole (`quadArea_add_of_parallelograms`). So every proposition of Book II rests on Postulate 5, through the parallels of I.29 and I.30 and the squares of I.46 and I.47, and II.10 cites the postulate by name.

<a name="areas"></a>**"AG is the rectangle AC, CB."** Book II names its areas in two ways. Euclid often names a piece of his figure by two opposite corners, as AG in II.4; the Lean names it by all four in order, `quadArea C A H G`, the triangles CAH and CHG that its diameter CH cuts it into, and a parallelogram has the same area named from any corner, either way round. The rectangle AC, CB and the square on AC are named by straight lines instead: `rect A C C B` is the area of every rectangle whose sides about a right angle are equal to AC and CB, and `sq A C` is `rect A C A C`. Euclid passes from piece to name with his reason, "for GC is equal to CB"; our proof adds what he reads off the figure, that AG is a parallelogram with a right angle at C, and `Rectangle.eq_rect` makes it `rect A C C B`. II.14 ends on "the square which can be described on EH": a name, with no piece drawn.

**"HF, CK, AG, GE are the whole ADEB."** Euclid sees the four pieces fill the square. Our areas add by one rule (System E, Diagram-area 2): a straight line from a corner of a triangle to a point between the ends of the opposite side cuts it into two that make up the whole. So the pieces add up because of where their points lie, and the proof says where. G, where CF crosses BD, lies between B and D, between C and F, and between H and K; H lies between A and D, and K between B and E. `Parallelogram.figure` gives each, for parallels keep order; I.30 makes the complements parallelograms; and `Parallelogram.about_diameter` cuts ADEB along HK, then each half along CF. In another order the same letters could name figures that overlap, or reach outside the square, and their sum would not be the square.
