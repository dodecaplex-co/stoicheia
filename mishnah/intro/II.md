**Algebra without numbers.** Book II is the book of rectangles and squares on cut straight lines, sometimes called Euclid's geometrical algebra: what we would write as a(b + c + d) = ab + ac + ad, (a + b)² = a² + b² + 2ab and their cousins, done with figures alone. Euclid ends it with three famous results: the cut later called the golden section (II.11), the two partners of I.47 that are now the law of cosines (II.12, II.13), and a square equal to any straight-sided figure (II.14). Each page reads as Book I's do, and [Book I's introduction](../I/index.md) says how. The decisions behind our statements and words are gathered in [Book II's brief](../../docs/briefs/book-II.md).

**"The rectangle contained by AB, BC."** Book II has two definitions of its own. A rectangle is said to be contained by the two straight lines about its right angle, and Euclid often names it by them alone, "the rectangle AB, BC" (II.2). A gnomon is one of the parallelograms about the diameter of a parallelogram, Euclid's word for its diagonal, with the two complements (II.5). Our Lean defines no gnomon: II.5 to II.8 write each one as the sum of the areas of its three parallelograms, and each page says so where its gnomon enters the proof. In our foundations, the rectangle contained by two straight lines, `rect`, and the square on one, `sq`, are areas, defined from the lengths rather than assumed. So we state each proposition as Euclid does, as a sum of areas without numerical measures of area.

Two enunciations are wider than the case Euclid's proof draws; there the main theorem keeps his case and companions take the rest. II.1 is proved for two points of section, and its companion for any number. II.14 starts with the two triangles of I.45's proof, unchecked; its companion `Prop14.outline` takes any rectilineal figure, given by its boundary alone (an outline), and draws a square equal to its area, the sum every division of it into triangles gives, as [Book I's introduction](../I/index.md) tells. Its companions for a figure given with a checked division into triangles, triangles that do not overlap, no point inside two of them, stay beside it, and square those triangles together.

**What Book II borrows from Book I.** Page after page, Euclid takes the same few things for granted. We prove each once, in [`Rectangles.lean`](../../Elements/BookII/Rectangles.lean) or, for one of them, in [I.13's file](../../Elements/BookI/Prop13.lean), and the line under each page's table names those its proof calls on. A rectangle he draws is the rectangle contained by its sides, or by straight lines equal to them, by I.4 and I.34 (`Rectangle.eq_rect`). One right angle makes a parallelogram a rectangle, by I.29, which, Heath remarks under II.4, I.46 has already shown (`Rectangle.of_right`). When a straight line set up on a straight line makes one right angle, the other angle it makes is right too, by I.13 and Common Notion 3 (`Tacit.adjacent_eq_of_right`, which is named among the tacit rules, though we prove it). And I.47 is said of the squares on straight lines, for II.9 to II.14 (`BookI.Prop47.sq`).

**"For the same reason."** With these words II.4 passes from one parallelogram about the diameter of a square to the other: both are squares, and we prove it once (`Square.about_diameter`). The square I.46 describes is the square on its side (`Square.described`). It stands on one side of its straight line, and a point there, with no letter in Heath's figure, picks the side: `exists_off_line` finds one with I.1's two circles. II.7's "let the figure be drawn" is the figure II.4 draws line by line, with all that I.43 asks of it (`Parallelogram.figure`).

**Will they meet?** Book II draws parallels as Book I did, and names the points where they meet other straight lines. A straight line meeting one of two parallels meets the other, after Proclus (`BookI.Prop30.meet`, from I.30). [Book Zero](../../Elements/Figure) proves that parallels keep the order of points (`Parallel.between`), and that parallelograms side by side make up the whole (`quadArea_add_of_parallelograms`). Proclus' step rests on Postulate 5 through I.29, as do the squares of I.46 and I.47, which is how the postulate stands beneath every proof here; Book Zero's two lemmas do not use it, and II.10's text calls on the postulate itself, which Heath's reference names.

<a name="areas"></a>**"AG is the rectangle AC, CB."** This paragraph and the next follow the figure of [II.4](04.md), where the square ADEB is cut into two squares and two rectangles. Book II names its areas in two ways. Euclid often names a piece of his figure by two opposite corners, as AG in II.4. The Lean names it by all four in order, `quadArea C A H G`: the two triangles CAH and CHG that its diameter CH cuts it into. A parallelogram has the same area named from any corner, either way round. The rectangle AC, CB and the square on AC are named by straight lines instead: `rect A C C B` is the area of every rectangle whose sides about a right angle are equal to AC and CB, and `sq A C` is `rect A C A C`. Euclid passes from piece to name with his reason, "for GC is equal to CB"; our proof adds what he reads off the figure, that AG is a parallelogram with a right angle at C. One right angle makes it a rectangle (`Rectangle.of_right`), and `Rectangle.eq_rect` makes that rectangle `rect A C C B`. In II.14's main theorem, the sum of A's two triangles, unchecked, is proved equal to "the square which can be described on EH": a name, with no piece drawn.

**"HF, CK, AG, GE are the whole ADEB."** Euclid sees the four pieces fill the square. Our areas add by one rule (System E, Diagram-area 2): a straight line from a corner of a triangle to a point between the ends of the opposite side cuts it into two that make up the whole. So the pieces add up because of where their points lie, and the proof says where. G, where CF crosses BD, lies between B and D, between C and F, and between H and K; H lies between A and D, K between B and E, and F between D and E. `Parallelogram.figure` gives each, for parallels keep order; I.30 makes the complements parallelograms; and `Parallelogram.about_diameter` cuts ADEB along HK, then each of the two parts along CF. In another order the same letters could name figures that overlap, or reach outside the square, and their sum would not be the square.
